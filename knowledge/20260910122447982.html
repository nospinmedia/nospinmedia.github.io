How Does Facial Recognition Work?

October 1, 202614 min read

Facial recognition is a technology that uses computers to compare the appearance of a person's face with one or more previously recorded faces. Modern systems typically do this by converting facial information from an image or video into a mathematical representation and then comparing that representation with others.

The computer is not recognizing someone in the same way another person does. It uses algorithms to identify patterns in facial images and determine how similar those patterns are to stored or previously calculated representations.

Facial recognition can be used for tasks as simple as determining whether two photographs probably show the same person or as complex as searching a large database for possible matches.

Face Detection Comes First

Before a system can recognize a face, it usually has to find the face within an image.

This process is called face detection.

A photograph might contain people, buildings, furniture, vehicles and thousands of other visual features. Face-detection software analyzes the image and identifies regions that appear to contain human faces.

Modern systems commonly use machine-learning models trained on large collections of images to distinguish faces from other objects.

Detection answers a basic question: Where is the face?

It does not necessarily answer: Whose face is it?

Those are separate tasks.

What Happens After a Face Is Detected?

Once the system finds a face, it typically prepares the image for further analysis.

The face may be cropped from the larger image and adjusted to reduce differences caused by position, rotation or scale. Software may identify important facial landmarks, such as the locations of the eyes, nose, mouth and outline of the face.

The image can then be aligned so that facial features appear in more consistent positions before recognition takes place.

This preparation helps the recognition system compare faces even when the original photographs were taken from somewhat different angles or distances.

Does Facial Recognition Measure the Distance Between Your Eyes?

Early facial-recognition systems often relied heavily on explicitly measured features such as distances and geometric relationships among the eyes, nose, mouth and other facial landmarks.

Modern systems are generally much more sophisticated.

Many use deep neural networks that learn which visual patterns are useful for distinguishing one face from another. Instead of engineers manually specifying every measurement that should matter, the model learns useful representations from training data.

Geometric characteristics of the face still influence the result, but the system may incorporate a large number of patterns involving shapes, textures and relationships that cannot be summarized by a short list of measurements.

What Is a Face Embedding?

A key concept in modern facial recognition is the face embedding.

After analyzing a face, a neural network can convert it into a list of numbers. This numerical representation is designed so that images of the same person tend to produce representations that are closer to one another mathematically than images of different people.

The resulting list may contain hundreds or more numerical values, depending on the system.

It is sometimes casually described as a faceprint, although that term can give the misleading impression that the system stores a simple picture-like pattern comparable to a fingerprint.

A face embedding is better understood as a mathematical representation generated by a particular recognition model.

How Does the Computer Compare Two Faces?

Suppose a phone has a stored facial representation for its owner and its camera captures a new image of the person attempting to unlock it.

The recognition system processes the new face and produces another mathematical representation.

Software then measures how similar the new representation is to the enrolled representation.

Different systems can use different mathematical methods for measuring this similarity or distance.

If the similarity exceeds a required threshold, the system may determine that the two faces are sufficiently similar to be considered a match.

If the score does not meet the threshold, the system rejects the comparison.

Facial Recognition Does Not Usually Produce Absolute Certainty

Facial recognition generally works with similarity scores and statistical decisions, not perfect certainty.

A system might determine that two facial representations are highly similar, but that does not mathematically prove that the images depict the same human being.

A threshold determines how much similarity is required before a match is accepted.

Changing that threshold creates a tradeoff. A stricter threshold can reduce incorrect matches but may reject more legitimate matches. A less strict threshold may successfully recognize more genuine users while increasing the possibility of incorrectly matching different people.

The appropriate threshold depends on what the system is being used for and the consequences of making an error.

What Is Facial Verification?

Facial verification is usually a one-to-one comparison.

The system is essentially asking whether a person is who they claim to be.

For example, a device might compare the face in front of its camera with the enrolled face belonging to that device's authorized user.

The system does not need to search through millions of people. It compares the presented face with a specific reference identity.

This is sometimes described as a one-to-one, or 1:1, comparison.

What Is Facial Identification?

Facial identification is generally a one-to-many search.

Instead of asking whether a face matches one particular person, the system compares the face against many enrolled identities to find possible matches.

For example, a system might search a database containing thousands or millions of facial representations and return candidates whose representations are most similar to the submitted image.

This is commonly called a one-to-many, or 1:N, search.

Identification becomes more challenging as databases become larger because there are more opportunities for unrelated faces to produce relatively high similarity scores.

A candidate returned by a facial-recognition search should therefore not automatically be treated as proof of identity.

How Are Facial-Recognition Systems Trained?

Modern facial-recognition systems commonly rely on machine learning, particularly deep learning.

During training, a neural network is shown many facial images. The training process adjusts large numbers of internal model parameters so the network becomes better at producing useful representations of faces.

The goal is generally to make representations of the same person more similar while making representations of different people more distinguishable.

Training can require very large image collections because real-world faces appear under many conditions: different lighting, expressions, ages, camera qualities, hairstyles, viewing angles and backgrounds.

The quality and composition of the training data can strongly affect how well the resulting system performs.

Does the System Store a Photograph of Your Face?

It depends on the application.

Some systems may retain original photographs or video. Others may primarily store templates or mathematical representations derived from facial images. Some systems can store both.

A facial template is not necessarily interchangeable with the original photograph, but it can still be sensitive biometric information because it was created for the purpose of identifying or verifying a person.

Storage practices, retention periods and access controls vary substantially among products and organizations.

Why Does Lighting Matter?

A camera records light reflected from a person's face.

Changing the lighting changes the image presented to the recognition system. Strong shadows can hide features, bright backlighting can reduce detail and poor illumination can introduce image noise.

Modern recognition models are trained to tolerate many lighting differences, but image quality still matters.

A clear, properly exposed, forward-facing image generally provides better recognition information than a dark, blurry or heavily shadowed photograph.

Does the Camera Angle Matter?

Yes.

A face viewed directly from the front looks different from the same face viewed from the side. Parts of the face can become hidden, and apparent distances between features change because a three-dimensional object is being represented in a two-dimensional image.

Modern systems can handle a range of poses, but extreme angles can make recognition more difficult.

Face alignment and advanced neural networks help reduce this problem, but they cannot recover facial information that was never visible to the camera.

What About Glasses, Beards and Hairstyles?

Changes in appearance can affect facial recognition, but modern systems are generally designed to focus on patterns that remain relatively useful despite ordinary changes in hairstyle, facial hair or accessories.

Glasses can cover portions of the face. Masks and other coverings can hide even more information. Large changes in facial hair, makeup, aging or physical appearance may also affect similarity scores.

The effect depends on the recognition system and the quality of the available image.

A system with less visible facial information has fewer useful features from which to make its comparison.

How Does Facial Recognition Handle Aging?

Human faces change over time.

Skin texture changes, facial fat distribution can change and people may gain or lose weight, grow facial hair or develop other differences in appearance.

Modern recognition systems can often tolerate ordinary aging, particularly over modest periods, because they do not depend on one exact pixel-for-pixel image.

However, large differences between the age shown in a reference image and a current photograph can make recognition more difficult.

Some systems periodically update enrolled facial information to reflect changes in a user's appearance.

Can Facial Recognition Make Mistakes?

Yes.

Two important types of error are false matches and false non-matches.

A false match occurs when the system incorrectly determines that faces belonging to different people are a match.

A false non-match occurs when the system fails to recognize two images that actually depict the same person.

Error rates depend on the recognition algorithm, image quality, threshold settings, database size, camera conditions and the population and circumstances in which the technology is used.

Statements that facial recognition is simply accurate or inaccurate without describing the system and testing conditions can therefore be misleading.

Why Does Database Size Matter?

Imagine comparing one face against one known reference. There is only one candidate comparison.

Now imagine searching the same face against a database containing millions of people.

With so many comparisons, there are more opportunities for an unrelated face to produce a relatively high similarity score by chance or because two people have similar facial characteristics.

Large-scale identification systems therefore require careful threshold selection, testing and procedures for evaluating candidate matches.

This is one reason a one-to-one phone-unlocking system and a one-to-many database search should not be treated as equivalent applications simply because both use facial recognition.

Does Facial Recognition Work Equally Well for Everyone?

Not necessarily.

Performance can vary among demographic groups and under different imaging conditions. Differences can result from factors including the composition of training data, algorithm design, camera characteristics, image quality and the specific task being performed.

Researchers and testing organizations evaluate facial-recognition systems for differences in error rates across demographic groups.

The performance of the technology has improved substantially over time, but demographic performance differences remain an important consideration when evaluating a particular system.

The appropriate question is not simply whether facial recognition has bias, but how a specific system performs for different groups under conditions relevant to its intended use.

Can Facial Recognition Work From Security Cameras?

It can, but the quality of the camera image is critical.

A clear photograph taken at close range can contain far more useful facial information than a small face captured by a distant surveillance camera.

Motion blur, low resolution, compression, poor lighting, extreme camera angles and partially obscured faces can all reduce recognition performance.

Increasing the resolution of an entire camera image does not necessarily guarantee that each face contains enough detail for reliable identification.

Real-world surveillance footage can therefore be much more challenging than carefully controlled enrollment photographs.

Can Facial Recognition Identify Someone From a Crowd Instantly?

Sometimes systems can process many faces rapidly, but popular portrayals can exaggerate what is possible.

The system first needs usable images of the faces. It then has to detect them, generate representations and compare those representations against available reference data.

Poor image quality or an incomplete reference database can prevent reliable identification regardless of processing speed.

Most importantly, facial recognition cannot identify a person who has no useful reference information available to the system. A mathematical match requires something against which the detected face can be compared.

Is Facial Recognition the Same as Face Detection?

No.

Face detection determines that an image contains a face and locates it.

Facial recognition attempts to determine whether that face matches another known or stored face.

A camera can therefore detect faces without identifying the people in the image.

Many photography applications use face detection for autofocus, exposure or image organization without necessarily performing identity recognition.

Keeping these terms separate is important when discussing what a particular camera or software system actually does.

Is Facial Recognition the Same as Fingerprint Recognition?

Both are forms of biometric recognition, meaning they use characteristics of the human body for identification or verification.

However, they analyze different biological features.

Fingerprint systems examine patterns such as ridges and minutiae on fingers. Facial-recognition systems analyze visual characteristics of faces.

A practical difference is that facial images can sometimes be captured from a distance without physical contact, while fingerprint systems generally require a finger or fingerprint image to be presented to a sensor or otherwise obtained.

This difference contributes to some of the privacy concerns surrounding facial recognition.

How Do Phones Prevent Someone From Simply Using a Photograph?

Secure face-authentication systems can use additional techniques designed to determine whether they are observing a real person rather than a photograph or other imitation.

This is broadly known as liveness detection or presentation-attack detection.

Depending on the device, systems may use depth information, infrared imaging, movement, texture analysis or combinations of sensors and software.

Not every facial-recognition system has the same anti-spoofing capabilities. A basic application using an ordinary camera and simple recognition software may be much easier to fool than a purpose-built biometric authentication system.

Does Facial Recognition Require the Internet?

Not necessarily.

Facial recognition can be performed locally if a device has the necessary software, computing power and reference information.

Some phones perform biometric face authentication largely on the device rather than sending every facial image to a remote server.

Other facial-recognition applications use cloud computing or centralized servers, especially when searching large databases or processing images from many cameras.

Whether internet access is required depends on the architecture of the particular system, not on facial recognition itself.

What Are the Privacy Concerns?

Facial recognition raises privacy questions because a person's face is both visible in public and potentially usable as a biometric identifier.

Unlike a password, a face cannot simply be replaced if biometric information is compromised.

Concerns can involve how facial images are collected, whether people consented to their use, how long data is retained, who can search it, how accurately the system performs and whether facial recognition enables identification or tracking that people do not expect.

Laws and regulations governing facial recognition vary by jurisdiction and application.

These questions are separate from the technical question of whether a recognition algorithm can compare two facial images successfully.

Does a Match Prove Someone's Identity?

No.

A facial-recognition match means the system determined that two facial representations were sufficiently similar under its configured rules. It is evidence produced by a statistical comparison, not absolute proof of identity.

The significance of a match depends on factors such as image quality, the algorithm used, the threshold, the size and composition of the database and the circumstances of the search.

For high-consequence uses, facial-recognition results can be treated as investigative or supporting information requiring additional verification rather than as unquestionable identification.

The Bottom Line

Facial recognition works by finding a face in an image, preparing and aligning the facial region, and using algorithms to convert its visual characteristics into a mathematical representation. The system then compares that representation with one or more reference representations and calculates how similar they are.

A one-to-one verification system asks whether a face matches a particular enrolled person. A one-to-many identification system searches many possible identities for likely candidates. These are different problems with different error considerations.

Modern facial recognition relies heavily on deep-learning systems that learn useful facial representations from large collections of training images. These systems can recognize people despite many ordinary changes in lighting, expression and appearance, but their performance is not perfect.

Image quality, camera angle, database size, demographic performance, threshold settings and the particular algorithm all affect the result. A facial-recognition match is therefore a calculated similarity decision rather than absolute proof that two images show the same person.
