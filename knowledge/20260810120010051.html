What Is Climate Change?

August 10, 20265 min read

What Is Climate Change?

Climate change refers to long-term changes in Earth's average temperatures, weather patterns, and climate systems over decades or longer. While Earth's climate has changed naturally throughout its history, scientists have found that the rapid warming observed since the late 19th century is largely driven by increased concentrations of greenhouse gases produced by human activities.

Climate change affects temperatures, rainfall, sea levels, ecosystems, and weather patterns around the world. Because these changes occur over long periods, climate change is different from day-to-day weather.

What Is the Difference Between Weather and Climate?

Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions, such as today's temperature, rainfall, wind, or snowfall.

Climate describes the average pattern of weather in a region over many years, typically measured over periods of at least 30 years.

For example:

  • A snowstorm is a weather event.
  • Average winter temperatures over several decades are part of the climate.

Because weather naturally varies from day to day, individual storms or unusually hot or cold days do not by themselves demonstrate climate change.

What Causes Climate Change?

Earth's climate is influenced by many natural and human factors.

Natural influences include:

  • Changes in solar energy reaching Earth.
  • Volcanic eruptions.
  • Natural climate cycles.
  • Long-term changes in Earth's orbit and tilt.

Since the Industrial Revolution, human activities have become the dominant driver of the long-term warming trend observed by scientists.

The largest human contributors include:

  • Burning coal, oil, and natural gas.
  • Deforestation.
  • Certain agricultural practices.
  • Industrial processes.

These activities increase the concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.

What Are Greenhouse Gases?

Greenhouse gases are gases that absorb and re-emit heat within Earth's atmosphere.

The major greenhouse gases include:

  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂).
  • Methane (CH₄).
  • Nitrous oxide (N₂O).
  • Water vapor.
  • Fluorinated gases used in some industrial applications.

These gases create the greenhouse effect, a natural process that helps keep Earth warm enough to support life.

Human activities have increased the concentration of several greenhouse gases, strengthening this natural effect and contributing to rising global temperatures.

How Do Scientists Know the Climate Is Changing?

Scientists use many independent sources of evidence.

These include:

  • Surface temperature measurements.
  • Satellite observations.
  • Ocean temperature records.
  • Ice cores.
  • Tree rings.
  • Glacier measurements.
  • Sea level observations.
  • Historical weather records.

Together, these records show that Earth's average surface temperature has increased since the late 1800s and that many aspects of the climate system have changed over time.

What Are Some Observed Effects?

Scientists have documented a variety of long-term changes associated with a warming climate.

These include:

  • Rising average global temperatures.
  • Melting glaciers and ice sheets.
  • Rising sea levels.
  • Changes in precipitation patterns.
  • More frequent and intense heat waves in many regions.
  • Shifts in growing seasons and ecosystems.

The extent and severity of these effects vary by region.

Scientists continue to study how climate change influences hurricanes, floods, droughts, wildfires, and other extreme weather events. In many cases, climate change can increase the likelihood or intensity of certain events, but it is often not possible to attribute any single weather event to climate change alone.

Can Climate Change Be Reduced?

Scientists generally describe two broad approaches.

Mitigation focuses on reducing greenhouse gas emissions or increasing the removal of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

Examples include:

  • Improving energy efficiency.
  • Expanding renewable energy.
  • Developing lower-emission technologies.
  • Protecting and restoring forests.

Adaptation involves preparing for changes that are already occurring or expected in the future.

Examples include:

  • Improving flood protection.
  • Managing water resources.
  • Updating building standards.
  • Developing crops better suited to changing conditions.

Many governments, businesses, and organizations pursue a combination of mitigation and adaptation strategies.

Why Is Climate Change Sometimes Controversial?

The scientific evidence supporting the existence of long-term climate change and the role of human-generated greenhouse gases is supported by major scientific organizations around the world.

Public debate often centers on questions such as:

  • How quickly emissions should be reduced.
  • Which energy sources should be used.
  • The economic costs of different policies.
  • How governments should respond.
  • How responsibility should be shared among countries.

These policy discussions involve economic, political, and social considerations that extend beyond the underlying science.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that climate change and weather are the same thing. Climate describes long-term patterns, while weather refers to short-term conditions.

Another misconception is that a single cold winter or major snowstorm disproves climate change. Individual weather events occur naturally and do not by themselves reflect long-term climate trends.

It is also sometimes assumed that climate has never changed naturally before. In fact, Earth's climate has changed many times throughout its history. What scientists study today is the unusually rapid warming observed over the past century and the factors contributing to it.

Conclusion

Climate change refers to long-term changes in Earth's climate system, including average temperatures, precipitation patterns, and other environmental conditions. While natural processes have influenced Earth's climate throughout history, scientific evidence shows that human activities have become the primary driver of the recent warming trend by increasing greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere.

Understanding climate change requires distinguishing long-term climate trends from everyday weather while recognizing that scientific research continues to improve our understanding of how Earth's complex climate system responds to both natural and human influences.
