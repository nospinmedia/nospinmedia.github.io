What Are the Different Wi-Fi Generations?

October 9, 202619 min read

Wi-Fi has gone through many generations since the first wireless networking standards appeared in the 1990s. Each generation has improved some combination of speed, capacity, efficiency, reliability and the way multiple devices share the wireless network.

Modern generations have consumer-friendly names such as Wi-Fi 6 and Wi-Fi 7, but those names correspond to technical standards developed by the IEEE 802.11 family of wireless networking specifications.

The major generations are:

  • Early Wi-Fi: 802.11, 802.11b, 802.11a and 802.11g
  • Wi-Fi 4: 802.11n
  • Wi-Fi 5: 802.11ac
  • Wi-Fi 6: 802.11ax
  • Wi-Fi 6E: 802.11ax operating in the 6 GHz band
  • Wi-Fi 7: 802.11be

A newer generation does not simply mean a higher maximum speed. Many of the most important improvements involve allowing more devices to communicate efficiently at the same time.

Why Do Wi-Fi Generations Have Two Different Names?

For many years, Wi-Fi standards were primarily identified by IEEE names such as 802.11b, 802.11g, 802.11n and 802.11ac.

These names made sense within the standards process but were increasingly difficult for ordinary consumers to interpret. Someone shopping for a router could not easily tell from the letters whether 802.11n or 802.11ac was newer.

The Wi-Fi Alliance introduced simpler generational names for major modern standards.

802.11n became Wi-Fi 4.

802.11ac became Wi-Fi 5.

802.11ax became Wi-Fi 6.

802.11be became Wi-Fi 7.

The older 802.11b and 802.11a technologies can retrospectively be associated with Wi-Fi 1 and Wi-Fi 2, and 802.11g with Wi-Fi 3, but those numbered names are not commonly used on modern products.

What Was the Original Wi-Fi?

The original IEEE 802.11 standard was released in 1997.

It supported data rates of only 1 or 2 megabits per second. That is extremely slow compared with modern Wi-Fi, but the standard established the foundation for wireless local-area networking.

The technology quickly evolved into several improved versions.

Two particularly important early standards were 802.11b and 802.11a.

What Was 802.11b?

802.11b became one of the first widely adopted Wi-Fi technologies around the beginning of the 2000s.

It operated in the 2.4 GHz band and supported a maximum theoretical data rate of 11 megabits per second.

The 2.4 GHz band offered useful range and could penetrate walls reasonably well, but it was also shared with many other devices and technologies.

Microwave ovens, cordless phones, Bluetooth devices and neighboring Wi-Fi networks could all contribute to interference in the same general spectrum.

Despite its limitations, 802.11b helped make wireless home networking practical and popular.

What Was 802.11a?

802.11a appeared during roughly the same period as 802.11b but used a very different approach.

It operated in the 5 GHz band and supported theoretical speeds up to 54 megabits per second.

The higher-frequency band provided more available spectrum and generally less interference from common 2.4 GHz devices, but equipment was initially more expensive and 5 GHz signals generally had shorter practical range through buildings than 2.4 GHz signals.

802.11a never achieved the same early consumer popularity as 802.11b, but its use of 5 GHz anticipated an increasingly important direction for later Wi-Fi generations.

What Was 802.11g?

802.11g combined important advantages of earlier standards.

Introduced in the early 2000s, it operated in the 2.4 GHz band like 802.11b but supported theoretical speeds up to 54 megabits per second.

It was also designed for backward compatibility with 802.11b devices.

This combination helped 802.11g become extremely common in home and business wireless networks.

For many people, 802.11g represented the era when Wi-Fi became an expected feature of laptops, broadband routers and public internet access.

What Is Wi-Fi 4?

Wi-Fi 4 is the consumer name for IEEE 802.11n.

It represented a major change in Wi-Fi technology and became widely available around the end of the 2000s.

Wi-Fi 4 can operate in both the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands.

One of its most important innovations was widespread use of MIMO, or multiple-input multiple-output.

MIMO allows a wireless system to use multiple antennas and multiple spatial streams to transmit more than one stream of data through the radio environment at the same time.

Instead of treating reflected radio signals only as a problem, MIMO can exploit different signal paths to increase data capacity.

Wi-Fi 4 also introduced wider 40 MHz channels in addition to traditional 20 MHz channels.

Under ideal configurations, 802.11n could theoretically reach hundreds of megabits per second, with a maximum specified PHY rate of 600 megabits per second using four spatial streams and a 40 MHz channel.

Actual consumer devices often supported fewer spatial streams and achieved considerably lower real-world throughput.

Why Was MIMO Such a Major Improvement?

Before MIMO, increasing Wi-Fi performance depended heavily on transmitting information faster through essentially one radio stream.

MIMO allowed multiple spatial streams to carry different data simultaneously.

Imagine that a device can reliably distinguish several paths through which radio signals travel between antennas. Instead of sending the same information through every path, the system can use those paths to carry separate streams.

This can dramatically increase the amount of information transmitted during the same period.

MIMO became a foundation of later Wi-Fi generations and has grown increasingly sophisticated.

What Is Wi-Fi 5?

Wi-Fi 5 corresponds to IEEE 802.11ac.

Wi-Fi 5 focused on the 5 GHz band and substantially increased potential wireless speeds.

It expanded the use of wider channels, including 80 MHz channels and optional 160 MHz channels.

Wi-Fi 5 also supported more spatial streams and introduced multi-user MIMO, commonly called MU-MIMO, for downlink communication in later implementations.

Traditional single-user MIMO primarily improved the connection between an access point and one device at a time. MU-MIMO allowed an access point to transmit separate data streams to multiple compatible devices simultaneously under suitable conditions.

Wi-Fi 5 products could advertise theoretical aggregate PHY rates of several gigabits per second, although typical client devices used far fewer antennas and achieved much lower individual speeds.

Why Did Wi-Fi 5 Use 5 GHz?

The 5 GHz band provides considerably more spectrum for Wi-Fi than the traditional 2.4 GHz band.

That makes it easier to use wide channels without overlapping as many neighboring networks.

Wider channels can carry more data, which helped Wi-Fi 5 achieve much higher potential speeds.

The tradeoff is that 5 GHz signals generally experience greater attenuation through distance and building materials than lower-frequency 2.4 GHz signals under comparable conditions.

This is one reason dual-band networks remained important: 2.4 GHz could provide useful coverage while 5 GHz offered greater capacity and speed where signal conditions were good.

What Is Wi-Fi 6?

Wi-Fi 6 is based on IEEE 802.11ax.

Unlike Wi-Fi 5, Wi-Fi 6 operates in both the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands.

Wi-Fi 6 improved maximum speeds, but its larger goal was improving efficiency in networks containing many devices.

This became increasingly important as homes, offices, stadiums, schools and public spaces filled with phones, laptops, televisions, cameras, sensors and other wireless equipment.

Major Wi-Fi 6 technologies include OFDMA, improved MU-MIMO, higher-order modulation and features designed to reduce unnecessary device power consumption.

What Is OFDMA?

Orthogonal frequency-division multiple access, or OFDMA, is one of the most important improvements introduced with Wi-Fi 6.

Earlier Wi-Fi systems often gave most or all of a channel to one transmission at a time, even when a device had only a small amount of data to send.

OFDMA can divide a Wi-Fi channel into smaller groups of subcarriers called resource units.

Different resource units can be assigned to different devices during the same transmission opportunity.

This can make communication much more efficient when many devices need to exchange relatively small amounts of data.

The benefit is not simply higher peak speed. OFDMA can reduce waiting and improve the efficiency of a busy wireless network.

What Is 1024-QAM?

Wi-Fi transmits digital information by changing characteristics of radio signals according to a modulation scheme.

Wi-Fi 6 introduced support for 1024-QAM, allowing more bits of information to be represented by each transmitted symbol than the 256-QAM commonly associated with Wi-Fi 5.

Higher-order modulation can increase data rates when signal conditions are excellent.

However, distinguishing among more closely spaced signal states requires a cleaner and stronger radio connection.

A device far from the access point or experiencing substantial interference may automatically use a more robust, lower-order modulation instead.

This is why the highest advertised Wi-Fi rates are generally available only under favorable radio conditions.

What Is Target Wake Time?

Wi-Fi 6 introduced a feature called Target Wake Time, or TWT.

It can allow compatible devices and access points to coordinate when devices wake to transmit or receive data.

Instead of a battery-powered device repeatedly activating its radio unnecessarily, it may be able to sleep for longer periods and communicate according to an agreed schedule.

This can reduce power consumption and network contention, particularly for some Internet of Things devices.

Actual use and benefit depend on device and network support.

What Is Wi-Fi 6E?

Wi-Fi 6E is not a completely new Wi-Fi protocol generation.

It is essentially Wi-Fi 6 technology extended into the 6 GHz frequency band where regulators have made spectrum available for unlicensed wireless use.

The E stands for Extended.

Wi-Fi 6 originally operated in 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz spectrum. Wi-Fi 6E devices can additionally operate in 6 GHz.

This additional spectrum is extremely valuable because it provides many more channels and makes wide 80 MHz and 160 MHz channels easier to deploy without competing with as many neighboring legacy Wi-Fi networks.

Why Is 6 GHz Important?

The 2.4 GHz band is relatively narrow and often crowded.

The 5 GHz band provides much more capacity but can also become congested in dense environments.

Opening 6 GHz spectrum provided Wi-Fi with a large new area of radio spectrum.

In the United States, the 6 GHz Wi-Fi allocation spans 1200 MHz of spectrum from 5.925 to 7.125 GHz, although available spectrum and regulatory rules differ among countries.

The additional spectrum allows many non-overlapping wide channels.

This can significantly reduce channel contention and make very high wireless speeds more practical in environments where 6 GHz coverage is suitable.

Does 6 GHz Travel Farther Than 5 GHz?

Generally, no.

Higher-frequency radio signals tend to experience somewhat greater propagation loss under comparable conditions, and building materials can further reduce indoor coverage.

As a result, 6 GHz Wi-Fi is particularly useful for high-capacity connections at relatively short distances with good signal conditions.

It does not automatically replace 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz.

Modern networks can use different bands for different purposes, balancing coverage, capacity and device compatibility.

Can an Older Device Connect to a 6 GHz Wi-Fi Network?

Only devices with compatible 6 GHz Wi-Fi hardware and software can communicate in the 6 GHz band.

A Wi-Fi 5 device, for example, cannot suddenly use 6 GHz because the access point supports Wi-Fi 6E.

A multi-band access point can still provide 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz service for older devices while newer devices use 6 GHz.

This distinction is important: backward compatibility between Wi-Fi generations does not mean that old radio hardware can operate on frequency bands it was never designed to support.

What Is Wi-Fi 7?

Wi-Fi 7 is based on IEEE 802.11be, also known as Extremely High Throughput.

Wi-Fi 7 builds on the technologies introduced in Wi-Fi 6 and Wi-Fi 6E while substantially increasing potential throughput and flexibility.

It can operate across the 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz bands, subject to regional spectrum availability and device support.

Major Wi-Fi 7 improvements include 320 MHz channels, 4096-QAM, enhanced multi-user operation and Multi-Link Operation, or MLO.

Theoretical maximum aggregate PHY rates can exceed 40 gigabits per second in configurations using the standard's most advanced capabilities, although ordinary client devices use far fewer spatial streams and achieve much lower real-world rates.

What Are 320 MHz Channels?

A wider radio channel can carry more information at once, much like adding lanes to a highway.

Wi-Fi 5 and Wi-Fi 6 can support channels as wide as 160 MHz.

Wi-Fi 7 can double that maximum to 320 MHz where sufficient spectrum is available.

The 6 GHz band is particularly important because its large amount of contiguous spectrum can accommodate channels this wide more readily than older Wi-Fi bands.

A 320 MHz channel can potentially provide enormous throughput, but it also consumes a large amount of spectrum.

Whether such wide channels are appropriate depends on local regulations, interference, neighboring networks and the deployment environment.

What Is 4096-QAM?

Wi-Fi 7 can use 4096-QAM, also called 4K-QAM.

This modulation method can encode more bits in each symbol than Wi-Fi 6's 1024-QAM.

Under excellent signal conditions, that increases potential throughput.

The tradeoff is that 4096-QAM requires a very high-quality connection because the receiver must distinguish among a large number of closely spaced signal states.

It is therefore most useful at shorter distances with strong signal quality and low interference.

A Wi-Fi 7 connection does not continuously use 4096-QAM simply because both devices support it. The system adapts modulation according to current radio conditions.

What Is Multi-Link Operation?

Multi-Link Operation, or MLO, is one of the defining capabilities of Wi-Fi 7.

Traditional Wi-Fi clients generally establish their active connection through a particular radio link at a time.

With MLO, compatible Wi-Fi 7 devices can coordinate communication using multiple links across available bands or channels.

Depending on the implementation and operating mode, this can allow multiple links to contribute to throughput, provide alternate paths when one link is busy or reduce latency by giving traffic more opportunities to be transmitted.

For example, a compatible device might have links associated with both 5 GHz and 6 GHz resources rather than treating them entirely as unrelated connections.

MLO is therefore not merely a speed feature. It can also improve efficiency, reliability and latency.

Does Wi-Fi 7 Mean Every Device Gets Multi-Gigabit Speed?

No.

The maximum numbers associated with a Wi-Fi generation describe capabilities available under particular configurations, not guaranteed speeds for every device.

A typical phone or laptop has fewer antennas and spatial streams than a high-end access point.

The client may also support narrower channels, be too far from the access point to use the highest modulation rate or encounter interference from other networks.

Internet speed can impose another limit.

A Wi-Fi link capable of several gigabits per second cannot download from the internet at several gigabits per second if the internet connection itself is slower.

The Ethernet connection between an access point and the rest of the network can also become a bottleneck.

What Is a Spatial Stream?

Modern Wi-Fi devices can use multiple antennas to transmit and receive independent streams of information.

These are called spatial streams.

A notation such as 2x2 MIMO commonly indicates that a device has two transmit and two receive radio chains and can generally support up to two spatial streams in the relevant configuration.

Higher-end access points may support many more spatial streams than individual phones or laptops.

This is one reason adding together all of an access point's theoretical radio capabilities can produce a marketing number far higher than the speed any single client will ever receive.

The access point's additional radio resources can still be valuable because they help it serve multiple clients efficiently.

What Does a Router Rating Such as AX3000 or BE11000 Mean?

Consumer networking products frequently use labels such as AX3000, AX6000, BE9300 or BE11000.

The letters generally identify the underlying Wi-Fi generation: AX refers to 802.11ax, or Wi-Fi 6/6E, while BE refers to 802.11be, or Wi-Fi 7.

The number is usually an approximate total of the maximum theoretical PHY rates available across the product's radio bands under particular configurations.

It is not a promise that one device will achieve that speed.

For example, a tri-band access point may add together theoretical capacity from its 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz radios to produce the advertised class number even though one ordinary client cannot use all of that capacity as a single conventional connection.

These labels are therefore most useful for comparing broad product classes rather than predicting actual throughput.

Why Is Real Wi-Fi Speed Lower Than the Advertised Rate?

Wi-Fi speed specifications usually refer to the PHY rate, the raw signaling rate of the wireless link.

Actual application throughput is lower.

Wi-Fi requires management traffic, acknowledgments, error correction, contention procedures, encryption overhead and other protocol operations.

Devices also share airtime with other clients.

Signal strength, interference, channel width, modulation, spatial streams and retransmissions all affect performance.

The result is that a device reporting a 1.2 gigabit-per-second Wi-Fi link should not be expected to transfer application data at exactly 1.2 gigabits per second.

The link rate and useful throughput measure different things.

Why Doesn't a Newer Wi-Fi Generation Always Have Better Range?

Newer Wi-Fi standards improve efficiency and signal-processing capabilities, but they do not repeal the physics of radio propagation.

Range depends heavily on frequency, transmit power, antenna design, receiver sensitivity, walls, floors, interference and the required data rate.

A newer Wi-Fi device may maintain useful performance more effectively under some conditions, but Wi-Fi 7 does not automatically travel farther than Wi-Fi 6 simply because the number is larger.

The frequency band often matters more for coverage than the generation label.

A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi 6 connection may reach farther through a building than a 6 GHz Wi-Fi 7 connection, even though the Wi-Fi 7 link can provide much greater capacity at shorter range.

Are Wi-Fi Generations Backward Compatible?

Generally, Wi-Fi is designed with substantial backward compatibility.

A modern access point can often communicate with devices using older Wi-Fi generations when it supports the appropriate frequency band and legacy modes.

A Wi-Fi 6 access point operating at 5 GHz, for example, can normally communicate with compatible Wi-Fi 5 clients.

The connection uses capabilities supported by both sides. An older client does not gain Wi-Fi 6 features merely because it connects to a Wi-Fi 6 access point.

There are limits to backward compatibility. Very old standards may be disabled on modern equipment for performance or security reasons, and 6 GHz operation requires newer equipment designed for that band.

Can One Old Wi-Fi Device Slow Down the Whole Network?

An older device can consume disproportionate airtime, particularly when it communicates at a low data rate, but the idea that one old device automatically forces every other client to operate at the same slow speed is misleading.

Wi-Fi devices can transmit using different rates and capabilities.

However, slower clients require more airtime to transfer the same amount of data. Legacy compatibility mechanisms can also introduce additional overhead.

Because airtime is a shared resource, inefficient clients can reduce the amount of airtime available to faster devices.

This effect is particularly important in dense networks with many active clients.

Does Wi-Fi 7 Require Wi-Fi 7 on Both Ends?

Yes, if you want to use Wi-Fi 7-specific capabilities.

A Wi-Fi 7 access point can usually communicate with older compatible clients, but a Wi-Fi 6 phone connecting to it still operates using Wi-Fi 6 capabilities.

Features such as Wi-Fi 7 Multi-Link Operation require compatible Wi-Fi 7 hardware and software on both sides of the connection.

Upgrading only the access point can still improve a network in some circumstances, especially if the old access point was a bottleneck, but existing clients do not magically acquire newer radio capabilities.

Is Wi-Fi 6E Faster Than Wi-Fi 6?

Wi-Fi 6E uses the same fundamental 802.11ax technology as Wi-Fi 6, so it is not inherently a faster modulation standard simply because of the E.

Its major advantage is access to the 6 GHz band.

That additional spectrum can provide cleaner channels and make wide channels easier to use, which can produce substantially better real-world performance in suitable environments.

A good Wi-Fi 6 connection at 5 GHz can still outperform a poor Wi-Fi 6E connection at 6 GHz.

The generation and band determine capabilities, but actual radio conditions determine what performance is achievable at a particular moment.

Is Wi-Fi 7 Always Faster Than Wi-Fi 6E?

Wi-Fi 7 has much greater maximum capabilities, but an individual connection is not guaranteed to be faster.

If both connections use similar channel widths, spatial streams and modulation because of device or signal limitations, the difference may be modest.

Wi-Fi 7's largest advantages appear when devices can take advantage of features such as 320 MHz channels, Multi-Link Operation, higher modulation rates and other improvements.

Network congestion, distance and the wired infrastructure behind the access point can also dominate performance.

A well-designed Wi-Fi 6E network can therefore outperform a poorly designed Wi-Fi 7 network.

What Happens When the Wired Network Is Slower Than Wi-Fi?

Modern Wi-Fi can be fast enough that the Ethernet connection feeding an access point becomes the limiting factor.

For many years, access points commonly used 1-gigabit Ethernet connections because Wi-Fi application throughput rarely exceeded that speed for an individual device.

Newer Wi-Fi systems can exceed 1 gigabit per second under favorable conditions.

As a result, higher-performance access points increasingly use 2.5-gigabit, 5-gigabit or faster Ethernet connections.

A client might establish a wireless link capable of several gigabits per second, but traffic traveling through a 1-gigabit Ethernet uplink cannot exceed the practical capacity of that wired connection.

A fast wireless network therefore depends on the infrastructure behind the access points as well as the Wi-Fi radios themselves.

Which Wi-Fi Generation Do You Actually Need?

The answer depends on the network.

Wi-Fi 5 remains capable of handling many ordinary internet activities, but Wi-Fi 6 provides important efficiency improvements for environments with many devices.

Wi-Fi 6E adds access to 6 GHz spectrum, which can be particularly useful in crowded environments or for high-speed connections over shorter distances.

Wi-Fi 7 adds significantly greater potential capacity and flexibility, especially through Multi-Link Operation and wider channels.

But buying the newest generation does not automatically solve poor Wi-Fi coverage, interference, bad access-point placement or a slow internet connection.

Network design remains important regardless of the generation being used.

The Bottom Line

Wi-Fi generations represent successive improvements to the IEEE 802.11 family of wireless networking technologies.

Early standards such as 802.11b and 802.11g helped establish Wi-Fi as a practical replacement for Ethernet cables in many everyday devices. Wi-Fi 4 introduced widespread MIMO and operation across both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. Wi-Fi 5 greatly expanded 5 GHz capacity and introduced technologies such as wider channels and downlink MU-MIMO.

Wi-Fi 6 focused heavily on efficiency as networks became crowded with many devices, introducing technologies such as OFDMA and 1024-QAM. Wi-Fi 6E extended Wi-Fi 6 into the large new 6 GHz band.

Wi-Fi 7 builds on those developments with capabilities including 320 MHz channels, 4096-QAM and Multi-Link Operation, allowing compatible equipment to use wireless resources more flexibly and reach much higher potential throughput.

The number on the box, however, tells only part of the story. Real Wi-Fi performance depends on the capabilities of both the access point and client, frequency band, channel width, spatial streams, signal strength, interference, network congestion and the speed of the wired network behind the access point.

A newer generation provides more tools for building a faster and more efficient network, but good Wi-Fi still depends on good network design and favorable radio conditions.
