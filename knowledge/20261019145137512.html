Why Do Gas Prices Rise Quickly but Fall Slowly?

October 26, 202613 min read

When crude oil prices rise sharply, drivers often see gasoline prices increase within days. But when oil prices fall, the price at the pump may decline much more slowly.

Sometimes crude oil becomes cheaper than it was before the original increase, yet gasoline still costs more than it did at the beginning.

This pattern is familiar to motorists, and economists have studied it for decades. It is commonly called the rockets-and-feathers effect: gasoline prices can rise like a rocket but fall like a feather.

The explanation involves more than the price of crude oil. Wholesale gasoline markets, refinery operations, fuel inventories, local competition, and retail pricing decisions all influence how quickly changes reach consumers.

The pattern is real in many markets, although it does not occur in every situation. Understanding it requires looking at what happens between the oil market and the gas pump.

What Is the Rockets-and-Feathers Effect?

The rockets-and-feathers effect describes a situation in which retail prices respond more quickly to increases in underlying costs than to equivalent decreases.

Economists call this asymmetric price adjustment.

For example, suppose crude oil prices increase by $20 per barrel. Gasoline prices might rise substantially over the following days or weeks.

Later, crude oil prices fall by the same $20. Drivers might expect gasoline prices to return to their original level just as quickly.

Instead, the decline may take considerably longer.

Research has found evidence of this behavior in gasoline markets, although studies differ about its size, causes, and consistency. Some research finds strong asymmetry, while other studies find that the result depends on the market, time period, and statistical methods used.

The effect is therefore a documented economic phenomenon, not a rule that applies to every price movement.

Why Do Gas Stations Raise Prices So Quickly?

Gas stations purchase fuel from suppliers and must continually replace the gasoline they sell.

When wholesale gasoline prices increase, a station faces the prospect of paying more for its next delivery.

Even if the gasoline currently in its underground tanks was purchased at a lower price, the station may raise its retail price to reflect the higher cost of replacing that inventory.

This is known as replacement-cost pricing.

Consider a simplified example.

A station purchases gasoline for $2.50 per gallon before taxes and retail expenses. Its supplier then announces that the next delivery will cost $3.00 per gallon.

The station may increase its pump price before receiving the more expensive delivery because it must prepare to purchase replacement fuel at the new price.

That does not necessarily mean the station is earning an unusually large profit. The higher selling price may help offset the higher cost of its next purchase.

But replacement costs are only part of the explanation, especially when prices are falling.

Why Don't Prices Fall Just as Quickly?

When wholesale prices decline, retailers do not always face the same immediate pressure to lower their selling prices.

A station may still have fuel purchased at a higher price. It may also be uncertain whether the wholesale decline will last.

More importantly, the pressure to pass savings along depends partly on competition.

If nearby stations maintain higher prices, an individual retailer may have little immediate incentive to make a large reduction unless doing so would attract enough additional customers.

When one station cuts its price, competing stations may respond. But that competitive process can unfold gradually.

As a result, retail margins may temporarily increase while wholesale fuel costs decline.

The size and duration of that increase vary by market. A larger margin does not automatically equal a larger net profit because retailers still have operating expenses.

Why Can Oil Fall Below Its Original Price While Gasoline Remains Expensive?

This is one of the most important questions about gasoline pricing.

Suppose crude oil initially costs $75 per barrel and gasoline sells for $3.10 per gallon.

Oil then rises to $95 per barrel, and gasoline increases to $3.85 per gallon.

Several weeks later, oil falls to $70 per barrel, which is $5 below its original price.

But gasoline is still selling for $3.50 per gallon.

How can gasoline remain 40 cents more expensive when crude oil is cheaper than before?

There are several possible explanations.

First, the wholesale price of finished gasoline may not have fallen as much as crude oil. Refinery capacity, gasoline demand, seasonal fuel requirements, and regional supply conditions can keep gasoline expensive even when crude becomes cheaper.

Second, wholesale gasoline prices may have declined, but retailers and distributors have not yet passed the full reduction through to consumers.

Third, some costs may have changed during the period. Transportation expenses, refinery operations, taxes, or regional supply disruptions can affect the final price independently of crude oil.

Finally, prices can reflect different points in time. A crude oil quotation is a current market price, while the gasoline sold at a station may have moved through several stages of production and distribution.

The key distinction is that a lower crude oil price does not automatically mean an equally large or immediate reduction in the retail price of gasoline.

Crude Oil and Wholesale Gasoline Are Different Markets

Crude oil is the raw material from which gasoline is produced.

Wholesale gasoline is a finished or nearly finished petroleum product sold through a separate market.

Although their prices are closely related, they are not identical.

Refineries produce gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and other products from crude oil. The market value of those products depends on their own supply and demand conditions.

If gasoline supplies are tight, wholesale gasoline prices can remain elevated even when crude oil prices decline.

For example, an unexpected refinery shutdown may reduce the amount of gasoline available to distributors. Buyers may then compete for supplies from other refineries, keeping wholesale prices high.

A driver comparing crude oil prices with local pump prices may therefore be comparing two markets that are moving differently.

To understand the difference, it helps to examine wholesale gasoline prices as well as crude oil prices.

What Role Do Refinery Margins Play?

Refineries purchase crude oil and convert it into gasoline and other petroleum products.

The difference between the value of refined products and the cost of crude oil is often measured using a refining margin or an indicator called the crack spread.

A crack spread is not the same as refinery profit. It is a simplified measure that does not include all operating costs.

Suppose crude oil prices fall while wholesale gasoline prices remain relatively high.

The difference between the two prices increases, creating a larger estimated refining margin.

That can happen when gasoline demand is strong, refinery capacity is limited, or disruptions reduce available fuel supplies.

Conversely, refining margins may shrink when gasoline supplies are plentiful or demand weakens.

This explains why gasoline prices can remain high even when the raw material used to produce gasoline becomes cheaper.

It also shows why the price of crude oil alone cannot establish whether refiners or retailers are earning unusually high profits.

Do Gas Stations Make More Money When Oil Prices Fall?

They sometimes can, particularly if their wholesale fuel costs decline faster than their retail selling prices.

The difference between a station's selling price and its fuel acquisition cost is commonly described as its gross fuel margin.

That margin must cover expenses such as labor, electricity, equipment maintenance, property costs, insurance, and payment-processing fees.

When wholesale costs fall and pump prices remain elevated, the gross margin may temporarily increase.

However, an increase in gross margin does not necessarily mean the same increase in net profit.

The station may have previously experienced compressed margins when wholesale prices rose. It may also face changes in sales volume or operating expenses.

Some stations are independently owned, while others operate under agreements with fuel suppliers or larger companies. Those arrangements can influence how prices are established.

To determine whether retailers are retaining more of a wholesale price reduction, the relevant comparison is between retail prices and the prices stations actually pay for gasoline.

How Does Competition Affect Price Reductions?

Competition is one of the most important factors influencing how quickly retail prices respond to falling costs.

Imagine two communities.

In the first, several gas stations operate close together. Drivers can easily compare prices and choose a cheaper station.

If one station lowers its price, nearby competitors may need to respond to avoid losing customers.

In the second community, only one or two stations serve a large area. Drivers may have fewer convenient alternatives.

In that market, a retailer may face less immediate pressure to reduce prices.

Competition does not guarantee identical prices or immediate reductions. Stations have different supplier contracts, operating costs, and business strategies.

But the ability of consumers to compare prices and switch stations can influence how rapidly lower wholesale costs reach the pump.

Economists have also developed models showing that asymmetric pricing can arise even when businesses are competing and consumers have incomplete information about prices.

Illegal coordination is not required for the rockets-and-feathers effect to occur.

Do Fuel Inventories Explain the Delay?

Inventories contribute to pricing delays, but they do not provide a complete explanation.

Fuel passes through refineries, pipelines, storage terminals, distributors, and retail stations before reaching consumers.

Some gasoline is purchased under arrangements that reflect earlier prices. Other supplies are priced closer to current market conditions.

When wholesale prices fall, a station may still have inventory purchased at a higher cost.

But when wholesale prices rise, retailers may adjust pump prices based on expected replacement costs rather than waiting until every gallon of cheaper inventory has been sold.

That difference is one reason drivers question why inventory costs seem to matter more when prices are falling.

The answer is that pricing decisions reflect both past purchases and anticipated future costs, along with competition and market conditions.

Inventory timing can explain some delays. It cannot, by itself, explain every prolonged difference between crude oil and retail gasoline prices.

How Long Should It Take Gas Prices to Fall?

There is no fixed number of days that applies to every market.

The speed of adjustment depends on the size of the wholesale price change, whether it persists, refinery and distribution conditions, retail competition, and other factors.

An analysis published by the U.S. Energy Information Administration found that, under the market conditions and statistical methods it studied, roughly half of a crude oil price change was typically reflected in retail gasoline prices within two weeks, holding other factors constant.

That is an average relationship, not a guarantee that prices will return to a particular level within two weeks.

Researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis have also examined the slower adjustment of gasoline prices when crude oil becomes cheaper.

In an analysis published in August 2026, they used a statistical model to examine a hypothetical situation in which oil prices returned to their earlier level and then remained unchanged.

The model suggested that gasoline prices could take approximately six months to return to within 25 cents per gallon of their earlier level under those assumed conditions.

That result was a model-based illustration, not a universal waiting period or a forecast for every future price decline.

The broader lesson is that gasoline prices can remain elevated well after the initial oil price increase has reversed.

Is This Evidence of Price Gouging?

Not by itself.

The rockets-and-feathers effect demonstrates that prices may respond differently to rising and falling costs. It does not automatically establish unlawful conduct.

Price gouging has different legal definitions depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances. Some laws apply specifically during declared emergencies or other defined situations.

A station charging more than its competitors, maintaining a high retail margin, or reducing prices slowly is not necessarily violating the law.

At the same time, higher margins can be a legitimate subject of economic scrutiny.

Researchers and regulators may examine whether prices reflect supply constraints, operating costs, competition, or conduct that violates applicable laws.

Illegal price fixing or other anticompetitive behavior requires evidence of conduct prohibited by law.

The distinction matters: an observed price pattern can be real without proving that every business involved is acting improperly.

Why Don't Gas Prices Always Follow the Same Pattern?

The rockets-and-feathers effect is not equally strong everywhere or during every period.

In some markets, gasoline prices fall rapidly when wholesale costs decline.

A station may lower prices aggressively to attract customers. A sudden increase in gasoline supplies may also produce substantial wholesale price reductions.

Regional differences matter. Refineries, pipelines, environmental fuel specifications, transportation costs, and competition can vary widely.

Research findings also depend on which prices are compared and how the data are analyzed.

Some studies identify substantial differences between the speed of price increases and decreases. Others find weaker or statistically uncertain asymmetry under particular conditions.

It is therefore more accurate to say that gasoline prices often rise faster than they fall, rather than claiming they always do.

How Can Drivers Tell Whether Lower Oil Prices Are Reaching the Pump?

The most useful approach is to compare several prices over the same period.

Crude oil prices show what is happening to the primary raw material.

Wholesale gasoline prices show what buyers are paying for gasoline before it reaches retail stations.

Retail gasoline prices show what consumers actually pay.

If crude oil falls but wholesale gasoline remains high, refining or broader gasoline supply conditions may be responsible for much of the difference.

If wholesale gasoline falls substantially while retail prices remain elevated, the distribution and retail portion of the price has widened, at least temporarily.

That difference may reflect costs, timing, or pricing behavior, and it deserves closer examination before conclusions are drawn.

Taxes should also be considered. Most fuel taxes do not immediately change simply because crude oil prices rise or fall, although tax structures vary by jurisdiction.

Comparing the same region and gasoline grade over a consistent period gives a more meaningful picture than comparing a national crude oil price with one local gas station.

Will Gasoline Eventually Return to Its Earlier Price?

Not necessarily.

If crude oil prices fall and remain low, that generally creates downward pressure on gasoline prices, all else being equal.

But other costs and market conditions may have changed since the original price increase.

Refining margins could remain higher, transportation expenses could increase, gasoline demand could strengthen, or a region could experience a supply shortage.

Those factors can keep gasoline prices above their previous level even after crude oil becomes cheaper.

On the other hand, if wholesale gasoline prices decline, supplies are adequate, and competition is strong, retail prices may eventually approach or fall below their earlier levels.

There is no economic rule requiring gasoline to return to a particular historical price simply because crude oil returns to an earlier price.

The Bottom Line

Gasoline prices often rise quickly when oil or wholesale fuel becomes more expensive but decline more slowly when those costs fall. Economists call this the rockets-and-feathers effect.

Part of the explanation involves replacement fuel costs, inventories, refinery operations, and the time required for wholesale price changes to move through the supply chain.

Competition and retail pricing decisions also matter. When wholesale prices fall, some businesses may maintain higher selling prices for a period, increasing their gross margins until competitive pressure or other conditions lead to further reductions.

Crude oil prices and finished gasoline prices are not identical, which helps explain why oil can fall below its previous price while gasoline remains more expensive than before.

The effect has been documented in economic research, but it is not universal, and it does not automatically prove illegal price gouging.

To understand whether consumers are receiving the benefit of falling oil prices, the most revealing comparison is not just crude oil against pump prices. It is the relationship among crude oil, wholesale gasoline, and the final retail price.
