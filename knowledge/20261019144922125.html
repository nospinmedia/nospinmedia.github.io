Why Do Gasoline Prices Rise When Oil Prices Increase?

October 25, 202612 min read

When crude oil prices rise, gasoline prices usually follow. But the relationship is not as simple as adding a fixed amount to the cost of a barrel of oil.

The price drivers see at a gas station reflects several separate costs, including crude oil, refining, transportation, distribution, taxes, and retail operations. Each can change independently.

Crude oil is usually the largest single component of the retail gasoline price, which is why major changes in global oil markets often become noticeable at the pump.

Understanding how these costs fit together helps explain why gasoline prices can change even when local supplies appear normal.

What Is Crude Oil?

Crude oil is a naturally occurring mixture of hydrocarbons found in underground geological formations.

It is extracted through wells and transported to refineries, where it is separated and processed into useful products.

These products include gasoline, diesel fuel, jet fuel, heating oil, lubricants, and materials used in chemical manufacturing.

Crude oil cannot simply be pumped directly into a modern gasoline-powered vehicle. It must first be refined into fuel that meets the required specifications.

Because gasoline begins with crude oil, changes in the cost of crude affect the economics of producing gasoline.

How Much of the Gasoline Price Comes From Crude Oil?

Crude oil generally accounts for the largest portion of the price consumers pay for gasoline.

The exact percentage changes with market conditions.

The U.S. Energy Information Administration divides the retail gasoline price into four major components:

  • Crude oil: The cost of the raw petroleum used to make gasoline.
  • Refining: The cost of processing crude oil into finished gasoline.
  • Distribution and marketing: The costs of transporting, storing, distributing, and selling gasoline.
  • Taxes: Federal, state, and applicable local fuel taxes.

Crude oil can account for roughly half or more of the retail price in many market conditions, but its share can be substantially higher or lower.

When crude oil becomes more expensive, refiners generally face higher raw-material costs. Those costs influence the wholesale prices charged for gasoline, which eventually affect retail prices.

Why Does Oil Cost More at Some Times Than Others?

Crude oil is traded in international markets, and its price responds to supply and demand.

Supply depends on how much oil producers can and choose to extract, how much is available in storage, and whether oil can be transported to buyers.

Demand depends on economic activity, transportation, industrial production, seasonal consumption, and other factors.

Prices can rise when demand increases faster than available supply or when market participants expect future supplies to become tighter.

For example, an interruption at a major oil-producing facility may reduce available supply. Even if the disruption occurs far from the United States, it can influence international prices.

Oil prices can also rise because of shipping restrictions, sanctions, production decisions, or concerns about possible future disruptions.

The price reflects not only the oil available today but also expectations about future market conditions.

Why Do International Events Affect Gasoline Prices in America?

The United States is a major producer of crude oil, but American oil prices are still connected to global markets.

Oil is traded internationally, and producers generally have economic incentives to sell their products where they can obtain competitive prices after accounting for transportation, quality, and other costs.

If a major international disruption reduces available supplies, buyers may compete for oil from other producers.

That competition can raise prices across multiple markets, including the United States.

For example, a conflict affecting an important oil-exporting region can increase shipping costs, reduce production, or create uncertainty about future supplies.

Even if no American refinery loses access to oil immediately, the market price of crude may rise because the global balance between supply and demand has changed.

Domestic production helps supply the American market, but it does not completely isolate consumers from international price movements.

How Does a Refinery Turn Oil Into Gasoline?

A refinery processes crude oil into fuels and other petroleum products.

One of the first steps is distillation, which separates crude oil into components based on their boiling temperatures.

Additional processes, including cracking, reforming, and treating, convert and improve these components so they can be blended into finished fuels.

Gasoline must meet specifications involving properties such as octane, volatility, and environmental requirements.

Refineries also produce diesel, jet fuel, and other products from the same crude oil supply.

The amount of gasoline produced depends on the type of crude oil, refinery equipment, operating conditions, and product demand.

Refining requires energy, specialized equipment, workers, maintenance, and compliance with safety and environmental regulations.

These costs are reflected in the price of finished gasoline.

Why Can Gasoline Prices Rise Even When Crude Oil Prices Stay the Same?

Crude oil is only one part of the gasoline supply chain.

Gasoline prices can increase because of problems or changes in refining, distribution, or regional supply.

For example, a refinery may temporarily shut down because of equipment problems, planned maintenance, severe weather, or an unexpected emergency.

If the refinery normally supplies a large region, the disruption can reduce available gasoline even when crude oil remains plentiful.

Wholesale gasoline prices may rise as distributors compete for fuel from other sources.

Transportation problems can have similar effects. Pipeline outages, shipping delays, and other logistical restrictions can increase costs or temporarily limit supplies.

This is why gasoline prices sometimes rise in one region while remaining relatively stable elsewhere.

What Are Refining Margins?

A refining margin is a measure of the difference between the value of refined petroleum products and the cost of the crude oil used to produce them.

One commonly discussed measure is the crack spread.

Despite its unusual name, the crack spread is simply a way of estimating the economic value created by turning crude oil into products such as gasoline and diesel.

For example, if the market value of the products obtained from a quantity of crude oil rises faster than the cost of the crude itself, the estimated refining margin may increase.

A larger crack spread does not necessarily mean a refinery is earning an equally large profit. It does not account for all operating expenses, transportation costs, maintenance, financing, and other costs.

Refining margins change with fuel demand, refinery capacity, seasonal requirements, and market conditions.

They help explain why gasoline prices do not always move in perfect proportion to crude oil prices.

How Does Gasoline Get From a Refinery to a Gas Station?

After refining, gasoline travels through a distribution network before reaching consumers.

Large volumes are transported through pipelines, ships, barges, rail systems, and storage terminals.

At distribution terminals, gasoline may be blended with ethanol and additives before being loaded into tanker trucks.

Those trucks deliver the finished fuel to gas stations, where it is stored in underground or aboveground tanks until purchased.

Each stage has costs involving transportation, equipment, storage, labor, insurance, and operations.

These costs vary by location.

A station close to a major distribution terminal may face different transportation expenses from a remote station that requires longer truck deliveries.

Regional supply arrangements and fuel specifications can also affect costs.

How Much Do Taxes Add to Gasoline Prices?

Gasoline taxes are another component of the retail price.

In the United States, the federal gasoline excise tax is generally 18.4 cents per gallon, including the portion associated with the federal Leaking Underground Storage Tank Trust Fund.

States impose their own fuel taxes, which vary significantly. Some jurisdictions also have additional local taxes or other charges.

Certain states use fixed per-gallon taxes, while others include variable components that may change according to statutory formulas or scheduled adjustments.

Because tax systems differ, two states can have noticeably different gasoline prices even when their underlying fuel costs are similar.

A fixed tax per gallon does not automatically increase when crude oil prices rise. However, taxes tied to fuel prices or adjusted through other mechanisms may change over time.

How Do Gas Stations Set Their Prices?

Most gas stations do not produce gasoline. They purchase fuel from wholesalers, distributors, or suppliers and sell it to motorists.

The station's retail price must account for the cost of purchasing fuel, operating expenses, applicable taxes, and the amount the business expects to earn.

Operating expenses can include employee wages, electricity, equipment maintenance, insurance, payment-processing fees, and property costs.

Competition also matters.

A station located near several competitors may price fuel differently from a station with few nearby alternatives.

Some stations use gasoline sales to attract customers who may also purchase food, beverages, or other products inside the store.

Retail prices therefore reflect both wholesale market conditions and local business decisions.

Why Do Gasoline Prices Sometimes Rise Quickly but Fall Slowly?

Drivers often notice that gasoline prices increase soon after oil prices rise but appear to decline more gradually when oil becomes cheaper.

Economists sometimes describe this pattern as asymmetric price adjustment, informally called the rockets-and-feathers effect.

Several factors can contribute.

When wholesale costs rise, retailers may increase prices to cover the higher cost of replacing fuel inventory. When costs fall, some stations may reduce prices more gradually depending on competition, inventory costs, and expectations about future prices.

Wholesale price changes also take time to move through distribution contracts, storage facilities, and retail inventories.

The pattern is not universal. In competitive markets, retail prices can fall quickly, and changes in local supply or demand can outweigh movements in crude oil prices.

A slow decline in retail prices does not, by itself, establish that retailers are acting unlawfully or coordinating prices.

Does a Gas Station Sell Fuel Based on What It Paid or What Replacement Fuel Will Cost?

Both historical purchase costs and expected replacement costs can influence pricing.

Suppose a station purchased gasoline at one wholesale price, but the cost of its next delivery rises sharply.

The station may raise its retail price before the existing inventory is exhausted because it must generate enough revenue to purchase replacement fuel and cover operating expenses.

The opposite can occur when wholesale prices decline, although competitive pressures and pricing decisions affect how quickly those savings reach customers.

This helps explain why retail prices can change even though the gasoline already stored in the station's tanks was purchased earlier.

Why Are Gasoline Prices Different Across the Country?

Gasoline prices can vary substantially between regions.

Several factors contribute to these differences, including state taxes, transportation distances, refinery availability, local competition, and environmental fuel requirements.

Some areas require gasoline formulations designed to reduce air pollution under particular conditions.

Producing and distributing these fuels can involve additional costs, especially when only certain refineries or suppliers provide them.

Regions with limited pipeline connections or fewer nearby refineries may also face higher transportation expenses or greater exposure to local supply disruptions.

Even neighboring communities can have different prices because of retail competition, operating costs, and supplier arrangements.

Why Does Gasoline Sometimes Cost More in Summer?

Gasoline prices often experience seasonal changes.

In many parts of the United States, gasoline demand increases during warmer months as people travel more.

Refineries and fuel distributors also transition between winter and summer gasoline specifications.

Summer gasoline generally must meet stricter volatility requirements to reduce evaporative emissions that contribute to air pollution.

Producing and distributing summer-grade gasoline can be more expensive in some markets.

Refinery maintenance schedules and regional supply conditions can further influence seasonal prices.

However, summer prices are not guaranteed to be higher every year. Global crude oil prices and other market forces can outweigh typical seasonal patterns.

Would Producing More American Oil Lower Gasoline Prices?

Increasing domestic oil production can add supply to the market and may place downward pressure on crude oil prices, all else being equal.

However, the effect depends on how much production increases, when the additional oil becomes available, the type of crude produced, and conditions in the global market.

American refineries are designed to process particular combinations of crude oil. Not every barrel produced domestically is an ideal substitute for every imported barrel.

Additional crude production also does not automatically increase gasoline output if refining capacity or distribution systems are constrained.

Because crude oil is traded internationally, changes in worldwide production and demand remain important even when domestic output increases.

Can the Government Control Gasoline Prices?

Governments can influence gasoline prices through taxes, regulations, energy policies, emergency measures, and decisions involving public petroleum reserves.

For example, a government may temporarily reduce a fuel tax or release crude oil from emergency reserves under applicable legal authority.

Such actions can affect market conditions, but their effects depend on supply, demand, timing, and the size of the intervention.

The federal government does not ordinarily establish a single retail gasoline price that every American station must charge.

Gasoline prices are primarily determined through market transactions involving producers, refiners, distributors, retailers, and consumers.

Laws also prohibit certain forms of anticompetitive conduct, and authorities may investigate violations when supported by evidence.

Does the Price of a Barrel of Oil Translate Directly Into the Price of a Gallon of Gasoline?

A standard barrel of crude oil contains 42 U.S. gallons.

Dividing the price of a barrel by 42 provides a rough crude oil cost per gallon of input, but it does not calculate the retail price of gasoline.

For example, at a hypothetical crude oil price of $84 per barrel, the crude input cost would be $2 per gallon of crude oil.

That does not mean gasoline should sell for $2 per gallon.

A refinery produces multiple products from crude oil, and the yields depend on the crude and refining processes. Refining costs, product market values, transportation, taxes, and retail expenses must also be considered.

The relationship between crude oil prices and gasoline prices is therefore strong but not a simple one-to-one calculation.

The Bottom Line

Gasoline prices usually rise when crude oil becomes more expensive because crude oil is the primary raw material used to produce gasoline and often represents the largest component of its retail cost.

But crude oil is only part of the final price.

Refineries must process the oil, distributors must transport the fuel, governments impose taxes, and retailers must cover operating expenses. Supply disruptions, seasonal fuel requirements, and local competition can also affect what drivers pay.

Prices at the pump do not always change immediately or by the same amount as crude oil prices because each stage of the supply chain has its own costs and market conditions.

Understanding these components explains why gasoline prices can rise or fall for reasons that extend well beyond the price of oil alone.
