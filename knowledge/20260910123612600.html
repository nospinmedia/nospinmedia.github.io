What Is Radioactive Half-Life?

October 6, 202614 min read

Radioactive half-life is the amount of time required for half of the radioactive atoms in a large sample to decay.

If a radioactive material has a half-life of 10 years, after 10 years about half of the original radioactive atoms will remain. After another 10 years, half of those remaining atoms will have decayed, leaving about one-quarter of the original amount. After another 10 years, about one-eighth will remain.

The process continues in the same way. A half-life does not mean that all of the material disappears after two half-lives, nor does it tell us when one particular atom will decay.

Instead, half-life describes the predictable statistical behavior of a large population of unstable atomic nuclei.

What Makes an Atom Radioactive?

Atoms contain a central nucleus made of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons.

Different forms of the same chemical element can contain different numbers of neutrons. These forms are called isotopes.

Some combinations of protons and neutrons form stable nuclei that can persist indefinitely under ordinary conditions. Other combinations are unstable.

An unstable nucleus can spontaneously transform into a more stable configuration while releasing particles, electromagnetic radiation or both.

This process is called radioactive decay.

An isotope that undergoes radioactive decay is called a radioisotope or radionuclide.

What Exactly Is a Half-Life?

Half-life describes how quickly a particular radioactive isotope decays statistically.

Suppose a sample begins with 1,000,000 radioactive atoms and the isotope has a half-life of one year.

After one year, approximately 500,000 radioactive atoms would be expected to remain.

After two years, approximately 250,000 would remain.

After three years, approximately 125,000 would remain.

After four years, approximately 62,500 would remain.

The important point is that each half-life removes approximately half of whatever radioactive atoms remain at that time, not half of the original amount every time.

Why Is Radioactive Decay Random?

For an individual unstable nucleus, radioactive decay is fundamentally probabilistic according to quantum mechanics.

Scientists can determine the probability that a nucleus of a particular isotope will decay during a given interval, but they generally cannot predict the exact moment when one particular nucleus will decay.

One atom might decay almost immediately. Another identical atom might remain unchanged for many half-lives.

With enormous numbers of atoms, however, the random behavior averages out into an extremely predictable pattern.

This is similar to repeated coin tosses. You cannot know whether one particular toss will produce heads, but with millions of tosses you can make very reliable predictions about the overall proportions.

Radioactive half-life is therefore a statistical property rather than a countdown timer inside each atom.

Does an Atom Know How Old It Is?

No.

A radioactive nucleus does not become progressively more likely to decay simply because it has survived for a long time.

For ordinary radioactive decay, a nucleus that has existed through several half-lives has essentially the same probability of decaying during the next interval as an identical nucleus that formed recently.

This property produces the characteristic exponential decay pattern of radioactive materials.

The population steadily decreases, but the same fraction of the remaining radioactive nuclei is expected to decay during each equal interval of time.

What Is Exponential Decay?

Radioactive decay follows an exponential pattern.

After one half-life, one-half remains.

After two half-lives, one-quarter remains.

After three half-lives, one-eighth remains.

After four half-lives, one-sixteenth remains.

After ten half-lives, only about one-thousandth of the original radioactive atoms remain.

The quantity approaches zero progressively but does not suddenly become zero after a particular number of half-lives.

For real samples containing a finite number of atoms, eventually individual atoms can of course all decay, but the exponential model describes the statistical behavior of large populations.

Does Radioactive Material Disappear When It Decays?

Usually, the atom does not simply vanish.

Instead, its nucleus changes into another nucleus while releasing energy and radiation.

The new nucleus is called a decay product or daughter nuclide.

In some cases the daughter is stable. In other cases it is itself radioactive and undergoes additional decay.

A sequence of radioactive transformations is called a decay chain.

Some naturally occurring radioactive elements pass through many intermediate radionuclides before eventually reaching a stable isotope.

What Types of Radiation Can Be Released?

Radioactive decay can occur in several ways.

Alpha decay releases an alpha particle consisting of two protons and two neutrons. The original nucleus therefore changes into a different element.

Beta decay involves transformations between neutrons and protons and can release an electron or positron along with other particles.

Gamma radiation consists of high-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted when a nucleus moves from a higher-energy state to a lower-energy state.

Other nuclear decay processes also exist.

Different radioactive isotopes undergo different types of decay and can release radiation with very different energies and abilities to penetrate matter.

Does Every Radioactive Material Have the Same Half-Life?

No.

Half-lives vary enormously among radioactive isotopes.

Some unstable nuclei have half-lives much shorter than a second. Others have half-lives measured in minutes, days, years, millions of years or billions of years.

For example, carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years, while uranium-238 has a half-life of about 4.5 billion years.

Other medically useful radionuclides may have half-lives measured in hours or days.

Half-life is a characteristic property of a particular radionuclide and nuclear state.

Does a Long Half-Life Mean Something Is More Dangerous?

Not necessarily.

Half-life alone does not determine how dangerous a radioactive material is.

A short-lived isotope generally undergoes more decays per unit time for the same number of radioactive atoms, meaning it can have high activity. A very long-lived isotope decays more slowly.

But radiation risk also depends on the type and energy of radiation, the amount of material, how a person is exposed, how long exposure lasts and whether radioactive material enters the body.

The chemical behavior of the element matters as well because it affects where the material may travel or accumulate inside the body.

Two radioactive materials with similar half-lives can therefore present very different hazards.

What Is Radioactive Activity?

Activity describes how many radioactive decays occur in a sample during a particular amount of time.

The SI unit of radioactive activity is the becquerel, abbreviated Bq.

One becquerel means one nuclear decay per second.

Another older unit, the curie, is also used in some contexts.

As radioactive atoms decay and fewer unstable nuclei remain, the activity of the sample decreases according to the same half-life relationship.

After one half-life, the activity is approximately half its original value, assuming the activity is dominated by that radionuclide and complications from radioactive daughter products are not being considered.

Is Half-Life the Same as How Long Radiation Lasts?

Not exactly.

Half-life tells us how quickly the number of radioactive nuclei decreases.

After one half-life, significant radioactivity remains. After two half-lives, one-quarter remains. Even after several half-lives, measurable activity can still be present.

There is therefore no universal point at which radioactive material suddenly becomes nonradioactive.

For practical purposes, a source may eventually become weak enough that its remaining activity is insignificant for a particular application or exposure scenario.

How many half-lives are considered sufficient depends on the isotope, initial quantity and context.

Why Do People Sometimes Say a Material Is Gone After Ten Half-Lives?

After ten half-lives, the remaining fraction is about 1/1,024 of the original amount, or just under 0.1 percent.

That is a large reduction, so ten half-lives is sometimes used as a convenient rule of thumb when discussing how dramatically radioactivity decreases.

But the material has not mathematically or physically become zero at that point.

Whether the remaining activity matters depends on how much material was present initially and what level of radiation is relevant to the situation.

A tiny fraction of an enormous initial quantity can still represent substantial activity.

Can Temperature Change a Radioactive Half-Life?

For most ordinary nuclear radioactive decay, changes in temperature, pressure and chemical state have little or no meaningful effect on half-life.

Heating a radioactive substance, freezing it or changing it from a solid to a liquid generally does not stop nuclear decay.

This is because radioactive decay involves processes within the atomic nucleus, while ordinary chemical and temperature changes primarily involve electrons and interactions among atoms.

There are specialized nuclear decay processes for which the surrounding electronic environment can have small measurable effects, but these are exceptions to the useful general rule.

The relative independence of radioactive half-life from ordinary environmental conditions is one reason radioactive isotopes can serve as reliable clocks for scientific dating.

Can Radiation Be Turned Off?

Radioactive decay itself generally cannot be switched off like an electrical device.

A radioactive nucleus continues to have its characteristic probability of decay regardless of whether people are using the material.

Radiation exposure can instead be controlled through methods such as time, distance, shielding and containment.

A radioactive source can be placed behind shielding or moved farther away, but the unstable nuclei continue decaying inside the source.

Nuclear reactors are different in an important way. A reactor's controlled nuclear fission chain reaction can be reduced or stopped, but radioactive fission products created during reactor operation continue undergoing radioactive decay afterward.

This continuing decay produces radiation and heat even after the reactor's chain reaction has stopped.

What Is Decay Heat?

Radioactive decay releases energy.

When large quantities of radioactive material are present, the energy released by many decays can appear as measurable heat called decay heat.

This is particularly important in nuclear reactors.

After a reactor is shut down and the fission chain reaction stops, radioactive fission products continue to decay. Their radiation deposits energy in surrounding material, producing heat.

The amount of decay heat decreases with time but initially can be substantial enough that reactor fuel requires continued cooling.

This is one reason shutting down a nuclear reactor does not immediately eliminate all cooling requirements.

How Is Half-Life Used in Medicine?

Radioactive isotopes are widely used in medical imaging and treatment.

For diagnostic imaging, doctors can introduce a small quantity of a radioactive tracer into the body. Detectors measure radiation emitted by the tracer to show how particular organs or biological processes are functioning.

Medical radionuclides are often selected partly for their half-lives. The isotope must remain radioactive long enough to prepare, administer and perform the examination but ideally should not remain active unnecessarily long afterward.

Radioactive materials are also used in cancer treatment, where radiation can damage the DNA of targeted cells.

The isotope's half-life, radiation type and biological behavior all influence its medical usefulness.

What Is Biological Half-Life?

When radioactive material enters the body, another concept becomes important: biological half-life.

Biological half-life describes how long it takes the body to eliminate half of a particular substance through biological processes such as metabolism and excretion.

This is different from radioactive, or physical, half-life.

An atom can leave the body before it decays, or it can decay while still inside the body.

When both physical radioactive decay and biological elimination are occurring, scientists can describe an effective half-life that reflects the combined reduction of radioactive material in the body.

These distinctions are important in nuclear medicine and radiation protection.

How Does Carbon Dating Use Half-Life?

One of the best-known applications of radioactive half-life is radiocarbon dating.

Carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope of carbon produced naturally in Earth's atmosphere. Living organisms continually exchange carbon with their environment, so while alive they contain carbon-14 along with stable carbon isotopes.

When an organism dies, this exchange stops. The carbon-14 already present continues to decay with a half-life of about 5,730 years.

By measuring the amount of carbon-14 remaining and accounting for relevant calibration factors, scientists can estimate when once-living material stopped exchanging carbon with the environment.

Radiocarbon dating is particularly useful for archaeological and other relatively recent geological materials, but it is not suitable for dating extremely old objects such as Earth itself.

How Do Scientists Date Very Old Rocks?

For materials millions or billions of years old, scientists use radioactive isotopes with much longer half-lives.

One important example is uranium-lead dating.

Certain uranium isotopes decay through chains of intermediate radionuclides and eventually form stable isotopes of lead.

By measuring parent and daughter isotopes in appropriate minerals and applying known decay rates, scientists can determine when the mineral formed or when its isotopic system became closed to later exchange.

Different radiometric dating systems use different isotopes and are appropriate for different materials and timescales.

These methods provide much of the evidence used to determine the ages of ancient rocks, meteorites and Earth itself.

How Do Scientists Know an Isotope's Half-Life?

Half-lives are determined experimentally.

For relatively short-lived isotopes, scientists can measure how the activity of a sample decreases over time.

For very long-lived isotopes, waiting through an entire half-life is unnecessary. Researchers can measure the number of radioactive atoms present and the rate at which decays occur, then calculate the decay constant and half-life statistically.

Repeated measurements using different instruments and techniques allow scientists to determine many half-lives with high precision.

The predictable relationship between activity and the number of radioactive atoms is fundamental to nuclear physics.

Can You Predict When One Atom Will Decay?

No, not in the ordinary quantum-mechanical description of radioactive decay.

Scientists can calculate the probability that a particular type of unstable nucleus will decay during an interval, but not the exact decay time of one individual nucleus.

If an isotope has a half-life of one hour, that does not mean every atom survives exactly one hour and then decays.

Some atoms will decay seconds after the observation begins. Some will remain after several hours.

After one hour, the prediction is about the population: approximately half of a sufficiently large collection will remain.

This distinction between individual randomness and population-level predictability is one of the central ideas behind radioactive half-life.

Does Half-Life Mean Half the Mass Disappears?

Not literally.

When half of the radioactive parent nuclei have decayed, those atoms have generally been transformed into other nuclei rather than simply disappearing from existence.

The physical sample can therefore still contain nearly all of its original matter, but much of that matter may now consist of decay products rather than the original radioactive isotope.

Small amounts of mass are converted into energy during nuclear processes according to the relationship between mass and energy, but this is very different from saying that half of the visible material vanishes every half-life.

Half-life refers to the number of undecayed radioactive nuclei of a particular radionuclide.

What Is the Difference Between Radioactive Decay and Nuclear Fission?

Radioactive decay and nuclear fission are related nuclear processes but are not the same thing.

Radioactive decay is a spontaneous transformation of an unstable nucleus according to its characteristic decay probability.

Nuclear fission occurs when a heavy nucleus splits into smaller nuclei, releasing energy and usually neutrons. Some nuclei can undergo spontaneous fission, but controlled nuclear reactors primarily use neutron-induced fission.

The neutrons released by one fission can trigger additional fissions, producing a chain reaction.

Radioactive decay does not ordinarily require a chain reaction and continues according to the properties of the unstable nuclei themselves.

Why Is Half-Life Important for Nuclear Waste?

Radioactive waste can contain many different radionuclides, each with its own half-life and radiation characteristics.

Short-lived isotopes may initially produce high levels of radiation but decay substantially over relatively short periods.

Long-lived isotopes can remain radioactive for very long periods, although their individual decay rates may be lower.

Managing nuclear waste therefore requires considering much more than one half-life. Engineers and scientists evaluate the mixture of radionuclides, their activities, radiation types, heat production, chemical behavior and how those properties change over time.

This is why different categories of radioactive waste require different storage, shielding and disposal strategies.

The Bottom Line

Radioactive half-life describes the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a large sample to decay.

The decay of an individual nucleus is random, but a large population follows a highly predictable exponential pattern. After one half-life, about one-half remains. After two, one-quarter remains. After three, one-eighth remains, and the same proportional reduction continues.

Half-life does not mean radioactive material suddenly becomes safe or disappears after a fixed number of intervals. The original radioactive atoms transform into other nuclei, and those decay products may themselves be stable or radioactive.

Half-lives range from tiny fractions of a second to billions of years. That enormous range makes radioactive isotopes useful for very different purposes, from medical imaging lasting hours to geological dating spanning the history of Earth.

Most importantly, half-life illustrates an unusual feature of nature: individual radioactive decays are unpredictable, while the behavior of enormous numbers of radioactive atoms can be predicted with remarkable precision.
