How Do Economic Sanctions Work?

July 28, 20264 min read

How Do Economic Sanctions Work?

Economic sanctions are restrictions imposed by one or more governments to influence the behavior of another country, organization, business, or individual. Rather than using military force, sanctions seek to apply economic and financial pressure to encourage changes in policy or conduct.

Sanctions are commonly used in response to issues such as armed conflicts, terrorism, nuclear weapons programs, cyberattacks, human rights concerns, election interference, or violations of international law.

Who Can Impose Sanctions?

Economic sanctions may be imposed by:

  • Individual countries, such as the United States.
  • Groups of countries, including the European Union.
  • The United Nations Security Council.
  • Other international organizations acting within their legal authority.

Different countries may choose to participate in sanctions or maintain normal economic relations.

Who Administers U.S. Sanctions?

In the United States, most economic sanctions are administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), a division of the U.S. Department of the Treasury.

Other federal agencies, including the Department of Commerce and the Department of State, may also enforce export controls, licensing requirements, and other trade restrictions.

What Types of Sanctions Are There?

Governments can use many different types of sanctions, including:

  • Freezing assets owned by individuals or organizations.
  • Restricting access to banks and financial systems.
  • Prohibiting certain exports or imports.
  • Limiting access to advanced technology.
  • Restricting investments.
  • Blocking specific businesses from conducting transactions.
  • Restricting shipping, aviation, or insurance services.
  • Travel bans on government officials or other designated individuals.

Sanctions are often combined to increase economic pressure.

What Are Primary and Secondary Sanctions?

Governments may enforce sanctions in different ways.

Primary sanctions apply directly to people, companies, banks, and organizations that fall under the jurisdiction of the country imposing the sanctions. For example, U.S. citizens, U.S. businesses, and many transactions involving the U.S. financial system must comply with U.S. sanctions laws.

Secondary sanctions are designed to discourage foreign companies and financial institutions from doing business with sanctioned individuals or organizations. Rather than directly prohibiting those foreign entities from acting, secondary sanctions may restrict their access to the U.S. financial system or other U.S. markets if they continue certain prohibited dealings.

Because many international transactions rely on the U.S. dollar and the global banking system, secondary sanctions can significantly influence the behavior of businesses outside the United States.

Who Can Be Sanctioned?

Sanctions are not always directed at an entire country. They may target:

  • National governments.
  • Government agencies.
  • Military organizations.
  • Individual political leaders.
  • Companies.
  • Banks.
  • Ships and aircraft.
  • Terrorist organizations.
  • Criminal networks.

Targeted sanctions are intended to reduce the impact on ordinary civilians while increasing pressure on decision-makers.

How Do Sanctions Affect Trade?

Sanctions can limit or prohibit the sale, purchase, financing, transportation, or shipment of certain goods and services.

For example, sanctions may prevent companies from selling advanced technology, military equipment, or sensitive industrial products to a sanctioned country. Financial sanctions may also make it difficult for businesses to receive payment or conduct international transactions.

Companies that violate sanctions may face substantial civil or criminal penalties.

Are Sanctions the Same as Tariffs?

No.

Although both affect international trade, they serve different purposes.

Sanctions are generally foreign policy or national security tools intended to influence behavior.

Tariffs are taxes imposed on imported goods. Governments often use tariffs to generate revenue, protect domestic industries, or respond to trade disputes.

A country may impose tariffs without sanctions, sanctions without tariffs, or both at the same time.

Do Economic Sanctions Always Work?

The effectiveness of sanctions varies.

Some sanctions have encouraged negotiations or policy changes, while others have had limited impact or remained in place for many years. Their effectiveness often depends on factors such as international cooperation, the target country's economy, the scope of the restrictions, and the availability of alternative trading partners.

Why Are Economic Sanctions Frequently in the News?

Economic sanctions are commonly mentioned in news involving:

  • Russia.
  • Iran.
  • North Korea.
  • China.
  • Venezuela.
  • International conflicts.
  • Nuclear programs.
  • Cybersecurity incidents.
  • Human rights issues.
  • Terrorism financing.

Sanctions may also affect global energy markets, supply chains, financial markets, and international trade.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that sanctions completely stop all trade with a country. In reality, many sanctions target only specific industries, products, companies, financial institutions, or individuals, while other forms of trade may continue.

Another misunderstanding is that sanctions are the same as tariffs or embargoes. While these measures can all affect trade, they are different legal and economic tools with different purposes.

Finally, sanctions do not automatically require military action. They are generally considered non-military measures used to apply economic and diplomatic pressure.

Conclusion

Economic sanctions are restrictions used by governments and international organizations to influence the actions of countries, businesses, organizations, or individuals without using military force. By limiting trade, financial transactions, technology transfers, or access to global markets, sanctions seek to encourage policy changes while remaining one of the most commonly used tools of international diplomacy and national security.
