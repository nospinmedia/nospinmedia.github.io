What Is Cyclospora?

August 8, 20263 min read

What Is Cyclospora?

Cyclospora is a microscopic parasite that can cause an intestinal illness known as cyclosporiasis. People become infected by swallowing food or water contaminated with the parasite, most commonly fresh produce that has not been cooked.

Unlike many bacteria that cause food poisoning, Cyclospora is a parasite and requires time outside the human body before it becomes capable of infecting another person. As a result, the illness is generally spread through contaminated food or water rather than directly from one person to another.

What Causes Cyclospora Infections?

Cyclospora infections are caused by the parasite Cyclospora cayetanensis.

The parasite is primarily found in human waste. If food or water becomes contaminated and proper sanitation is not maintained, people can become infected after consuming the contaminated product.

How Do People Get Infected?

People most often become infected by consuming:

  • Fresh fruits.
  • Leafy greens.
  • Herbs such as cilantro or basil.
  • Fresh vegetables.
  • Contaminated drinking water.

Because cooking kills the parasite, outbreaks are most often associated with fresh produce that is eaten raw.

What Are the Symptoms?

Symptoms usually begin about 2 to 14 days after infection.

Common symptoms include:

  • Watery diarrhea.
  • Loss of appetite.
  • Weight loss.
  • Stomach cramps.
  • Bloating.
  • Nausea.
  • Fatigue.
  • Low-grade fever.

Without treatment, symptoms may last for several weeks and sometimes improve before returning.

How Is Cyclospora Diagnosed?

Doctors diagnose Cyclospora by testing stool samples in a laboratory.

Because routine stool tests may not always detect the parasite, healthcare providers often need to specifically request testing for Cyclospora.

How Is It Treated?

Cyclospora infections are commonly treated with the prescription antibiotic combination trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX).

People who cannot take this medication should discuss alternative treatment options with their healthcare provider. Drinking plenty of fluids is also important because diarrhea can lead to dehydration.

Who Is Most at Risk?

Anyone can become infected, but complications are more likely in:

  • Young children.
  • Older adults.
  • People with weakened immune systems.
  • Individuals who become severely dehydrated.

Most healthy people recover completely with appropriate treatment.

How Can Cyclospora Be Prevented?

Although washing fresh produce is recommended, it may not completely remove Cyclospora because the parasite can be difficult to eliminate from fruits and vegetables.

Cyclospora is also resistant to the levels of chlorine and other sanitizers commonly used to wash produce and treat commercial rinse water. As a result, washing fruits and vegetables may reduce dirt and some contaminants but may not completely eliminate the parasite.

Additional prevention measures include:

  • Washing hands before preparing food.
  • Using safe drinking water.
  • Following food safety guidance during recalls.
  • Avoiding recalled products.

Public health officials investigate outbreaks to identify contaminated foods and remove them from the marketplace. During an active outbreak, following FDA and CDC recall notices is often the most effective way to avoid contaminated products.

Why Is Cyclospora Frequently in the News?

Cyclospora is commonly mentioned in news involving:

  • Food recalls.
  • Fresh produce recalls.
  • Multistate outbreak investigations.
  • FDA food safety alerts.
  • CDC outbreak updates.
  • Imported fruits and vegetables.
  • Restaurant-related outbreak investigations.

Large Cyclospora outbreaks can lead to recalls affecting multiple states and widespread public health investigations as officials work to identify the contaminated food source and remove affected products from the marketplace.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that Cyclospora spreads easily from person to person. In reality, the parasite must mature in the environment before it becomes infectious, making direct transmission uncommon.

Another misunderstanding is that washing produce completely eliminates the risk. While washing is an important food safety practice, it may not remove every Cyclospora parasite.

Finally, Cyclospora is not a bacterium or a virus. It is a parasite that requires different laboratory testing and treatment than many other causes of foodborne illness.

Conclusion

Cyclospora is a microscopic parasite that causes an intestinal illness called cyclosporiasis. Most infections occur after consuming contaminated fresh produce or water, and outbreaks are often linked to multistate food recalls. Understanding how Cyclospora spreads, recognizing its symptoms, and following public health guidance can help reduce the risk of infection.
