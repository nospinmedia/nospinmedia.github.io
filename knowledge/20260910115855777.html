What Causes an Earthquake?

September 15, 20268 min read

An earthquake occurs when stored energy in Earth's crust is suddenly released, causing the ground to shake. Most earthquakes happen when stresses slowly build in rock until part of the crust abruptly slips along a fracture called a fault.

Earth's surface is constantly moving, but usually far too slowly for us to notice. Large sections of the outer Earth, called tectonic plates, move only centimeters per year in many places. Where those plates interact, however, enormous stresses can accumulate over decades, centuries or even longer.

Eventually, the rock may no longer be able to withstand the stress. It breaks or slips, releasing energy that travels through Earth as seismic waves. Those waves are what we experience as an earthquake.

Earth's Crust Is Constantly Moving

Earth's rigid outer layer is divided into large and small pieces called tectonic plates. These plates move slowly relative to one another over the softer, hotter material beneath them.

At plate boundaries, the plates can move toward each other, pull apart or slide past one another.

The movement is not always smooth. Friction can cause sections of rock to remain locked together even while the plates continue trying to move.

Stress then builds in the surrounding rock.

Eventually, the stress can become greater than the friction and strength holding the rocks in place. The rocks suddenly slip, sometimes by only a small amount and sometimes by several meters during a major earthquake.

That sudden movement releases the accumulated energy.

What Is a Fault?

A fault is a fracture or zone of fractures in Earth's crust where blocks of rock have moved relative to one another.

Faults can range from small cracks to enormous geological structures extending hundreds or thousands of kilometers.

Not every fault is constantly producing earthquakes. Some remain quiet for very long periods while stress gradually accumulates.

When a locked portion of a fault finally slips, an earthquake occurs.

The point underground where the rupture begins is called the hypocenter, or focus. The location on Earth's surface directly above it is called the epicenter.

An earthquake is not necessarily strongest at the epicenter in every circumstance. The amount of shaking at a particular location also depends on the direction of the rupture, distance from the fault, local geology and other factors.

What Actually Makes the Ground Shake?

When a fault suddenly moves, stored elastic energy is released into the surrounding rock.

That energy travels outward as seismic waves.

Different types of seismic waves move through Earth in different ways. Some travel through Earth's interior, while others move along the surface.

Surface waves can produce particularly strong rolling and side-to-side motions and are often responsible for substantial damage during large earthquakes.

The fault movement itself may last only seconds or minutes, but seismic waves can travel great distances from where the earthquake began.

Are All Earthquakes Caused by Tectonic Plates?

No, although tectonic activity causes most significant earthquakes.

Earthquakes can also occur because of volcanic activity, landslides, collapsing underground cavities and certain human activities.

These events all involve a sudden movement or change in stress capable of producing seismic waves, although their mechanisms and typical sizes differ.

Earthquakes associated with volcanoes, for example, can occur as magma moves underground and changes the stress within surrounding rock.

Can Humans Cause Earthquakes?

Yes. Earthquakes caused or triggered by human activity are known as induced earthquakes or induced seismicity.

Humans generally do not create entirely new major faults. Instead, an activity can change the stresses or fluid pressures underground enough to cause an existing fault that was already under stress to slip.

Activities associated with induced seismicity include:

  • Underground injection of fluids
  • Oil and gas extraction
  • Hydraulic fracturing
  • Geothermal energy operations
  • Mining
  • Filling very large reservoirs behind dams
  • Withdrawal or injection of fluids underground

The size and frequency of induced earthquakes vary greatly depending on the geology and the activity involved.

Can Fracking Cause Earthquakes?

Hydraulic fracturing, commonly called fracking, works by injecting fluid at high pressure into underground rock formations to create or enlarge fractures and allow oil or natural gas to flow more easily.

Creating those fractures produces very small seismic events as part of the process. Most are too small to be felt at the surface.

In some circumstances, however, hydraulic fracturing can interact with an existing fault and trigger an earthquake large enough to be felt.

There is also an important distinction between hydraulic fracturing itself and another activity commonly associated with oil and gas production: wastewater disposal.

Oil and gas wells can produce large quantities of salty underground water along with hydrocarbons. That wastewater is often injected into deep disposal wells.

Injecting large volumes of fluid underground can increase pressure within rock formations. If that pressure reaches a fault that is already close to slipping, it can reduce the effective friction holding the fault in place and trigger an earthquake.

Some regions that experienced major increases in earthquake activity during periods of intensive oil and gas development have linked much of that increase to wastewater disposal rather than directly to the hydraulic-fracturing process.

So the statement that 'fracking causes earthquakes' can be misleading without explaining the difference. Hydraulic fracturing can induce earthquakes, but wastewater injection associated with oil and gas production has been responsible for many of the more significant induced-earthquake sequences observed in the United States.

Can Injected Water Really Move a Fault?

It can, but the water does not normally act like a powerful stream physically pushing the fault apart.

Rock deep underground contains tiny pores and fractures that can hold fluids. Increasing the pressure of those fluids can change the forces acting across a fault.

A fault may already be under enormous natural tectonic stress but remain locked because friction prevents it from moving.

Higher fluid pressure can reduce the effective force pressing the two sides of the fault together. If the fault is already close to failure, that relatively small change can be enough to allow it to slip.

This is why the geological conditions surrounding an injection site matter greatly. The same type of operation may produce little measurable seismic activity in one location and significantly more in another.

Why Do Earthquakes Happen Far From Plate Boundaries?

Most earthquakes occur near tectonic plate boundaries, but not all of them do.

Ancient faults exist throughout the interiors of continents. Even far from an active plate boundary, stresses transmitted through a tectonic plate can occasionally reactivate one of these old faults.

These are known as intraplate earthquakes.

Because some regions experience them infrequently, people may incorrectly assume that an area without an obvious plate boundary cannot have earthquakes. In reality, earthquakes can occur almost anywhere if geological stresses cause a fault to slip.

What Are Aftershocks?

A large earthquake changes the distribution of stress in the surrounding crust.

Nearby faults and other portions of the same fault then adjust to those changes, producing additional earthquakes known as aftershocks.

Aftershocks are usually smaller than the main earthquake, but they can still be damaging, particularly to structures already weakened by the initial shaking.

They can continue for days, months or even years after a major earthquake, generally becoming less frequent over time.

Sometimes an earthquake initially considered the main event is followed by an even larger earthquake. In that case, the earlier earthquake is reclassified as a foreshock and the larger event becomes the mainshock.

How Is Earthquake Size Measured?

Scientists measure earthquakes using instruments called seismometers, which detect and record ground motion.

Modern earthquake reports commonly describe earthquake size using moment magnitude, which estimates the amount of energy associated with the fault rupture. It has largely replaced the original Richter scale for measuring moderate and large earthquakes.

The magnitude scale is logarithmic. An increase of one whole magnitude represents about 10 times greater measured ground-motion amplitude and roughly 32 times more energy release.

Magnitude is different from intensity, which describes how strongly an earthquake is experienced at a particular location. The same earthquake can produce severe shaking near the fault and much weaker shaking hundreds of kilometers away.

Why Are Some Earthquakes More Destructive Than Others?

Magnitude is important, but it is not the only factor determining damage.

The depth of the earthquake, distance from populated areas, local soil and rock conditions, construction standards and duration of shaking all matter.

Soft sediments can amplify seismic waves in some locations. Poorly constructed buildings may fail during shaking that better-designed structures can withstand.

Under certain conditions, water-saturated loose soil can temporarily lose strength during intense shaking, a phenomenon called liquefaction.

Undersea earthquakes that suddenly move the seafloor can also displace enormous amounts of water and generate tsunamis.

Can Scientists Predict Earthquakes?

Scientists can identify faults, measure their movement, study historical earthquake patterns and estimate the probability of earthquakes occurring in a region over long periods.

They cannot currently predict the exact time, location and magnitude of a future earthquake with enough reliability to provide a precise advance warning days or weeks beforehand.

Earthquake early-warning systems are different from earthquake prediction. These systems detect an earthquake after it has already begun and rapidly send alerts to locations that have not yet experienced the strongest shaking. Because electronic signals travel much faster than seismic waves, people farther from the earthquake may receive several seconds or, in some circumstances, more warning before strong shaking arrives.

The Bottom Line

Most earthquakes occur because Earth's tectonic plates are constantly moving. Stress builds where rocks and faults resist that movement until the rock suddenly slips, releasing energy as seismic waves that shake the ground.

But tectonic movement is not the only cause. Volcanic processes and certain human activities can also produce earthquakes. Hydraulic fracturing can trigger earthquakes under some conditions, while deep wastewater injection associated with oil and gas production has caused significant induced seismicity by changing underground fluid pressures near existing faults.

Whether natural or human-induced, the basic event is similar: stress on rock reaches a point where a fault or fracture suddenly moves, releasing stored energy into the surrounding Earth.
