How Does a QR Code Work?

September 26, 202611 min read

A QR code works by storing digital information in a two-dimensional pattern of dark and light squares. A camera captures that pattern, and software analyzes its structure, converts the encoded pattern back into data, and then determines what to do with that information.

QR stands for Quick Response. Unlike a traditional one-dimensional barcode, which stores information mainly along a single row of bars, a QR code stores information both horizontally and vertically. This allows a relatively small symbol to contain much more data.

A QR code can contain a website address, plain text, contact information, Wi-Fi configuration information and several other types of data. The code itself does not need an internet connection to be decoded. Internet access is only necessary if the information it contains points to something online.

What Are the Black and White Squares?

A QR code is made from a grid of small square units called modules.

Each module is normally displayed as either dark or light. Together, these modules represent encoded information as well as structural information that helps a scanner understand the code.

Not every square is part of the user's actual message. Some areas have specific jobs, such as helping the scanner locate the QR code, determine its orientation, understand its size and correct errors.

The familiar pattern is therefore a carefully organized data structure rather than a random collection of squares.

How Does a Phone Find a QR Code?

One of the most recognizable features of a QR code is the set of three large square patterns near three of its corners.

These are called finder patterns or position detection patterns.

They allow scanning software to quickly recognize that it is looking at a QR code and determine how the code is oriented.

Because three corners have finder patterns while the fourth does not, the scanner can determine which way the symbol is rotated. A QR code can therefore normally be read whether it is upright, sideways or upside down.

The scanner also analyzes other structural patterns to determine the geometry of the grid and compensate for some distortion caused by viewing the code from an angle.

What Are the Other Patterns For?

QR codes contain several types of structural information in addition to the three large finder patterns.

Timing patterns consist of alternating dark and light modules. They help the decoder determine the spacing and coordinates of the grid.

Larger QR codes also contain alignment patterns, which help compensate for distortion. This is particularly useful when a code is printed on a curved surface or photographed at an angle.

A clear border around the outside of the QR code, known as the quiet zone, separates the symbol from surrounding text, images and other visual information. This makes it easier for scanning software to identify the boundaries of the code.

QR codes also contain format information and, in larger versions, version information that tells the decoder important details about how the symbol is constructed.

How Is Information Turned Into a QR Code?

Creating a QR code begins with data, such as a website address.

Software converts that information into a standardized digital representation. Depending on the type of data, the QR system can use different encoding modes designed for numbers, certain combinations of letters and numbers, bytes or other supported character sets.

The encoded information is then combined with additional data used for error correction.

These bits are placed into the QR code's grid according to rules defined by the QR Code standard. Structural patterns such as the finder, timing and alignment patterns occupy predetermined areas, while the encoded data and error-correction information fill the remaining available locations.

The result is the square pattern recognized as a QR code.

How Does the Scanner Decode It?

When you point a phone camera at a QR code, the phone first captures an image.

Software identifies the finder patterns and determines the location, size, rotation and perspective of the code. It then maps the image onto the expected grid of modules.

The decoder determines which modules represent dark and light values, reads the format and structural information, and extracts the encoded data.

It also uses the code's error-correction information to detect and, within limits, reconstruct missing or incorrectly read data.

Finally, the recovered bits are converted back into the original information.

If that information is a website address, the phone can offer to open the site in a browser. If it is plain text, the phone can display it. If it contains compatible Wi-Fi configuration information, the phone may offer to join the network.

How Much Information Can a QR Code Hold?

The amount depends on the size and configuration of the QR code and the type of information being encoded.

Standard QR Codes are available in multiple versions. Higher versions contain larger grids with more modules and therefore have room for more information.

The smallest standard QR Code, Version 1, contains a 21 by 21 module grid. Each increase in version adds four modules to each side. The largest standard version, Version 40, contains a 177 by 177 module grid.

Maximum capacity also depends on the encoding mode and error-correction level. Under suitable conditions, a standard QR Code can store thousands of numeric digits or several thousand bytes of data.

In everyday use, however, QR codes frequently contain something much shorter, such as a web address.

Why Can a Damaged QR Code Still Work?

One of the most useful features of QR codes is error correction.

When a QR code is created, extra mathematical information is added using a form of error-correcting code known as Reed-Solomon error correction.

This redundant information allows a scanner to reconstruct some data even if parts of the QR code are dirty, scratched, obscured or incorrectly read by the camera.

QR codes support multiple error-correction levels. Higher levels provide greater ability to recover from damage but leave less room for the original data because more of the symbol must be devoted to error-correction information.

This tradeoff allows QR codes to be designed for different uses.

How Can QR Codes Have Logos in the Middle and Still Work?

Some QR codes intentionally place a company logo, picture or other graphic over part of the symbol.

This can work because error correction allows a certain amount of missing information to be reconstructed.

However, error correction is not unlimited. Covering too much of the code, interfering with important structural patterns or printing the code with poor contrast can make it unreadable.

QR codes containing logos are therefore usually generated with enough error-correction capacity and tested to make sure scanners can still decode them reliably.

The logo is not normally being interpreted as part of the data. The scanner is recovering the encoded information despite part of the symbol being obscured.

Why Do QR Codes Look Different Even When They Lead to the Same Website?

There can be more than one valid QR code representing the same information.

One reason is a process called masking. Certain arrangements of dark and light modules can make QR codes difficult for scanners to interpret reliably. During generation, the QR system can apply different predefined mask patterns to the data area and choose a pattern that produces a more readable overall symbol.

Different QR generators may also make different choices about encoding modes, error-correction levels, versions or the exact text being stored.

For example, two web addresses that ultimately lead to the same page might differ slightly in their written form and therefore produce different QR patterns.

The visual appearance alone does not tell a person what information the code contains.

Does a QR Code Contain the Website Itself?

Usually not.

When a QR code opens a website, the code normally stores the web address, or URL, rather than the contents of the webpage.

The phone decodes the URL and then uses an internet connection to request that page from a web server.

This distinction explains why a QR code can be scanned without internet access but the linked webpage may fail to load until a connection becomes available.

The QR code contains the address telling the phone where to go, not a complete copy of the website.

What Is a Dynamic QR Code?

The QR Code standard itself does not contain a special mechanism that magically changes information after printing.

What companies commonly call a dynamic QR code usually contains a web address controlled by a redirect service. When someone scans the code, the phone visits that address, and the server redirects the user to another destination.

The owner can later change the server's redirect without changing the printed QR code.

A static QR code, by comparison, directly contains information that does not change unless a new QR code is created and printed.

This means the apparent ability of a dynamic QR code to change comes from the online service behind the URL, not from the printed squares changing their encoded data.

Does Scanning a QR Code Automatically Open a Website?

That depends on the device and software.

Modern smartphones commonly recognize QR codes through their camera applications and display a notification or preview showing the detected action. The user can then choose whether to proceed.

Some specialized scanners or applications may be configured to perform actions automatically.

The QR code itself does not control the phone. It contains data. The scanning software decides how that data should be presented or acted upon.

Can QR Codes Be Dangerous?

The QR technology itself is simply a method for encoding information, but the information inside a code can direct someone toward a malicious destination.

A QR code can contain a link to a fraudulent website just as an email or text message can contain a malicious link. Attackers can also place a fraudulent QR code sticker over a legitimate code in a public location.

Because people cannot easily determine a QR code's contents by looking at its pattern, it is important to check the destination shown by the phone before opening an unfamiliar link.

A QR code should therefore be treated much like any other link: its safety depends on where it leads and what the user does after opening it.

Do QR Codes Track You?

A printed QR code does not inherently track the person scanning it.

A static QR code can simply contain information such as text or a URL, and decoding that information can happen locally on the phone.

However, if the code directs the phone to a website, that website can receive information associated with a normal web visit. Dynamic QR services can also record scans because users contact the service's server before being redirected to the final destination.

Tracking therefore comes from the website or online service connected to the code, not from the black and white printed pattern itself.

Why Were QR Codes Invented?

QR codes were developed in Japan in the 1990s by Denso Wave for tracking components in automotive manufacturing.

Traditional barcodes could store relatively limited information and often needed to be scanned from a particular orientation. QR codes were designed to store more data and be recognized quickly from different directions.

Their usefulness eventually expanded far beyond factories. Smartphones with high-quality cameras made QR codes convenient for websites, tickets, payments, menus, product information, authentication and many other applications.

QR Codes Versus Traditional Barcodes

A traditional one-dimensional barcode generally encodes information through the widths and spacing of parallel bars along one dimension.

A QR code uses a two-dimensional grid, allowing information to be encoded both horizontally and vertically.

That gives QR codes greater data capacity and allows them to incorporate substantial error correction. Their finder patterns also make them easy for camera-based systems to locate and orient.

Traditional barcodes remain extremely useful, especially for identifying products and inventory, but QR codes are better suited to applications requiring more information in a compact symbol.

The Bottom Line

A QR code stores digital information in an organized two-dimensional grid of dark and light modules.

Three large finder patterns help a camera locate and orient the symbol. Timing and alignment patterns help determine the grid's geometry, while other regions contain encoded data, formatting information and error-correction information.

After a camera captures the code, software reconstructs the grid, reads its binary information, corrects recoverable errors and converts the encoded bits back into useful data.

The result might be a website address, text, contact information or another supported type of information. If the code contains an online address, the phone can then connect to that destination.

What looks like a simple collection of black and white squares is therefore a carefully structured system combining digital encoding, image recognition and mathematical error correction to make information quick and reliable for machines to read.
