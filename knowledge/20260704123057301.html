What Is the Twelfth Amendment?

August 12, 20265 min read

What Is the Twelfth Amendment?

The Twelfth Amendment to the United States Constitution changed the way the President and Vice President are elected through the Electoral College. Ratified in 1804, it established the system still used today, requiring presidential electors to cast separate votes for president and vice president.

The amendment was adopted after problems with the nation's original election system became apparent during the election of 1800. By changing how electoral votes are cast and counted, the Twelfth Amendment helped prevent future constitutional crises and clarified the process for selecting the nation's two highest executive offices.

Why Was the Twelfth Amendment Needed?

When the Constitution was originally written, each presidential elector cast two votes for president. The candidate receiving the most electoral votes became president, while the candidate finishing second became vice president.

This system worked reasonably well before the rise of organized political parties. As political parties developed, however, serious problems emerged because presidential and vice-presidential candidates were now running together as a team.

The weakness of the original system became clear during the Election of 1800.

What Happened in the Election of 1800?

In the election of 1800, Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr each received the same number of electoral votes because every elector supporting their political party voted for both candidates.

Since neither candidate received more electoral votes than the other, the Constitution required the election to be decided by the U.S. House of Representatives.

After 36 ballots, the House elected Jefferson as president.

The lengthy and politically contentious process convinced many leaders that the Constitution needed to be changed to prevent similar situations in future elections.

What Did the Twelfth Amendment Change?

The Twelfth Amendment introduced several important changes.

Most significantly, it requires presidential electors to cast:

  • One vote for President.
  • One separate vote for Vice President.

This allows presidential and vice-presidential candidates to run together on the same ticket while ensuring they are elected to separate offices.

The amendment also established procedures for resolving elections in which no candidate wins a majority of electoral votes.

What Happens If No Candidate Wins 270 Electoral Votes?

If no presidential candidate receives a majority of electoral votes, the election moves to the U.S. House of Representatives.

Under the Twelfth Amendment:

  • The House chooses the president from the top three candidates who received the most electoral votes.
  • Each state delegation receives one vote, regardless of the state's population.
  • A majority of state delegations is required to elect the president.

At the same time:

  • The U.S. Senate chooses the vice president.
  • Senators vote individually.
  • The Senate selects from the top two vice-presidential candidates who received the most electoral votes.

This process is known as a contingent election.

Has the Twelfth Amendment Ever Been Used?

Yes.

The House of Representatives has selected the president under the Twelfth Amendment on two occasions:

  • 1801, before the amendment was ratified, when Thomas Jefferson was chosen under the original constitutional rules after the election of 1800.
  • 1825, after the election of 1824, when no presidential candidate won a majority of the Electoral College. The House elected John Quincy Adams as president.

The Senate has selected the vice president under the Twelfth Amendment once, in 1837, when it elected Richard Mentor Johnson after no vice-presidential candidate received a majority of electoral votes.

How Does the Twelfth Amendment Affect Modern Elections?

Although contingent elections are rare, the Twelfth Amendment governs every presidential election.

Every four years:

  • Political parties nominate separate candidates for president and vice president.
  • Voters cast ballots for a presidential ticket.
  • Presidential electors cast separate votes for president and vice president.
  • Congress counts the electoral votes.

If one presidential candidate receives at least 270 electoral votes, that candidate is elected president under the current Electoral College system.

Why Is the Twelfth Amendment Still Important?

The Twelfth Amendment remains one of the most significant constitutional provisions governing presidential elections.

It provides clear procedures for counting electoral votes, resolving elections without an Electoral College majority, and ensuring that the offices of president and vice president are filled separately.

Whenever discussions arise about Electoral College reform, contingent elections, or disputed presidential elections, the Twelfth Amendment often becomes part of the conversation.

Common Misunderstandings

Several misconceptions frequently arise about the Twelfth Amendment.

One is that it created the Electoral College. It did not. The Electoral College was established by the original Constitution. The amendment changed how electoral votes are cast and counted.

Another misconception is that the House of Representatives votes as individual members during a contingent presidential election. Under the Twelfth Amendment, each state delegation receives one vote, regardless of how many representatives the state has.

It is also sometimes assumed that the amendment applies only during disputed elections. In reality, its procedures govern every presidential election, even when the outcome is not close.

Conclusion

The Twelfth Amendment fundamentally reshaped the way the United States elects its president and vice president. By requiring separate electoral votes for each office and establishing procedures for elections without an Electoral College majority, it addressed weaknesses that became apparent during the nation's early elections.

More than two centuries after its ratification, the Twelfth Amendment continues to provide the constitutional framework that guides every presidential election and remains a key part of understanding how the American electoral system operates.
