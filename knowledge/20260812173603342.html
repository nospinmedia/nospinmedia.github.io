What Is the Defense Production Act?

August 24, 202613 min read

What Is the Defense Production Act?

The Defense Production Act of 1950, commonly called the DPA, is a federal law that gives the president authorities to influence domestic industry and supply chains when materials, products, services, or industrial capacity are important to national defense.

The law is often mentioned during wars, national emergencies, supply shortages, public health crises, and efforts to strengthen strategically important industries.

Despite its name, the Defense Production Act is not limited to producing weapons. Federal law defines national defense broadly enough to include military and energy production or construction, homeland security, stockpiling, space activities, emergency preparedness, and critical infrastructure protection and restoration.

The DPA also does not simply give the president power to take over private companies or order any business to manufacture anything. It provides specific authorities, subject to statutory requirements, for prioritizing contracts, allocating resources, expanding productive capacity, and coordinating with industry.

Why Was the Defense Production Act Created?

Congress enacted the Defense Production Act in 1950, shortly after the Korean War began.

The United States had relied on extensive economic controls and industrial mobilization during World War II. The DPA was designed to provide the federal government with authorities needed to ensure that the country's industrial base could meet national-defense requirements during the new conflict and future emergencies.

The original law was broader than the version that remains today. Congress has amended and reauthorized portions of it many times, while allowing other provisions to expire or repealing them.

The major authorities that remain are generally organized around Title I, Title III, and Title VII of the Act.

What Does "National Defense" Mean Under the DPA?

The term is much broader than military weapons and equipment.

Under federal law, national defense includes programs involving:

  • Military production and construction.
  • Energy production and construction.
  • Military or critical-infrastructure assistance to foreign nations.
  • Homeland security.
  • Stockpiling.
  • Space.
  • Directly related activities.
  • Emergency preparedness under the Stafford Act.
  • Critical infrastructure protection and restoration.

This broad definition helps explain why DPA authorities have been used for matters involving medical supplies, critical minerals, energy technologies, infrastructure, and industrial supply chains as well as traditional military equipment.

What Are the Main Parts of the Defense Production Act?

Three portions of the DPA contain most of the authorities discussed in modern news coverage: Title I, Title III, and Title VII.

Each serves a different purpose.

What Does Title I Do?

Title I covers priorities and allocations.

One of its most important authorities allows the president to require certain contracts or orders considered necessary or appropriate to promote the national defense to receive priority over other contracts or orders.

In practical terms, this can allow the federal government to place a qualifying priority-rated order with a company and require that order to be fulfilled ahead of lower-priority commercial work, subject to the law and implementing regulations.

Title I also provides authority, under statutory conditions, for allocating materials, services, and facilities when necessary or appropriate to promote the national defense.

These are powerful authorities, but they are not equivalent to the government taking ownership of a business.

What Is a Priority-Rated Order?

A priority-rated order is a contract or order that receives special priority under regulations implementing the Defense Production Act.

Companies subject to the applicable regulations generally must accept and prioritize properly rated orders, with specified exceptions.

The federal government uses systems implementing this authority, including the Defense Priorities and Allocations System, commonly called DPAS, for many defense-related orders.

Priority ratings can also flow through a supply chain. A company fulfilling a rated government order may need components or materials from its own suppliers, allowing priority requirements to be extended to qualifying orders necessary to complete the higher-priority contract.

This can help ensure that a critical government requirement is not delayed because necessary components are being produced for lower-priority customers.

Can the Government Allocate Scarce Materials?

The DPA provides allocation authority, but its use is more constrained than simply allowing the president to distribute anything at will.

Under Title I, the government can allocate materials, services, and facilities when the statutory requirements are satisfied. Allocation can involve controlling the distribution or use of resources considered necessary for national defense requirements.

Federal law and implementing regulations establish procedures and limitations for exercising these powers.

Priority authority is used much more routinely than broad allocation controls.

What Does Title III Do?

Title III focuses on expanding productive capacity and supply.

Instead of merely deciding which existing customer receives a product first, Title III addresses situations in which the United States may not have enough domestic capacity to produce something considered essential to national defense.

Depending on the statutory authority and circumstances, the federal government can use tools such as:

  • Purchases.
  • Purchase commitments.
  • Installation of equipment in certain circumstances.
  • Financial incentives authorized by law.
  • Loans or loan guarantees under applicable DPA authorities.
  • Other actions intended to expand or preserve production capability.

For example, if the government determines that domestic production of a critical material is inadequate for national-defense needs, Title III authorities may be used to help create, expand, protect, or restore the necessary industrial capacity.

This can involve long-term projects rather than an immediate response to a shortage.

Why Would the Government Guarantee Purchases?

Sometimes the problem is not that a company lacks the technical ability to produce something. The problem may be that investing millions of dollars in new capacity is too risky without confidence that someone will buy the resulting product.

A government purchase commitment can reduce that risk by creating a dependable customer for qualifying production.

That can encourage businesses to build facilities, purchase equipment, expand production lines, develop processing capabilities, or make other investments needed to increase domestic supply.

The objective is generally to address an industrial-base shortfall considered important to national defense.

What Does Title VII Do?

Title VII contains general provisions and additional authorities supporting national-defense preparedness.

Among its important provisions are mechanisms allowing the federal government to establish certain voluntary agreements with private industry to help coordinate preparedness and production activities.

Normally, cooperation among competing businesses can raise antitrust concerns. The DPA establishes a regulated process under which qualifying voluntary agreements and plans of action can receive specified legal protections when used for national-defense purposes and when statutory requirements are followed.

Title VII also contains provisions concerning industrial information, government organization, definitions, and other aspects of DPA administration.

Can the President Force a Company to Make Something?

The answer is more complicated than the phrase "force a company to manufacture something" suggests.

The DPA provides substantial authority to prioritize qualifying contracts and orders, and companies generally must comply with properly issued rated orders unless an applicable exception allows or requires rejection.

The government can also use Title III authorities to encourage or support expansion of productive capacity.

But the DPA is not a general power allowing the president to select any private business and command it to manufacture any product under any circumstances.

The particular DPA authority being used, statutory requirements, implementing regulations, available funding, the company's capabilities, and the relationship of the product or service to national defense all matter.

This distinction is important when officials say they are "invoking the Defense Production Act." That phrase alone does not explain which DPA authority is actually being exercised.

Does the DPA Allow the Government to Take Over Factories?

Not simply because the DPA has been invoked.

The modern Defense Production Act is primarily built around priorities, allocations, industrial-capacity incentives, and cooperation with industry.

A DPA priority order does not transfer ownership of a factory to the federal government. A company receiving government assistance under Title III also remains a private company unless some separate legal transaction or authority changes that ownership.

Describing routine use of the DPA as nationalizing a company is therefore generally inaccurate.

Does the DPA Allow the President to Control the Entire Economy?

No.

The original 1950 law contained broader economic authorities than the modern statute. Some earlier provisions involving areas such as price and wage stabilization were later allowed to expire or were repealed.

Today's DPA contains specific industrial and national-defense authorities rather than a general presidential power to control prices, wages, production, and commerce throughout the economy.

Does the Defense Production Act Apply Only During War?

No.

The United States does not have to be formally at war for DPA authorities to be used.

The statute's definition of national defense extends well beyond active warfare. DPA authorities can be relevant to emergency preparedness, homeland security, critical infrastructure, energy, space, stockpiling, and other national-defense requirements.

This is why presidents of both parties have used DPA authorities during peacetime as well as during military conflicts and national emergencies.

Does the DPA Apply Only to Weapons?

No.

Military equipment is an important part of the law's purpose, but the statute reaches much further.

Depending on the findings and authority involved, DPA actions can concern raw materials, minerals, components, manufacturing equipment, energy resources, medical supplies, infrastructure-related products, and other industrial resources considered important to national defense.

The statute defines materials broadly to include raw materials, minerals, metals, commodities, components, products, items of supply, and certain related technical information and services.

How Was the DPA Used During COVID-19?

The COVID-19 pandemic provided a prominent example of the DPA being used for something other than traditional weapons production.

The federal government used DPA authorities to address shortages and production requirements involving medical and public-health supplies. Priority authorities and industrial-capacity tools were applied to products and supply chains involving items such as ventilators, personal protective equipment, vaccine-related supplies, testing materials, and other medical resources.

The pandemic demonstrated why the statutory definition of national defense includes emergency preparedness rather than being limited to military combat.

Can the DPA Be Used for Critical Minerals and Energy?

Yes, when the statutory requirements are satisfied.

Critical minerals can be essential to military systems, electronics, batteries, aerospace equipment, energy systems, and other technologies important to national security.

DPA Title III authorities have therefore been used to support domestic production and processing capacity for strategically important materials and industrial resources.

Energy production and construction are also expressly included within the DPA's statutory definition of national defense.

A presidential determination or other DPA action does not mean a mine, processing facility, or factory will appear immediately. Projects involving mineral extraction, processing, manufacturing facilities, environmental review, equipment, permitting, financing, and skilled workers can take years to develop.

Who Actually Administers the Defense Production Act?

Although the statute grants major authorities to the president, presidents can and do delegate DPA responsibilities to federal departments and agencies.

Different agencies administer DPA authorities in areas related to their responsibilities. These can include departments involved in defense, homeland security, energy, health, transportation, commerce, agriculture, and other sectors.

For example, the Department of Defense is a major user of DPA authorities for the defense industrial base, while other agencies can administer priority and allocation authorities involving resources within their areas of responsibility.

As a result, the president does not personally manage individual DPA contracts or production projects.

Does Invoking the DPA Mean Production Immediately Increases?

No.

Some DPA authorities can affect production relatively quickly. A priority-rated order, for example, can move a government requirement ahead of lower-priority work in an existing production schedule.

But priority does not create resources that do not exist.

If the underlying problem is insufficient factories, specialized machinery, raw materials, trained workers, mines, processing facilities, or technical expertise, expanding supply can take months or years.

Title III is designed in part to address these deeper industrial-capacity problems, but financial support cannot eliminate the physical time required to construct and operate new production capacity.

What Happens If a Company Ignores a DPA Requirement?

The Defense Production Act includes enforcement provisions and criminal penalties for certain willful violations of the statute, regulations, rules, or orders issued under it.

Companies receiving properly issued priority-rated orders are also subject to detailed regulations governing when orders must be accepted, when they may or must be rejected, and how competing priority requirements are handled.

A company therefore cannot simply treat a valid DPA priority requirement as an ordinary customer request that it is free to disregard.

At the same time, the regulations recognize situations in which a company cannot fulfill an order as requested, making compliance more structured than a simple command to produce at any cost.

Common Misunderstandings

The DPA Lets the President Take Over Private Companies

No.

The law provides specific authorities involving priorities, allocations, industrial capacity, and industry coordination. Using those authorities does not automatically transfer ownership or management of a private business to the federal government.

Invoking the DPA Nationalizes Factories

No.

A company can receive a priority-rated order or federal assistance while remaining privately owned and operated.

The DPA Can Only Be Used During a War

No.

The statutory concept of national defense includes emergency preparedness, homeland security, energy, critical infrastructure, space, and other activities beyond active warfare.

The DPA Only Applies to Weapons

No.

It can apply to a broad range of materials, services, technologies, and industrial capabilities when the statutory connection to national defense exists.

The President Can Order Any Company to Make Anything

No.

The DPA provides substantial but defined legal authorities. The government must act under the statute and applicable regulations rather than exercising unlimited control over private production.

A DPA Order Creates More Products Immediately

Not necessarily.

Priority authority can redirect existing production, but it cannot instantly create factories, raw materials, specialized equipment, or trained workers that do not exist. Expanding industrial capacity can take significant time.

The President Personally Runs DPA Programs

No.

Presidential DPA authorities are extensively delegated to federal departments and agencies that administer particular programs and priority systems.

What Should You Look for When the DPA Is Invoked?

When a news report says the president has invoked the Defense Production Act, the most useful question is: Which authority is actually being used?

A Title I priority order is different from a Title III investment intended to create new production capacity. A voluntary industry agreement under Title VII serves another purpose entirely.

It is also useful to ask what product or industrial capability is involved, why the government considers it important to national defense, what federal agency is administering the action, and whether the objective is to redirect existing production or create additional capacity.

Those details reveal much more than simply saying that the DPA has been "activated."

Conclusion

The Defense Production Act is a federal law designed to ensure that the United States has access to industrial resources and productive capacity needed for national defense.

Its principal modern authorities allow the government to prioritize qualifying contracts and allocate resources under Title I, support expansion of critical domestic production capacity under Title III, and use additional preparedness and industry-coordination mechanisms under Title VII.

The DPA is broader than weapons production and can apply to areas such as emergency preparedness, energy, critical infrastructure, medical supplies, space, and strategically important supply chains.

But it is not unlimited presidential control over private industry. Invoking the DPA does not automatically nationalize factories, seize businesses, or instantly create additional production. Understanding which part of the law is being used is the key to understanding what a particular Defense Production Act action actually does.
