How Does a Bill Become Federal Law?

August 30, 202616 min read

How Does a Bill Become Federal Law?

A federal bill becomes law through a process involving both houses of Congress and, in most cases, the president.

The basic path is straightforward: a bill is introduced, considered by congressional committees, approved by both the House of Representatives and Senate in identical form, and presented to the president.

The actual process can be much more complicated. Bills may be amended repeatedly, remain in committee without receiving a vote, face a filibuster in the Senate, move between the House and Senate several times, or be vetoed by the president.

Most bills introduced in Congress never become law.

Where Can a Bill Start?

Most bills can originate in either the House of Representatives or the Senate.

A House bill is identified by H.R. followed by a number, such as H.R. 1234. A Senate bill is identified by S. followed by a number, such as S. 1234.

The Constitution contains an important exception. Article I requires bills for raising revenue to originate in the House of Representatives, although the Senate may amend those bills.

This requirement is commonly called the Origination Clause.

Who Can Introduce a Bill?

Members of Congress formally introduce legislation in their respective chambers.

A representative introduces a House bill, while a senator introduces a Senate bill.

The idea for a bill, however, may come from many sources, including:

  • Members of Congress.
  • Congressional staff.
  • Congressional committees.
  • The president or executive branch agencies.
  • State or local governments.
  • Businesses and organizations.
  • Advocacy groups.
  • Experts and researchers.
  • Members of the public.

Legislative language may be developed with assistance from congressional staff and professional legislative counsel, but a member of Congress must formally introduce a bill in the member's chamber.

What Happens When a Bill Is Introduced?

After introduction, the bill receives a number and is referred to the committee or committees with jurisdiction over its subject matter.

For example, a bill involving agriculture may be referred to an agriculture committee, while legislation involving federal taxes may be referred to a committee responsible for tax policy.

Some bills are referred to more than one committee because they involve several areas of jurisdiction.

Introduction does not mean Congress has approved the proposal. It simply places the legislation into the formal congressional process.

What Happens in Committee?

Committees perform much of the detailed work of Congress.

A committee may study a bill, request information, hold hearings, receive testimony from government officials or outside experts, and consider possible changes.

The committee may also refer the legislation to a subcommittee specializing in a narrower part of the subject.

Not every bill receives a hearing or a vote.

A committee can effectively end a bill simply by taking no further action on it. This is one reason most legislation introduced in Congress never reaches a vote by the full House or Senate.

What Is a Committee Hearing?

A congressional hearing allows committee members to gather information about legislation or a policy issue.

Witnesses may include:

  • Government officials.
  • Academic or technical experts.
  • Business representatives.
  • Advocacy organizations.
  • People directly affected by the issue.

Committee members can question witnesses and use the testimony when evaluating the legislation.

Hearings are an important congressional tool, but a hearing is not required for every bill.

What Is a Markup?

A markup is a committee meeting where members formally consider, debate, amend, and vote on legislation.

During markup, members may propose changes to the bill. The committee votes on whether to accept or reject those amendments and eventually may vote on whether to report the bill to the full chamber.

If the committee votes to report the bill favorably, the legislation can move toward consideration by the full House or Senate.

The committee may also issue a written report explaining the bill, its purpose, and the committee's recommendations.

What Happens on the House Floor?

If a House bill reaches the floor, representatives may debate it, consider permitted amendments, and eventually vote on passage.

For major legislation, the House Rules Committee frequently establishes the terms under which the bill will be considered. A House rule may determine:

  • How much debate is allowed.
  • Which amendments may be offered.
  • How amendments will be considered.
  • Other procedures governing the floor debate.

Because House rules allow debate to be limited, the House generally can bring legislation to a final vote without the type of filibuster found in the Senate.

A bill generally passes the House when it receives the required majority vote with a quorum present.

What Happens in the Senate?

A House-passed bill sent to the Senate can be referred to a Senate committee, debated, amended, and voted on.

The Senate operates under different procedures from the House. Debate on ordinary legislation can often continue unless the Senate agrees to end it.

That creates the possibility of a filibuster.

For most ordinary legislation, ending debate through cloture requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn, normally 60 votes when all 100 Senate seats are filled.

This does not mean every Senate bill needs 60 votes for final passage. Most ordinary legislation can pass with a simple majority once the Senate reaches a final vote.

The 60-vote hurdle usually concerns ending debate and overcoming a filibuster.

Can a Bill Pass the House and Senate With Different Language?

The House and Senate can approve different versions while legislation is moving through Congress, but those differences must eventually be resolved.

For a bill to be sent to the president, both chambers must approve the same legislative text.

If the House passes one version and the Senate amends it, the House may agree to the Senate amendments. The House may instead make additional changes and send the measure back to the Senate.

The chambers can exchange amendments several times while attempting to reach agreement. This is sometimes informally called ping-ponging legislation between the House and Senate.

For major disagreements, Congress may use a conference committee.

What Is a Conference Committee?

A conference committee is a temporary group of House and Senate members appointed to negotiate differences between versions of legislation passed by the two chambers.

The members are called conferees.

If the negotiators reach agreement, they produce a conference report containing compromise legislative language.

The House and Senate must each approve the conference agreement before the legislation can proceed to the president.

Conference committees are one method of resolving differences, but they are not required for every bill. Congress frequently uses exchanges of amendments and other negotiations instead.

Why Must Both Chambers Pass Identical Text?

The Constitution requires legislation to pass both the House and Senate before being presented to the president.

That means Congress cannot send the president one version approved by the House and a different version approved by the Senate.

The final legislation must reflect an agreement by both chambers on the same text.

Once that happens, the final version is prepared as an enrolled bill and presented to the president.

What Can the President Do With a Bill?

Once both chambers approve identical legislation and it is presented to the president, several outcomes are possible.

The president may:

  1. Sign the bill. It becomes law.
  2. Veto the bill. The legislation is returned to Congress with the president's objections.
  3. Take no action and allow the bill to become law without a signature under the conditions established by the Constitution.
  4. Pocket veto the bill when the constitutional requirements for a pocket veto are met.

The president cannot rewrite the bill before signing it.

What Is a Presidential Veto?

A veto occurs when the president rejects legislation and returns it to the chamber where it originated along with objections.

A presidential veto does not necessarily end the legislation.

Congress can attempt to override the veto.

To override a presidential veto, two-thirds of both the House and Senate must vote to override under the Constitution's requirements.

If both chambers successfully override the veto, the bill becomes law without the president's approval.

If either chamber fails to achieve the required two-thirds vote, the veto stands and the bill does not become law.

Can the President Veto Part of a Bill?

No, not under current federal constitutional law.

The president generally must approve or reject the bill presented by Congress as a whole.

Congress attempted to give the president a statutory line-item veto in the 1990s, which would have allowed the president to cancel certain individual spending and tax provisions after signing legislation.

In Clinton v. City of New York (1998), the Supreme Court held that procedure unconstitutional because it did not comply with the Constitution's requirements for enacting legislation.

A president therefore cannot simply cross out provisions of a federal bill, sign the rest, and make the altered version law.

Can a Bill Become Law Without the President's Signature?

Yes.

Article I, Section 7 of the Constitution gives the president 10 days, excluding Sundays, after a bill is presented to act on it.

If the president does not sign or veto the bill within that period and Congress has not adjourned in a way that prevents the bill from being returned, the legislation becomes law without the president's signature.

This is an important exception to the common statement that every federal law must be signed by the president.

What Is a Pocket Veto?

A pocket veto can occur when the president does not sign a bill during the constitutional 10-day period and Congress adjourns in a way that prevents the president from returning the legislation.

In that situation, the bill does not become law.

Unlike an ordinary veto, a pocket veto does not return the legislation to Congress for an immediate override vote.

If lawmakers still want the proposal to become law, Congress generally must pass legislation again through the legislative process.

Whether a particular congressional adjournment permits a pocket veto has produced constitutional disputes, so the important principle is that the adjournment must prevent the president from returning the bill as contemplated by the Constitution.

Does the President Have Exactly 10 Calendar Days?

No.

The Constitution provides 10 days excluding Sundays.

The timing begins when the bill is formally presented to the president, not simply when Congress announces that it passed the legislation.

This is why news reports sometimes identify the date a measure was formally presented to the White House when discussing the president's deadline for action.

What Happens After a Bill Becomes Law?

Once enacted, a public bill or joint resolution becomes a public law and receives a public law number.

Federal laws are published in the United States Statutes at Large, the official chronological collection of laws enacted by Congress.

Provisions of permanent and general federal law may also be organized into the United States Code.

Some laws take effect immediately when enacted. Others contain a future effective date or instruct federal agencies to develop regulations before parts of the law can be implemented.

Passing a law therefore does not always mean every provision takes practical effect immediately.

Does a Bill Automatically Become a Regulation?

No.

Congress often passes laws giving federal agencies responsibility for carrying out particular programs or requirements.

An agency may then need to issue regulations explaining how the law will be implemented.

Federal rulemaking is a separate process governed by statutes including the Administrative Procedure Act and other laws applicable to the agency or program.

Congress creates the statutory authority. Agencies implement that authority within the limits established by law.

What Is the Difference Between a Bill and a Joint Resolution?

A bill is the most common form of federal legislation and is designated H.R. in the House or S. in the Senate.

A joint resolution is designated H.J.Res. or S.J.Res.

For ordinary legislation, a joint resolution generally follows the same constitutional process as a bill: it must pass both chambers in identical form and be presented to the president.

Joint resolutions are sometimes used for continuing appropriations, emergency legislation, and other specific purposes.

There is one major exception: a joint resolution proposing an amendment to the Constitution does not go to the president. A constitutional amendment proposed by two-thirds of both chambers instead goes to the states for possible ratification under Article V.

What Is a Concurrent Resolution?

A concurrent resolution is approved by both the House and Senate but is not presented to the president and does not have the force of federal law.

Concurrent resolutions may be used for matters affecting both chambers, such as congressional procedures or expressions of congressional sentiment.

The congressional budget resolution is another important example of a concurrent resolution.

Because concurrent resolutions do not become statutory law, a headline saying Congress "passed a resolution" does not necessarily mean Congress enacted a federal law.

What Is a Simple Resolution?

A simple resolution concerns only one chamber of Congress.

House simple resolutions are designated H.Res., while Senate simple resolutions are designated S.Res.

They can be used for internal rules, chamber operations, expressions of opinion, and other matters involving that chamber.

Simple resolutions are not presented to the president and do not become federal statutory law.

Does Every Bill Receive a Vote?

No.

Thousands of bills may be introduced during a Congress, and many never receive a vote in either chamber.

Legislation can stop at numerous stages:

  • A committee may take no action.
  • A committee may vote against advancing it.
  • House or Senate leaders may never schedule it for floor consideration.
  • The House may defeat it.
  • The Senate may defeat it or fail to overcome a filibuster.
  • The chambers may fail to agree on identical language.
  • The president may veto it without Congress overriding the veto.

Simply introducing a bill therefore says very little about its chances of becoming law.

What Happens to Unfinished Bills at the End of a Congress?

Each Congress lasts approximately two years, beginning in January of an odd-numbered year and ending when the next Congress begins.

A bill that has not completed the legislative process by the end of that Congress generally dies.

It does not automatically carry forward into the next Congress.

If lawmakers still want to pursue the proposal, the legislation generally must be introduced again and begin the legislative process in the new Congress.

The relevant deadline is therefore the end of the two-year Congress, not December 31 of every calendar year.

Does Passing the House Mean a Bill Is Law?

No.

House passage is only one stage.

The Senate must also approve the legislation, and the two chambers must agree to identical text before it can be presented to the president.

The same is true in reverse when legislation passes the Senate first.

Does Every Senate Bill Need 60 Votes?

No.

Most ordinary legislation can pass the Senate on a simple-majority final vote.

The familiar 60-vote threshold generally arises from the Senate's cloture rule, which is used to limit debate and overcome a filibuster on most legislation.

Some legislation operates under special procedures. For example, qualifying budget reconciliation legislation receives limited debate and therefore is not subject to the ordinary legislative filibuster.

Understanding whether a Senate vote concerns cloture or final passage is essential when interpreting congressional vote totals.

Can Congress Skip Committees?

Congressional rules provide mechanisms that can sometimes allow legislation to reach the floor without following the most common committee path.

The exact procedures differ between the House and Senate and depend on the circumstances.

The committee process remains the normal route for most legislation, but the simplified diagram showing every bill moving through precisely the same sequence should not be treated as an absolute rule.

Common Misunderstandings

The House Passed the Bill, So It Is Law

No.

Both the House and Senate must approve identical legislation, and the constitutional presentment process must still be completed.

Congress Passed Different Versions, So the President Can Choose Between Them

No.

The House and Senate must agree to the same legislative text before the measure can be presented to the president.

Every Bill Needs 60 Votes in the Senate

No.

Most ordinary legislation requires a simple majority for final passage. Sixty votes are commonly needed to invoke cloture and overcome a filibuster before that final vote can occur.

The President Must Sign Every Bill for It to Become Law

No.

A bill can become law without the president's signature if the president takes no action during the constitutional 10-day period, excluding Sundays, and Congress remains able to receive a veto return.

A veto can also be overridden by the required two-thirds votes in both chambers.

The President Can Sign the Parts of a Bill They Like and Veto the Rest

No.

The federal government currently has no constitutional line-item veto allowing the president to rewrite legislation in that manner.

A Committee Has to Vote Against a Bill to Kill It

No.

A committee can effectively stop legislation simply by never advancing it.

A Bill Dies at the End of Every Calendar Year

No.

Unfinished legislation generally dies at the end of the two-year Congress in which it was introduced.

Every Federal Law Starts as a House or Senate Bill

Not exactly.

Joint resolutions can also become federal law through essentially the same process as bills, except that joint resolutions proposing constitutional amendments follow the Article V ratification process and do not go to the president.

A Congressional Resolution Is Always a Law

No.

Joint resolutions can become law, but concurrent and simple resolutions generally do not have the force of statutory law.

Why the Legislative Process Matters

Political news often describes legislation using phrases such as introduced, reported by committee, passed the House, cleared cloture, passed the Senate, sent to the president, vetoed, or signed into law.

Those phrases describe very different stages.

A proposal introduced by a member of Congress may be far from becoming law. A bill passed by one chamber still has major hurdles ahead. Even legislation approved by both chambers cannot be presented to the president until the House and Senate have agreed to identical text.

Understanding those stages helps readers distinguish between a policy proposal, legislation that has advanced, and an actual change in federal law.

Conclusion

A bill becomes federal law through a process involving Congress and the president. A member of Congress introduces the legislation, committees generally review it, and the House and Senate consider and vote on it. Both chambers must eventually approve identical text.

The final legislation is then presented to the president, who may sign it, veto it, or take no action. A bill can become law without a presidential signature under the Constitution's 10-day rule, while Congress can override a regular presidential veto with the required two-thirds vote in each chamber.

The process contains many opportunities for legislation to change or fail, which is why introduction or passage by one chamber should never be confused with enactment. A proposal becomes federal statutory law only after it successfully completes the constitutional legislative process.
