What Is New Hampshire's First-in-the-Nation Presidential Primary?

The First-in-the-Nation Presidential Primary is the first primary election held during the presidential nomination process. For more than a century, New Hampshire has traditionally held this distinction, making it one of the earliest and most closely watched events of every presidential election cycle.

Because it takes place before nearly every other presidential primary, the New Hampshire primary often receives intense media coverage and attracts candidates from across the country. While it does not determine who will become president, it can significantly influence the race for a political party's nomination.

What Does "First-in-the-Nation" Mean?

The phrase "First-in-the-Nation" refers to New Hampshire's long-standing tradition of holding the nation's first presidential primary.

It is important to distinguish between a primary and a caucus.

A primary is an election in which voters cast ballots to help determine a political party's nominee.

A caucus is a party-run meeting where participants gather to discuss candidates, select delegates, or conduct other party business.

Although Iowa's caucuses have historically occurred before New Hampshire's primary, New Hampshire has traditionally remained the first presidential primary election.

Why Is New Hampshire First?

New Hampshire's presidential primary dates back to the early 20th century. The state enacted its presidential primary law in 1916, but the contest became especially influential after changes to the presidential nomination process in the late 1960s and early 1970s gave voters a larger role in selecting party nominees.

New Hampshire Revised Statutes Annotated (RSA) 653:9 requires the Secretary of State to schedule the presidential primary at least seven days before any other similar state-run presidential primary. As a result, New Hampshire election officials have often adjusted the election date to preserve the state's first-in-the-nation status.

Why Does the First Primary Matter?

Although relatively few delegates are awarded in New Hampshire compared to larger states, the results can shape the rest of the campaign.

A strong performance may:

  • Increase national media attention.
  • Boost fundraising.
  • Improve polling in later states.
  • Demonstrate a candidate's ability to attract voters.

Likewise, a poor performance may lead candidates to reconsider their campaigns or shift their strategy.

Because the primary occurs so early, many voters and political observers view it as an early test of candidate organization, messaging, and voter support.

Why Do Candidates Spend So Much Time in New Hampshire?

New Hampshire is one of the nation's smallest states by population.

Supporters of the early primary argue that its size encourages "retail politics," where candidates spend time meeting voters in town halls, community centers, restaurants, schools, and other local venues rather than relying primarily on large rallies or television advertising.

This allows voters to ask questions directly and evaluate candidates up close before larger states begin voting.

Why Is It Controversial?

The First-in-the-Nation Primary has long been debated.

Supporters argue that New Hampshire's size allows lesser-known candidates to compete without needing enormous campaign budgets. They also believe voters benefit from direct interactions with candidates and that the state's long history provides stability in the presidential nomination process.

Critics argue that no single state should have such an influential role in selecting presidential nominees. They note that New Hampshire's population does not reflect the full geographic, racial, ethnic, and economic diversity of the United States. Some also believe rotating the early contests or placing different states first would provide broader representation.

State Law and National Party Rules

The presidential nomination calendar is influenced by both state laws and the rules established by the national political parties.

New Hampshire law requires the state to hold its presidential primary before any other similar state-run presidential primary.

At the same time, the Democratic National Committee (DNC) and Republican National Committee (RNC) establish rules governing their presidential nominating processes, including which states are authorized to hold early contests. If a state or state party does not follow the approved calendar, the national party may impose penalties, such as reducing or refusing to seat some of that state's delegates at the national convention.

Because state law and national party rules do not always align, disagreements have occasionally arisen over the scheduling of presidential primaries. These disputes reflect the ongoing balance between state authority over elections and the political parties' authority to set their own nomination procedures.

Common Misunderstandings

The First Primary Decides the Presidential Election

No.

The New Hampshire primary is only one step in the presidential nomination process. Candidates must continue competing in primaries and caucuses across the country before delegates formally select a party's nominee.

New Hampshire Always Holds the First Election

Not exactly.

New Hampshire has traditionally held the first presidential primary, but presidential caucuses—most notably Iowa's—have often taken place before it.

Every Candidate Must Win New Hampshire

No.

Many eventual presidential nominees and presidents have lost the New Hampshire primary or performed worse than expected before ultimately securing their party's nomination.

Why the First-in-the-Nation Primary Matters

The New Hampshire primary has become one of the best-known traditions in American presidential politics. Its early position on the election calendar often shapes media coverage, fundraising, campaign strategy, and public perceptions of candidates.

Whether its traditional role should continue remains the subject of ongoing debate, but its influence on the presidential nomination process has made it one of the most significant state elections in the United States.

Conclusion

The First-in-the-Nation Presidential Primary refers to New Hampshire's long-standing role as the first state to hold a presidential primary election. Although it awards only a small share of delegates, its early timing gives it an outsized role in shaping presidential campaigns. The state's tradition, its legal protections, and continuing debates over the national nominating calendar have made the New Hampshire primary a unique and enduring feature of American elections.
