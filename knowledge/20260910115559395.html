Why Do We Have Leap Years?

September 13, 20266 min read

A leap year is a year with 366 days instead of the usual 365. The extra day, February 29, is added because Earth's trip around the Sun does not take exactly 365 days.

Without occasional leap days, our calendar would slowly drift away from the seasons. Over centuries, dates associated with spring, summer, fall and winter would occur at increasingly different points in Earth's orbit.

Leap years are the calendar's way of correcting for that mismatch.

A Year Is Not Exactly 365 Days

A calendar year is intended to stay synchronized with the cycle of the seasons. But nature does not divide neatly into whole numbers of days.

The seasonal year, known as the tropical year, lasts approximately 365.2422 days.

That means a calendar containing exactly 365 days every year would be short by about 0.2422 day — a little less than six hours.

That difference may seem insignificant, but it accumulates.

After four years, a simple 365-day calendar would be almost one full day out of alignment with the seasonal year. After about a century, the error would grow to roughly 24 days if nothing were done to correct it.

Adding an extra day approximately every four years keeps the calendar much more closely aligned with Earth's orbit and the seasons.

Why Is There a Leap Year Every Four Years?

Because the difference is close to one-quarter of a day per year, adding one day every four years provides a simple correction.

Four calendar years of 365 days contain 1,460 days. Adding one leap day produces 1,461 days over four years, for an average of 365.25 days per year.

That is much closer to the actual length of the seasonal year than 365 days.

But it is still not exact.

The actual tropical year is about 365.2422 days, not 365.25 days. A calendar that added one day every four years forever would therefore add slightly too much time.

That small difference is why the leap-year rule has an additional correction.

The 100-Year and 400-Year Rules

The calendar used by most of the world today is the Gregorian calendar.

Its leap-year rules are:

  • A year divisible by 4 is normally a leap year.
  • A year divisible by 100 is not a leap year.
  • A year divisible by 400 is a leap year after all.

For example, 2024 was a leap year because it is divisible by 4.

The year 2100 will not be a leap year because it is divisible by 100 but not by 400.

The year 2000 was a leap year because it is divisible by 400.

This may seem unnecessarily complicated, but the extra rules significantly improve the calendar's accuracy.

Why Isn't 2100 a Leap Year?

If we simply added a leap day every four years, the average calendar year would be 365.25 days long. That is about 11 minutes longer than the tropical year.

Eleven minutes per year does not sound like much, but over many centuries it adds up.

By skipping leap years in most century years, the Gregorian calendar removes some of those extra days. Keeping years divisible by 400 as leap years prevents the correction from going too far.

Over a 400-year Gregorian calendar cycle, there are 97 leap years rather than 100.

Those 400 years contain 146,097 days, giving an average calendar year of 365.2425 days.

That is extremely close to the tropical year of about 365.2422 days.

Why Is the Extra Day in February?

The answer comes from the history of the calendar rather than astronomy.

The modern calendar developed from earlier Roman calendars. February historically occupied a special position in the Roman system and became the month in which calendar adjustments were made.

When the Roman calendar was reformed under Julius Caesar, the resulting Julian calendar established a regular system of leap years. February eventually became the month containing the additional leap day.

Later calendar reforms kept that tradition, which is why February has 29 days during a leap year instead of another month receiving an extra day.

There is no astronomical reason the leap day must occur in February. It is a historical convention.

What Was Wrong With the Julian Calendar?

The Julian calendar, introduced under Julius Caesar, used a simple rule: add a leap day every four years.

That produced an average year of exactly 365.25 days.

For everyday life, that was an enormous improvement over earlier calendar systems. But the small difference between 365.25 days and the actual seasonal year gradually accumulated.

Over many centuries, the calendar shifted relative to astronomical events and the seasons.

By the 1500s, the accumulated error had become large enough to create problems for the calculation of important religious dates tied to the seasonal cycle.

Pope Gregory XIII introduced the Gregorian calendar in 1582 to correct the accumulated drift and prevent it from returning as quickly.

The reform adjusted the calendar and introduced the century-year rules still used today.

Different countries adopted the Gregorian calendar at different times, so the transition did not occur everywhere at once.

Is a Leap Year Exactly the Same Length as Earth's Orbit?

No.

Leap years do not attempt to make every individual calendar year match one orbit of Earth exactly. Instead, the system makes the average length of many calendar years closely match the seasonal year.

A normal calendar year contains 365 days. A leap year contains 366. Over a 400-year Gregorian cycle, the mixture of normal and leap years averages 365.2425 days.

That is close enough that the calendar drifts by only a small amount over very long periods.

Even the Gregorian calendar is not mathematically perfect. Earth's rotation and orbital characteristics also change slightly over long periods, so no fixed calendar can remain perfectly synchronized forever.

Is a Leap Day Related to Leap Seconds?

No. Despite the similar names, they solve different problems.

A leap day helps keep the calendar aligned with Earth's revolution around the Sun and the seasonal year.

A leap second has been used to keep atomic time sufficiently close to time based on Earth's rotation. Earth's rotation is not perfectly uniform, so occasional adjustments have historically been made to civil timekeeping.

The two systems deal with different astronomical motions and should not be confused.

What Happens If Someone Is Born on February 29?

A person born on February 29 still ages normally, of course. Their birthday simply falls on a date that appears on the calendar only during leap years.

In non-leap years, people born on February 29 commonly celebrate on February 28 or March 1. Which date is treated as their legal birthday for particular purposes can depend on the laws or rules involved.

The unusual birthday is simply a consequence of how our calendar handles the extra fraction of a day in Earth's yearly cycle.

The Bottom Line

We have leap years because Earth's seasonal cycle does not fit neatly into a 365-day calendar. A tropical year lasts about 365.2422 days, leaving an extra fraction of a day that accumulates from year to year.

Adding February 29 approximately every four years corrects most of that difference. The Gregorian calendar improves the correction by skipping most century leap years while keeping years divisible by 400 as leap years.

The result is a calendar whose average length stays remarkably close to Earth's seasonal year, keeping our dates and seasons aligned over centuries.
