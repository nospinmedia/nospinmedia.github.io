What Happens When Congress Can't Pass a Budget?

August 31, 202612 min read

What Happens When Congress Can't Pass a Budget?

When people say Congress "can't pass a budget," they may mean several different things.

Congress might fail to adopt a budget resolution, fail to complete the annual appropriations bills that fund federal agencies, or fail to agree on temporary funding before existing appropriations expire.

Those situations have different consequences.

Most importantly, failure to adopt a congressional budget resolution does not automatically cause a government shutdown. A shutdown can occur when legal funding authority expires for federal operations and Congress has not enacted new appropriations or temporary funding to replace it.

The basic sequence is:

Budget resolution → appropriations → temporary funding if needed → funding lapse if funding expires → possible government shutdown.

Understanding those separate steps makes federal budget disputes much easier to follow.

What Is the Congressional Budget Resolution?

The congressional budget resolution is a framework Congress uses to establish overall fiscal goals involving federal spending, revenues, deficits, debt, and related budget policy.

The House and Senate must agree to the same budget resolution, but it is a concurrent resolution rather than a regular law.

That means it does not go to the president for signature and does not itself provide federal agencies with money to operate.

Instead, the budget resolution establishes congressional budget targets and can create procedural tools such as budget reconciliation instructions.

Congress can fail to adopt a budget resolution and still fund the federal government through appropriations legislation.

What Actually Funds Federal Agencies?

For much of the federal government's discretionary spending, the key legislation is appropriations.

An appropriation gives a federal agency legal budget authority to incur obligations and make payments for specified purposes.

This is different from the congressional budget resolution, which sets broader fiscal targets but does not itself provide operating money.

The practical question when a funding deadline approaches is therefore usually not simply whether Congress has passed a "budget." It is whether the necessary appropriations or other legal funding authority are in place.

What Are the 12 Regular Appropriations Bills?

Congress organizes most annual discretionary funding through 12 regular appropriations measures covering major areas of the federal government.

These include funding areas such as defense, agriculture, homeland security, transportation, housing, veterans programs, health and education programs, federal law enforcement, environmental programs, and general government operations.

Congress does not have to enact all 12 as separate standalone laws. Several appropriations measures can be combined into larger packages.

An omnibus appropriations bill generally combines many appropriations measures into one large package.

A minibus is an informal term generally used when several appropriations measures are combined, but fewer than would typically be described as an omnibus.

The important point is that Congress must provide valid funding authority. It does not have to enact exactly 12 separate laws to accomplish that.

What Happens on October 1?

The federal fiscal year begins on October 1.

Ideally, Congress has enacted the necessary appropriations for the new fiscal year before that date.

In practice, Congress frequently reaches October 1 without completing all of the regular appropriations work.

That does not automatically produce a shutdown.

Congress can pass temporary funding legislation called a continuing resolution, allowing covered federal operations to continue while lawmakers negotiate longer-term appropriations.

What Is a Continuing Resolution?

A continuing resolution, commonly called a CR, is temporary appropriations legislation that keeps covered federal agencies and programs funded for a specified period.

A CR often continues funding based on existing programs and funding arrangements, subject to whatever changes or exceptions Congress includes in the legislation.

For example, Congress might enact a CR funding affected agencies until November or December rather than completing full-year appropriations by October 1.

If negotiations remain unresolved when that CR approaches expiration, Congress can enact another one. Multiple CRs can therefore extend temporary funding for weeks or months while lawmakers continue negotiating.

A CR prevents an immediate funding lapse, but it does not mean Congress has completed the normal annual appropriations process.

Why Can Continuing Resolutions Be Difficult for Agencies?

Although a CR can keep the government operating, temporary funding creates uncertainty.

Agencies may not know their final annual funding levels and can face restrictions on beginning new programs or substantially changing existing activities unless Congress specifically provides authority to do so.

Temporary funding can complicate decisions involving:

  • Hiring.
  • New contracts.
  • Major purchases.
  • Program expansions.
  • Long-term projects.

Congress can also include special provisions in a CR to address particular funding needs that cannot reasonably wait for final appropriations.

What Happens If Temporary Funding Also Expires?

If an agency's existing appropriations expire and Congress has not enacted replacement funding or another source of legal budget authority, a lapse in appropriations occurs for the affected operations.

This is the point at which a government shutdown can begin.

The Antideficiency Act generally prevents federal agencies from incurring obligations or spending money without available appropriations or another lawful source of budget authority.

During a funding lapse, affected agencies must stop activities that are not legally authorized to continue.

Some activities can continue because they have another source of funding or fall within legal exceptions. Others must stop until Congress restores funding.

The issue is therefore not that the federal government has literally run out of money. The problem is that an agency may no longer have legal authority to obligate or spend it for affected operations.

Can Only Part of the Government Shut Down?

Yes.

Congress may complete appropriations for some parts of the government while leaving others unfinished.

If funding expires only for the unfinished agencies, the result can be a partial government shutdown.

Agencies whose funding has already been enacted can continue operating under that authority, while agencies affected by the lapse follow their shutdown procedures.

This is why the effects of a shutdown can vary considerably across the federal government.

What Happens to Federal Workers During a Funding Lapse?

Federal workers are not all treated the same way during a shutdown.

Some employees may be furloughed, meaning they are temporarily prohibited from working because their activities lack available funding and are not legally authorized to continue.

Other employees continue working because their duties are legally permitted during the lapse or because their work is supported by funding that has not expired.

A shutdown therefore does not mean every federal employee stops working.

What Happens to Mandatory Spending?

Failure to enact annual discretionary appropriations does not automatically stop all federal spending.

Much federal spending is classified as mandatory spending. The underlying law provides budget authority according to statutory rules rather than requiring a new annual appropriation for every benefit payment.

Major programs such as Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid are largely financed through mandatory spending structures.

Their underlying benefits therefore do not simply disappear because Congress has not completed the regular annual appropriations process.

However, agencies administering mandatory programs may depend on annually appropriated funds for some staffing and administrative functions. Those operations can still be affected by a shutdown.

Does the Government Shut Down If Congress Doesn't Pass a Budget Resolution?

No.

This is one of the most important distinctions in the federal budget process.

The budget resolution provides Congress with a fiscal framework, but it does not itself fund federal agencies.

Congress can fail to adopt a budget resolution and still enact appropriations that keep the government operating.

Conversely, Congress could adopt a budget resolution and still face a shutdown later if it fails to provide the necessary appropriations.

Why Do People Say Congress Has to "Pass a Budget"?

The phrase is often used as shorthand for the broader annual spending process.

When politicians or news reports say Congress must "pass a budget to keep the government open," they may actually be referring to appropriations legislation or a continuing resolution.

Those are the measures that provide the legal funding authority at issue in most shutdown deadlines.

The formal congressional budget resolution serves a different purpose.

Does Congress Have to Balance the Federal Budget?

No.

There is no general constitutional requirement that federal spending equal federal revenue every year.

When federal spending exceeds federal revenues during a fiscal year, the government runs a budget deficit.

A deficit does not mean Congress failed to pass a budget or that federal spending is automatically illegal.

The government finances part of the difference through borrowing, subject to applicable federal law.

What Is the Difference Between a Deficit and a Funding Lapse?

A budget deficit means federal spending exceeds federal revenues over a period.

A funding lapse occurs when legal budget authority expires for affected federal operations before replacement funding is enacted.

The federal government can run a large deficit while every agency remains funded and operating normally.

Likewise, a shutdown can occur because of an appropriations dispute even though the immediate dispute has little to do with whether the overall federal budget is balanced.

Is a Funding Dispute the Same as a Debt-Limit Crisis?

No.

The debt limit, often called the debt ceiling, concerns the Treasury Department's statutory authority to issue debt to finance federal obligations.

Appropriations concern the legal authority for federal agencies to incur obligations and spend money.

The congressional budget resolution establishes broader fiscal targets and congressional procedures.

These are separate issues:

  • Budget resolution: Congress's fiscal framework.
  • Appropriations: Funding authority for federal operations.
  • Debt limit: Treasury's statutory borrowing authority.

A shutdown caused by an appropriations lapse and a crisis involving the federal debt limit therefore are not the same event and can have different consequences.

Can Congress Fund Some Agencies for the Full Year and Others Temporarily?

Yes.

Congress can enact full-year appropriations for some agencies while temporarily extending funding for others.

That can leave only part of the government facing another funding deadline later.

Congress can also combine several full-year appropriations measures into a larger package rather than passing each one separately.

The result is that federal funding does not always move through Congress as one single all-or-nothing bill.

What If Congress Never Passes All 12 Regular Appropriations Bills Separately?

That does not necessarily prevent the government from being funded for the fiscal year.

Congress can combine appropriations into larger legislative packages or use other appropriations legislation to provide funding through the remainder of the fiscal year.

The important legal question is whether valid budget authority has been enacted for the affected federal operations, not whether Congress passed exactly 12 separate laws.

Is Budget Reconciliation Used to Keep the Government Open?

Generally, no.

Budget reconciliation is a special congressional process used to make certain changes involving mandatory spending, revenues, and the debt limit under expedited Senate procedures.

It is different from the annual appropriations process used to fund most discretionary federal agencies.

A reconciliation bill and an appropriations bill can both affect the federal budget, but they perform different functions.

What Does a Typical Funding Standoff Look Like?

A simplified example shows how the process can unfold.

Suppose October 1 is approaching and Congress has not finished several appropriations bills.

Rather than allow funding to expire, Congress passes a continuing resolution extending current funding until November 15.

Lawmakers continue negotiating during those additional weeks.

If they reach an agreement, they may enact full-year appropriations before November 15.

If they need more time, Congress could pass another CR extending funding into December.

If lawmakers fail to enact either full-year appropriations or another CR before the deadline, funding lapses for the affected agencies and a shutdown can begin.

This cycle explains why news coverage can contain several government-shutdown deadlines during a single fiscal year.

Common Misunderstandings

No Budget by October 1 Automatically Means a Government Shutdown

No.

Congress can use a continuing resolution to maintain funding while negotiations continue.

The Budget Resolution Funds Federal Agencies

No.

It establishes congressional fiscal targets and procedures. Appropriations and other sources of budget authority provide the legal authority to spend.

A Continuing Resolution Is the Federal Budget

No.

A CR is temporary appropriations legislation.

Congress Gets Only One Chance to Pass a CR

No.

Congress can enact multiple continuing resolutions, repeatedly extending funding deadlines.

Congress Must Pass Exactly 12 Separate Spending Laws

No.

The regular appropriations process is organized around 12 measures, but Congress can combine them into larger packages.

A Shutdown Stops Every Federal Program

No.

Programs and agencies can have different funding sources and legal authorities. A shutdown can also be partial.

Social Security Automatically Stops During a Shutdown

No.

Social Security benefits are primarily mandatory spending, although some administrative functions can still be affected.

Congress Must Balance the Federal Budget Every Year

No.

The federal government can legally operate with a budget deficit.

A Budget Deficit Means Congress Failed to Pass a Budget

No.

A deficit measures the difference between federal spending and revenue. It is not the same as failing to complete the congressional budget or appropriations process.

A Government Shutdown and a Debt-Limit Crisis Are the Same Thing

No.

A shutdown generally concerns a lapse in funding authority. The debt limit concerns Treasury's borrowing authority.

Why These Distinctions Matter

Federal budget news frequently compresses several different processes into the single word budget.

That can make a routine funding dispute sound simpler than it actually is.

Congress may fail to adopt a budget resolution without shutting down the government. It may adopt a budget resolution but still fail to complete appropriations. It may prevent a shutdown temporarily with one or more continuing resolutions. It may fund some agencies for the full year while leaving others on temporary funding.

The most useful question when a shutdown deadline approaches is therefore:

Has Congress provided legal funding authority for the federal operations whose current funding is about to expire?

That question tells readers far more about the actual risk of a shutdown than simply asking whether Congress has "passed a budget."

Conclusion

When Congress cannot agree on what is commonly called the federal budget, the consequences depend on which part of the budget process remains unfinished.

Failure to adopt a congressional budget resolution does not automatically cause a shutdown because the budget resolution is primarily a congressional fiscal framework rather than legislation that funds federal agencies.

If regular appropriations are unfinished, Congress can enact a continuing resolution to keep affected operations funded temporarily. Congress may use multiple CRs while negotiations continue. If the relevant funding eventually expires without replacement budget authority, a lapse in appropriations occurs and federal law generally requires affected operations that cannot legally continue to stop.

The distinction between budget resolutions, appropriations, continuing resolutions, funding lapses, and the debt limit explains why Congress can miss its normal budget deadlines without immediately shutting down the government—and why some missed funding deadlines eventually do produce a shutdown.
