What Is a Solar Eclipse?

September 1, 202613 min read

What Is a Solar Eclipse?

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun and blocks some or all of the Sun from view for people in certain parts of Earth.

Solar eclipses can be total, partial, annular, or hybrid depending on the alignment of the Sun, Moon, and Earth and the apparent size of the Moon in the sky.

Although the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun around every new moon, solar eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's orbit around the Sun.

What Causes a Solar Eclipse?

A solar eclipse requires the Sun, Moon, and Earth to line up closely enough for the Moon's shadow to fall on Earth.

The arrangement is:

Sun → Moon → Earth

This can happen only around the time of a new moon, when the Moon is positioned between Earth and the Sun.

The Moon casts different parts of its shadow onto Earth. Where an observer is located relative to those shadows determines what type of eclipse the person sees.

What Are the Types of Solar Eclipses?

There are four basic types of solar eclipses:

  • Total solar eclipse.
  • Partial solar eclipse.
  • Annular solar eclipse.
  • Hybrid solar eclipse.

Each results from a slightly different alignment and viewing geometry.

What Is a Total Solar Eclipse?

A total solar eclipse occurs when the Moon completely covers the Sun's bright visible surface for observers within a narrow area on Earth.

That area is known as the path of totality.

During totality, the sky can become dramatically darker, bright stars or planets may become visible, temperatures may drop, and the Sun's faint outer atmosphere—the corona—can be seen surrounding the dark Moon.

Totality lasts only a few minutes or less from most individual viewing locations, although the exact duration varies from one eclipse and location to another.

Observers outside the path of totality may see a partial eclipse instead.

What Is a Partial Solar Eclipse?

A partial solar eclipse occurs when the Moon covers only part of the Sun's visible disk from an observer's location.

People outside the path of totality during a total eclipse often experience a partial eclipse.

The percentage of the Sun that appears covered depends on the observer's location.

Even an extremely deep partial eclipse is different from totality. If a small portion of the Sun's bright surface remains visible, the corona does not become visible in the same way and proper solar eye protection remains necessary.

What Is an Annular Solar Eclipse?

An annular solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes directly in front of the Sun but appears too small to completely cover it.

This happens because the Moon's distance from Earth varies. When the Moon is relatively farther from Earth, its apparent size in the sky can be smaller than the Sun's apparent size.

A bright ring of the Sun remains visible around the Moon. This is commonly called the ring of fire.

Unlike a total solar eclipse, an annular eclipse never has a period when the Sun's bright surface is completely covered. Proper solar viewing protection is therefore required throughout the entire annular eclipse.

What Is a Hybrid Solar Eclipse?

A hybrid solar eclipse is an uncommon eclipse that appears total from some locations along its path and annular from others.

This can happen because Earth's surface is curved and the distances among the observer, Moon, and Sun vary slightly along the eclipse path.

At some locations, the Moon appears large enough to completely cover the Sun. At others, it appears slightly too small, producing an annular eclipse.

Why Isn't There a Solar Eclipse Every Month?

The Moon reaches the new-moon phase approximately once each month, but its orbit around Earth is tilted by about 5 degrees relative to the plane of Earth's orbit around the Sun.

Most months, the Moon's shadow therefore passes above or below Earth rather than falling on its surface.

Solar eclipses occur only when the new moon is sufficiently close to one of the locations where the Moon's orbit crosses Earth's orbital plane.

These crossing points are called nodes.

When the Sun is near one of those nodes at the same time the Moon is new, the alignment can produce a solar eclipse.

What Is an Eclipse Season?

An eclipse season is a period when the Sun is close enough to one of the Moon's orbital nodes for eclipses to occur.

Eclipse seasons occur a little less than six months apart and last for several weeks.

During an eclipse season, the geometry of the Sun, Earth, and Moon makes at least one eclipse possible. Depending on the timing of the lunar phases, a season can include solar and lunar eclipses.

This is why eclipses tend to occur in groups during certain periods rather than being distributed randomly throughout the year.

How Can the Moon Cover the Much Larger Sun?

The Sun is vastly larger than the Moon, but it is also vastly farther away from Earth.

Because of those very different sizes and distances, the Sun and Moon appear to be roughly similar in size when viewed from Earth.

This apparent-size coincidence makes total solar eclipses possible.

The Moon's distance from Earth changes because its orbit is not perfectly circular. When the Moon appears large enough, it can completely cover the Sun and create a total eclipse. When it appears slightly smaller, an annular eclipse can occur instead.

What Is the Path of Totality?

The path of totality is the relatively narrow region of Earth's surface where observers can see the Moon completely cover the Sun during a total solar eclipse.

This path forms as the Moon's darkest central shadow moves across Earth.

People inside the path can experience totality. People outside it may see only a partial eclipse, even if they are geographically close to the path.

This distinction is significant. A location experiencing 99 percent coverage is still seeing a partial eclipse rather than totality.

What Are the Umbra and Penumbra?

The Moon's shadow contains different regions.

The umbra is the darkest central portion of the shadow. During a total solar eclipse, observers reached by the umbra experience totality.

The penumbra is the lighter outer portion of the shadow. Observers in the penumbra see a partial eclipse.

During an annular eclipse, the geometry also produces an antumbra, where the observer sees the Moon directly in front of the Sun but too small to cover it completely.

How Long Does a Solar Eclipse Last?

The complete eclipse event at a particular location can last several hours from the beginning of the partial phase through the end.

Totality itself is much shorter, generally lasting only a few minutes or less at a particular location.

The exact duration depends on factors including the distances between Earth, the Moon, and the Sun, the observer's position within the eclipse path, and the relative motion of the bodies.

Someone near the center of the path of totality generally experiences a longer period of totality than someone near its edge.

Is It Safe to Look at a Solar Eclipse?

Looking directly at the Sun is unsafe during the partial phases of a solar eclipse without proper eye protection.

Ordinary sunglasses are not sufficient protection, regardless of how dark they are.

For direct solar viewing, appropriate eclipse glasses or handheld solar viewers designed for observing the Sun should be used. Solar viewers are commonly manufactured to meet the ISO 12312-2 safety requirements for direct observation of the Sun.

Viewers should be obtained from a reliable source and inspected before use. Damaged solar filters should not be used.

Can You Look at the Sun During Totality?

There is one important exception to the normal solar-viewing rule.

During a total solar eclipse, observers who are inside the path of totality may view the eclipse without solar filters during the brief period when the Moon completely covers the Sun's bright surface.

Solar filters must remain in use throughout all partial phases.

As soon as any portion of the bright Sun begins to reappear, viewers must look away and put their solar protection back on before looking toward the Sun again.

This exception applies only during actual totality. It does not apply to a partial or annular eclipse.

Is an Annular Eclipse Ever Safe to View Without Eclipse Glasses?

No.

During an annular eclipse, part of the Sun's bright surface remains visible at all times, including during the ring-of-fire phase.

There is no totality period.

Proper solar viewing protection must therefore be used throughout an annular solar eclipse when looking directly at the Sun.

Can You Use Sunglasses to Watch an Eclipse?

No.

Regular sunglasses transmit far too much sunlight for direct solar observation.

This remains true even if the sunglasses are very dark or several pairs are worn together.

Solar eclipse viewers are designed specifically to reduce intense visible light and other solar radiation to levels suitable for direct solar viewing.

What About Cameras, Binoculars, and Telescopes?

Cameras, binoculars, and telescopes require special precautions because their lenses concentrate sunlight.

Do not look through an unfiltered camera, telescope, binoculars, or similar optical device while wearing eclipse glasses. Concentrated sunlight can damage the solar viewer and seriously injure the eyes.

Optical equipment used for solar observation requires an appropriate solar filter designed for that equipment and mounted securely over the front of the optics, before sunlight enters the device.

Solar filters intended for telescopes, binoculars, and camera lenses are different products from ordinary wearable eclipse glasses.

Finder scopes attached to telescopes must also be fitted with an appropriate front-mounted solar filter or securely capped and not used. An unfiltered finder scope can concentrate sunlight and cause serious eye injury or damage to the instrument, including components such as crosshairs.

Can Looking at the Sun Damage Your Eyes?

Yes.

Looking directly at the Sun without proper protection can damage the light-sensitive retina at the back of the eye, a condition commonly called solar retinopathy.

Retinal injury can occur without immediate pain because the retina does not contain pain receptors that would warn someone to look away because damage is occurring.

Symptoms can include blurred or distorted vision, blind or dark spots, altered color vision, and reduced central vision.

The injury can be temporary or permanent depending on its severity.

Is the Sun More Dangerous During an Eclipse?

The eclipse does not cause the Sun to emit a special form of dangerous radiation.

The danger comes from looking directly at the Sun without appropriate protection.

Solar eclipses increase the risk because people who would normally never stare at the Sun may deliberately watch it for extended periods.

Even when most of the Sun is covered by the Moon, the remaining visible portion can still be bright enough to damage the eyes.

What Is the Difference Between a Solar and Lunar Eclipse?

A solar eclipse and lunar eclipse involve different alignments.

During a solar eclipse:

Sun → Moon → Earth

The Moon blocks the Sun from Earth's perspective. Solar eclipses occur around new moon.

During a lunar eclipse:

Sun → Earth → Moon

Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. Lunar eclipses occur around full moon.

Another major difference is visibility. A total solar eclipse can be seen only from a relatively narrow path on Earth, while a lunar eclipse can generally be seen from much of the nighttime side of Earth.

Do Animals React to Solar Eclipses?

Some animals can respond to the sudden changes in light and temperature during a total solar eclipse.

Behaviors associated with evening or nighttime may temporarily appear as the sky darkens, and some daytime animals may become less active.

Responses vary widely among species and individual animals, and scientists continue to study animal behavior during eclipses.

Are Solar Eclipses Dangerous During Pregnancy?

There is no scientific evidence that a solar eclipse itself creates a special danger to pregnant people or unborn children.

Traditional beliefs in some cultures have associated eclipses with pregnancy risks, but the astronomical event does not produce a known biological hazard specific to pregnancy.

The ordinary solar-viewing safety rules apply to everyone: directly viewing the Sun without proper protection can injure the eyes.

Can Scientists Predict Solar Eclipses?

Yes.

The motions of Earth and the Moon are predictable enough that astronomers can calculate solar eclipses far in advance.

Scientists can determine when an eclipse will occur, what type it will be, where its shadow will travel across Earth, when the eclipse will begin and end at particular locations, and how long its phases will last.

Eclipses also follow longer repeating patterns. One well-known pattern is the Saros cycle, in which similar eclipses recur after approximately 18 years and 11 days, although they occur in different locations on Earth.

Common Misunderstandings

A Solar Eclipse Happens Every New Moon

No.

The Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's orbital plane, so its shadow normally passes above or below Earth. An eclipse requires a new moon close to an orbital node.

Everyone on Earth Sees the Same Eclipse

No.

What someone sees depends on location. Some people may experience totality, others a partial eclipse, and much of Earth may see no solar eclipse at all.

A 99 Percent Eclipse Is Basically the Same as Totality

No.

Even a very deep partial eclipse is fundamentally different from totality. The Sun's bright surface remains visible, eye protection is still required, and the corona does not become visible in the same way.

Regular Sunglasses Are Enough

No.

Ordinary sunglasses are not designed for direct observation of the Sun and do not provide sufficient protection.

You Can Never Look at a Solar Eclipse Without Glasses

Not exactly.

During the brief total phase of a total solar eclipse, observers inside the path of totality may look directly at the completely covered Sun. Eye protection is required before and immediately after totality.

The Ring of Fire Is Safe to Look At

No.

An annular eclipse always leaves part of the bright Sun exposed. Proper solar viewing protection is required throughout the event.

An Eclipse Produces Special Harmful Radiation

No.

The Sun does not suddenly produce a new form of radiation during an eclipse. The danger comes from directly viewing the normally intense Sun without proper protection.

Solar Eclipses Are Dangerous to Pregnant People

There is no scientific evidence that an eclipse creates a special pregnancy-related biological danger.

Why Solar Eclipses Matter

Solar eclipses provide a striking demonstration of the motions and geometry of the Sun, Moon, and Earth.

Total eclipses have also historically given scientists opportunities to study the Sun's corona and other phenomena that are difficult to observe against the brightness of the Sun's visible surface.

Modern instruments can study the corona from space and with specialized equipment at other times, but total solar eclipses remain valuable for scientific observations, education, and public interest in astronomy.

They also provide a clear example of how apparent size, orbital motion, distance, and shadow geometry combine to create an event that can look dramatically different from locations only a relatively short distance apart.

Conclusion

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun and its shadow falls on part of Earth.

Depending on the alignment and the Moon's apparent size, observers may experience a total, partial, annular, or hybrid eclipse. Solar eclipses do not occur every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's orbital plane, so the required alignment happens only near the Moon's orbital nodes.

A total solar eclipse allows observers inside the path of totality to see the Sun's corona during the brief period when the bright solar surface is completely covered. At all other times—and throughout partial and annular eclipses—proper solar viewing protection is required when looking directly at the Sun.

Understanding the geometry behind an eclipse explains both why these events are relatively uncommon from any one location and why a few miles can make the difference between seeing a partial eclipse and experiencing totality.
