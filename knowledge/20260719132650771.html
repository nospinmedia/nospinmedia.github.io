How Does the FDA Approve New Drugs?

July 19, 20265 min read

How Does the FDA Approve New Drugs?

Before a new prescription drug can be sold in the United States, it must go through a lengthy review process designed to evaluate its safety, effectiveness, and manufacturing quality. This process is overseen by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which reviews scientific evidence before deciding whether a drug's benefits outweigh its known risks.

Developing a new medicine often takes many years and involves thousands of researchers, healthcare professionals, and study participants. While not every drug receives FDA approval, the review process is intended to ensure that approved medications meet established standards before they become available to the public.

Where Does Drug Development Begin?

The process starts long before the FDA reviews a drug application.

Scientists first identify a disease or medical condition they hope to treat. Researchers then study potential compounds in laboratories and conduct experiments using cells and animals to better understand how the drug works and whether it appears reasonably safe to test in people.

This stage, known as preclinical research, can take several years. Many potential drugs never move beyond this point because they fail to show enough promise or raise safety concerns.

What Happens Before Human Testing?

If preclinical research produces encouraging results, the drug's developer submits an Investigational New Drug (IND) application to the FDA.

The FDA reviews the available laboratory and animal data, manufacturing information, and the proposed clinical trial plans. If the agency determines that the planned studies can be conducted safely, researchers may begin testing the drug in human volunteers.

What Are Clinical Trials?

Clinical trials are carefully designed studies that evaluate how a drug performs in people. They are generally conducted in three main phases before approval.

Phase 1

A small group of healthy volunteers or patients receives the drug to evaluate:

  • Basic safety
  • Side effects
  • Appropriate dosage ranges
  • How the body absorbs and processes the medication

Phase 2

The drug is given to a larger group of people who have the condition it is intended to treat. Researchers study:

  • Whether the drug appears to work
  • Short-term safety
  • Common side effects
  • Optimal dosing

Phase 3

Thousands of patients may participate in Phase 3 trials. These studies compare the new treatment with existing therapies or a placebo, depending on the condition being studied.

Researchers collect extensive information about:

  • Effectiveness
  • Less common side effects
  • Overall benefits and risks
  • Appropriate patient populations

Not every drug successfully completes clinical trials. Some are discontinued because they are ineffective, while others are stopped because of safety concerns.

How Does the FDA Decide?

After successful clinical trials, the manufacturer submits a New Drug Application (NDA) to the FDA.

The application often contains hundreds of thousands of pages of scientific data, including clinical trial results, manufacturing information, proposed prescribing instructions, and plans for monitoring safety after approval.

Teams of physicians, pharmacists, statisticians, chemists, toxicologists, and other experts review the evidence. Their goal is to determine whether the drug's demonstrated benefits outweigh its known risks when used as directed.

The FDA may also inspect manufacturing facilities to verify that the drug can be produced consistently and meets federal quality standards.

If the evidence supports approval, the FDA authorizes the drug for specific medical uses described on its official label.

Does FDA Approval Mean a Drug Is Perfectly Safe?

No.

Every medication carries some level of risk. FDA approval means that, based on the available scientific evidence, the expected benefits outweigh the known risks for the approved use.

Doctors and patients still consider factors such as age, medical history, other medications, allergies, and potential side effects when deciding whether a treatment is appropriate.

What Happens After Approval?

FDA oversight continues after a drug reaches the market.

Manufacturers are required to monitor safety and report significant adverse events. Healthcare providers, patients, and manufacturers can all report suspected side effects through the FDA's MedWatch safety reporting system.

If new safety concerns emerge, the FDA may:

  • Update warning labels.
  • Require additional studies.
  • Restrict how the drug is prescribed.
  • Approve new uses based on additional evidence.
  • Remove the drug from the market if the risks outweigh the benefits.

Because millions of people may eventually use an approved medication, rare side effects sometimes become apparent only after widespread use.

Can the FDA Speed Up Drug Approval?

Yes. For medications that treat serious or life-threatening conditions, the FDA offers several programs designed to speed development and review when appropriate.

These include:

  • Fast Track designation
  • Breakthrough Therapy designation
  • Priority Review
  • Accelerated Approval

These programs do not eliminate scientific review. Instead, they are intended to help promising treatments reach patients more quickly while maintaining FDA standards for evaluating safety and effectiveness.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that the FDA develops new drugs. In reality, pharmaceutical companies, biotechnology firms, universities, and research organizations develop medications. The FDA's role is to review the evidence and regulate whether those drugs can be marketed.

Another misunderstanding is that every approved drug works for every patient. Individual responses can vary based on genetics, underlying health conditions, and other medications.

Finally, FDA approval does not end scientific research. New studies often continue for years, leading to additional safety information, expanded uses, or changes to prescribing recommendations.

Conclusion

The FDA drug approval process is designed to balance timely access to new treatments with careful scientific review. Through laboratory research, clinical trials, independent evaluation, and ongoing safety monitoring, the agency works to ensure that new medications meet established standards before they become widely available. While no medicine is completely free of risk, FDA approval indicates that the available evidence supports its use for specific medical conditions when prescribed and used appropriately.
