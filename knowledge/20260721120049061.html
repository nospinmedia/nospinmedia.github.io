How Does the U.S. Immigration System Work?

The U.S. immigration system is the collection of federal laws, regulations, and government agencies that determine who may enter, live, work, study, visit, seek protection, or become a permanent resident or citizen of the United States.

Congress establishes most immigration laws, while federal agencies administer and enforce those laws under policies adopted by the executive branch.

The system includes both legal immigration pathways and procedures for enforcing immigration law when individuals violate federal immigration requirements.

What Is the Difference Between Immigrant and Nonimmigrant Status?

The U.S. immigration system generally divides foreign nationals into two broad categories.

Immigrants are people who have permission to live permanently in the United States, usually through lawful permanent resident status, commonly known as holding a Green Card.

Nonimmigrants are people who enter the United States temporarily for a specific purpose, such as tourism, business, education, temporary employment, or diplomatic assignments.

What Are Common Types of U.S. Visas?

Some of the most common visa categories include:

  • B-1/B-2: Business and tourism.
  • F-1: Academic students.
  • J-1: Exchange visitors.
  • H-1B: Certain specialty occupations.
  • L-1: Intracompany transfers.
  • K-1: Fiancé(e) visas.
  • Immigrant visas: Permanent immigration through family, employment, or other authorized pathways.

Each visa category has its own eligibility requirements, permitted activities, and length of stay.

Who Oversees Immigration?

Several federal agencies share responsibility for administering the immigration system.

  • Department of Homeland Security (DHS) oversees most immigration enforcement and administration.
  • U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) processes immigration benefits such as Green Cards, naturalization, asylum applications, employment authorization, and many visa-related requests.
  • U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) inspects travelers and goods at ports of entry and helps secure U.S. borders.
  • U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) enforces immigration laws within the United States and oversees immigration detention and removal operations.
  • Department of State issues visas through U.S. embassies and consulates around the world.
  • Department of Justice (DOJ) operates the nation's immigration courts through the Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR).

How Does Someone Legally Immigrate to the United States?

People may become lawful permanent residents through several pathways established by federal law.

Common pathways include:

  • Family-sponsored immigration.
  • Employment-based immigration.
  • Refugee or asylum status, if eligible.
  • Diversity Visa Lottery (when available).
  • Certain humanitarian or special immigration programs established by Congress.

Each pathway has its own eligibility rules, documentation requirements, and processing procedures.

What Is a Green Card?

A Green Card is the common name for a Permanent Resident Card.

A lawful permanent resident may generally live and work permanently in the United States while continuing to meet federal immigration laws.

Permanent residency is different from U.S. citizenship, although many permanent residents later become eligible to apply for naturalization.

How Does Someone Become a U.S. Citizen?

Foreign nationals may become U.S. citizens through several legal pathways.

The most common include:

  • Birth in the United States or certain U.S. territories.
  • Birth abroad to qualifying U.S. citizen parents.
  • Naturalization after meeting eligibility requirements established by federal law.

Naturalization generally requires applicants to meet residency requirements, demonstrate knowledge of U.S. civics and English (with certain exceptions), and satisfy other legal qualifications.

What Happens When Someone Violates Immigration Law?

Federal immigration law provides procedures for investigating alleged violations and, when appropriate, initiating removal proceedings.

Depending on the circumstances, a person may:

  • Receive a notice to appear before an immigration judge.
  • Request certain forms of legal relief if eligible.
  • Appeal certain immigration decisions.
  • Be ordered removed from the United States.

The procedures available depend on federal law and the individual's immigration status and circumstances.

Why Is Immigration Frequently in the News?

Immigration is regularly discussed in news reports involving:

  • Border security.
  • Deportation and removal proceedings.
  • Visa policies.
  • Refugees and asylum.
  • Employment authorization.
  • Student visas.
  • Immigration court decisions.
  • Executive orders.
  • Congressional immigration legislation.

Changes to immigration policy can affect millions of people, employers, schools, state governments, and international travelers.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that immigration laws are administered by a single agency. In reality, multiple federal departments share responsibility for visas, border inspections, immigration benefits, law enforcement, and immigration courts.

Another misunderstanding is that holding a visa guarantees entry into the United States. In reality, a visa generally allows a person to travel to a U.S. port of entry, where a CBP officer determines whether the traveler may be admitted.

Finally, lawful permanent residence and U.S. citizenship are not the same. Permanent residents have many legal rights, but only U.S. citizens receive the full rights and responsibilities of citizenship.

Conclusion

The U.S. immigration system is a complex framework of federal laws, agencies, and legal processes that governs who may enter, live, work, study, and become a citizen of the United States. Because immigration policies affect national security, the economy, education, families, and international travel, they are frequently the subject of news reports and public debate.
