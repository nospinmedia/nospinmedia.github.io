What Is Cloture?

August 28, 202612 min read

What Is Cloture?

Cloture is the U.S. Senate procedure used to limit debate and move a matter toward a vote.

It is most commonly discussed in connection with the filibuster. Senate rules allow extended debate on many matters, so supporters of legislation may need to invoke cloture before they can bring debate to an end and move toward final passage.

For most legislation, cloture currently requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn. With all 100 Senate seats filled, that normally means 60 votes.

A cloture vote is not normally the same thing as a vote to pass the bill. That distinction explains why a measure may need 60 votes at one stage of Senate consideration but later pass with a simple majority.

Why Does the Senate Have Cloture?

For much of its early history, the Senate had no general procedure that allowed a majority to force an end to debate.

Senators could therefore use extended debate and other procedural tactics to delay or block action. As filibusters became more disruptive, pressure grew for a formal way to bring debate to a close.

In 1917, the Senate adopted Rule XXII, creating the cloture procedure. The original rule generally required a two-thirds majority to limit debate.

In 1975, the Senate changed the threshold for most matters to three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn, which ordinarily means 60 votes in today's 100-member Senate.

What Is the Difference Between Cloture and a Filibuster?

A filibuster is a tactic or procedural situation that delays or prevents Senate action by allowing debate or other proceedings to continue.

Cloture is the procedure the Senate can use to limit that debate and move the matter forward.

The two terms are therefore closely related but do not mean the same thing.

A filibuster creates or maintains the obstacle. Cloture is one of the Senate's main tools for overcoming it.

How Is a Cloture Motion Filed?

Under Senate Rule XXII, a cloture motion must be signed by at least 16 senators.

The motion asks the Senate to bring debate on the pending matter to a close.

Once the motion is filed, Senate rules normally require a waiting period before the cloture vote occurs. In practice, Senate leaders frequently use unanimous-consent agreements to adjust the timing of votes and other procedural steps.

The filing of a cloture motion does not mean cloture has been invoked. The Senate still must vote on whether to approve it.

How Many Votes Are Needed for Cloture?

For most legislation, Rule XXII requires the votes of three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn.

If all 100 Senate seats are filled, that normally means 60 votes.

The rule does not simply say "60 senators." Because the threshold is based on the number of senators duly chosen and sworn, vacancies can affect the exact number required.

For example, if fewer than 100 Senate seats are occupied, three-fifths of the senators duly chosen and sworn may produce a cloture threshold below 60.

Is a Cloture Vote the Same as a Vote to Pass a Bill?

No.

A cloture vote generally determines whether the Senate will limit further debate and move the matter toward a final decision.

The final passage vote determines whether the Senate approves the legislation itself.

Most ordinary bills can pass on a simple-majority vote once the Senate reaches final passage.

For example, a bill might have 55 senators who support it. If opponents are able to continue debate and supporters cannot obtain the votes required for cloture, the bill may not reach a final passage vote even though a majority of senators would vote for it.

If cloture is invoked with 60 votes, the Senate may later pass the bill 55-45.

That is why saying that every Senate bill "needs 60 votes to pass" is usually imprecise. The 60-vote hurdle typically concerns ending debate rather than final passage.

What Happens If Cloture Fails?

If the Senate does not obtain the required votes for cloture, debate is not limited through that cloture motion.

That does not necessarily mean the underlying bill or nomination has been formally defeated.

Senate leaders may continue negotiations, change strategy, schedule another cloture attempt, modify the legislation, or move to other business.

In practical terms, however, failure to invoke cloture can prevent a measure from advancing when opponents remain able to block further action.

What Happens If Cloture Passes?

If cloture is invoked, Senate debate becomes subject to limits established by Senate rules and applicable precedents.

Cloture does not always produce an immediate final vote.

The Senate may still have a period of post-cloture consideration during which permitted debate, amendments, and procedural actions can occur. The amount of time available depends on the type of matter and the rules or precedents governing it.

Senators can also agree unanimously to shorten the remaining time and vote sooner.

Eventually, after the permitted post-cloture proceedings are completed or yielded back, the Senate can move toward a vote on the matter itself.

Why Can a Bill Pass With Fewer Than 60 Votes After Cloture?

Because cloture and final passage answer different questions.

The cloture vote asks whether the Senate should limit debate.

The final vote asks whether the Senate should approve the legislation.

Suppose 60 senators vote to invoke cloture because they believe the Senate should be allowed to vote on a bill. Some of those senators may still oppose the bill itself.

The final passage vote could therefore be 52-48, 51-49, or another simple-majority result even though cloture required 60 votes earlier in the process.

Likewise, a senator may oppose cloture for procedural reasons while supporting some or all of the underlying legislation.

A cloture vote should therefore not automatically be interpreted as a vote for or against the substance of a bill.

Does Cloture Always Require 60 Votes?

No.

The 60-vote threshold is the familiar rule for most legislation when all 100 Senate seats are filled, but Senate procedure contains important exceptions.

Presidential nominations operate differently under current Senate precedents, and Rule XXII provides a different threshold for cloture on proposals to amend the Senate's standing rules.

Some legislation also proceeds under special statutes or procedures that limit debate without requiring ordinary Rule XXII cloture.

How Does Cloture Work for Presidential Nominations?

Under current Senate precedent, cloture on presidential nominations can generally be invoked by a simple majority.

This resulted from two major procedural changes.

In 2013, the Senate established a precedent allowing a simple majority to invoke cloture on most presidential nominations, including lower federal court nominees.

In 2017, the Senate extended the simple-majority cloture precedent to Supreme Court nominations.

As a result, the ordinary 60-vote cloture threshold that applies to most legislation does not currently apply to presidential nominations.

What About Changes to Senate Rules?

Rule XXII establishes a higher cloture threshold for a measure or motion to amend the Senate's standing rules.

For those matters, cloture generally requires two-thirds of senators present and voting.

This is different from the three-fifths threshold that normally applies to legislation.

The distinction also helps explain the importance of the Senate procedure commonly called the nuclear option. Rather than formally rewriting a standing rule through the normal rule-change process, a Senate majority can in certain circumstances establish a new procedural precedent through rulings and votes of the chamber.

That process was used for the nomination cloture changes in 2013 and 2017.

Can the Vice President Provide the 60th Cloture Vote?

Not in the ordinary sense of breaking a simple-majority tie.

The vice president's constitutional role allows a tie-breaking vote when the Senate is equally divided on a question decided by majority vote.

But Rule XXII's ordinary legislative cloture threshold is based on three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn.

If 59 senators support cloture when 60 are required, the Senate has not reached that three-fifths threshold. The vice president's ordinary tie-breaking authority does not simply convert 59 senatorial votes into the required 60.

The vice president may still cast a decisive vote on final passage if a bill later reaches a 50-50 vote requiring only a simple majority.

Is There Always a Cloture Vote Before a Senate Vote?

No.

The Senate conducts much of its work through unanimous consent.

Senators may agree to limit debate, schedule votes, waive procedural steps, or otherwise allow a matter to proceed without requiring a formal cloture process.

If nobody objects, the Senate can often move efficiently toward a vote.

Cloture becomes especially important when senators object to ending debate or when leaders need a formal procedural mechanism to overcome delay.

This is why many bills and nominations reach votes without a visible cloture battle.

Can Cloture Be Filed on More Than Just a Bill?

Yes.

Cloture can be used on several types of Senate business subject to Rule XXII and Senate precedent.

For example, cloture may be sought on:

  • Legislation.
  • A motion to proceed to consideration of legislation.
  • Amendments in appropriate circumstances.
  • Presidential nominations.
  • Other debatable Senate matters.

The applicable vote threshold and post-cloture procedures can differ depending on what the Senate is considering.

Why Does the Motion to Proceed Matter?

Before the Senate can debate and vote on many bills, it must first agree to take up the legislation.

The motion to proceed can itself be debatable and therefore potentially subject to a filibuster.

Senate leaders sometimes must obtain cloture simply to get the Senate onto the bill before the chamber even begins considering the legislation itself.

A second cloture process may later be required to end debate on the bill.

This helps explain why major legislation can generate multiple cloture votes before a final passage vote occurs.

What Does "Cloture Invoked" Mean in a News Report?

When a news report says the Senate invoked cloture, it means the Senate obtained the required votes to limit debate on the particular matter under consideration.

It does not necessarily mean:

  • The bill passed.
  • The nomination was confirmed.
  • Every senator who voted for cloture supports the underlying measure.
  • A final vote will occur immediately.

It means the Senate successfully used its procedure for bringing debate toward a close.

What Does "Cloture Not Invoked" Mean?

It means the Senate did not obtain the number of votes required to limit debate through that cloture motion.

For example, if ordinary legislation receives a 55-45 cloture vote when 60 votes are required, cloture fails even though more senators voted yes than no.

That result can look unusual because most democratic votes are decided by a simple majority. Cloture is different because Senate Rule XXII establishes a supermajority requirement for most legislation.

The underlying bill has not necessarily been defeated on a final vote. Instead, supporters failed to overcome the procedural obstacle to ending debate.

Common Misunderstandings

A Cloture Vote Is the Vote to Pass the Bill

No.

Cloture generally determines whether debate will be limited. Final passage is a separate vote on whether to approve the legislation.

If Cloture Fails, the Bill Was Voted Down

Not necessarily.

Failure to invoke cloture means the Senate did not end debate through that procedure. The underlying legislation may remain pending or could be considered again later.

Cloture Always Requires 60 Votes

No.

Three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn is the normal threshold for most legislation. Nominations generally operate under a simple-majority precedent, while proposals to change Senate rules have a different cloture threshold.

The Rule Permanently Says 60 Senators

No.

Rule XXII uses the formula three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn for most matters. Sixty is the normal result when all 100 Senate seats are filled.

Once Cloture Passes, the Senate Immediately Votes on the Bill

Not necessarily.

Post-cloture debate and other permitted proceedings may still occur before the final vote.

Cloture and Filibuster Mean the Same Thing

No.

A filibuster delays or blocks Senate action. Cloture is a procedure used to limit debate and overcome that obstruction.

Voting for Cloture Means Supporting the Bill

Not necessarily.

A senator may believe debate should end and the Senate should vote while still intending to vote against final passage.

The Vice President Can Supply the Missing 60th Vote

No.

The ordinary legislative cloture threshold is a three-fifths requirement based on senators duly chosen and sworn, not a simple-majority tie that the vice president can resolve.

Why Cloture Matters

Cloture is one of the most important procedures for understanding how the modern Senate works.

A bill may have support from a majority of senators but still be unable to advance because supporters cannot obtain enough votes to end debate. Conversely, senators may vote for cloture without supporting the legislation itself because they believe the chamber should reach a final vote.

This means Senate vote totals can be misleading if the type of vote is not identified.

When a headline says a proposal failed 55-45, the most useful question is: Was that the final vote on the bill, or was it a cloture vote requiring a larger majority?

That distinction can completely change what the result means.

Conclusion

Cloture is the Senate procedure used to limit debate and move a matter toward a vote. It was created in 1917 through Senate Rule XXII and later modified so that cloture on most legislation requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn—normally 60 votes in a full 100-member Senate.

Cloture is not the same as final passage and is not synonymous with a filibuster. A filibuster delays or blocks action, while cloture is the Senate's principal procedure for overcoming extended debate.

Current Senate precedents allow cloture on presidential nominations, including Supreme Court nominations, by simple majority, while different rules apply to formal changes to Senate rules and certain specially protected legislative procedures.

Understanding cloture explains one of the most confusing features of Senate coverage: a proposal can have majority support and still fail to advance because the Senate did not obtain enough votes to end debate.
