How Does a Carbon Monoxide Detector Work?

September 25, 202611 min read

A carbon monoxide detector works by sensing carbon monoxide gas in the air and sounding an alarm when exposure reaches potentially dangerous levels. Unlike a smoke alarm, which detects particles produced by combustion, a carbon monoxide alarm is designed to detect the gas itself.

Carbon monoxide, often abbreviated CO, is especially dangerous because it is colorless, odorless and tasteless. People cannot reliably detect it with their senses. A properly functioning carbon monoxide alarm provides an automatic warning before exposure becomes severe enough to cause serious illness or death.

What Is Carbon Monoxide?

Carbon monoxide is a gas made of one carbon atom and one oxygen atom. It is produced when carbon-containing fuels do not burn completely.

Potential sources include furnaces, boilers, fireplaces, wood stoves, gas appliances, portable generators and vehicle engines. Any fuel-burning device can potentially produce carbon monoxide if combustion is incomplete or exhaust gases are not properly vented.

Carbon monoxide becomes dangerous when it accumulates in occupied spaces.

Why Is Carbon Monoxide Dangerous?

When carbon monoxide is inhaled, it enters the bloodstream through the lungs.

It binds strongly to hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells responsible for carrying oxygen. This forms carboxyhemoglobin and reduces the blood's ability to transport and release oxygen effectively to tissues.

Carbon monoxide can also interfere with other biological processes involving oxygen use.

Exposure can cause symptoms such as headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, confusion and chest discomfort. Severe exposure can lead to loss of consciousness, permanent injury or death.

Because early symptoms can resemble illnesses such as influenza, carbon monoxide poisoning may not be immediately recognized.

How Does a Carbon Monoxide Alarm Detect the Gas?

Several sensing technologies have been used in carbon monoxide alarms, but electrochemical sensors are widely used in modern residential devices.

An electrochemical sensor contains electrodes and an electrolyte. Carbon monoxide enters the sensor through a gas-permeable opening or membrane and participates in a chemical reaction at one of the electrodes.

That reaction produces an electrical signal related to the amount of carbon monoxide reaching the sensor.

Electronic circuitry monitors the signal and estimates the concentration of carbon monoxide in the surrounding air.

If the measured exposure meets the alarm's programmed criteria, the device sounds its warning.

What Happens Inside an Electrochemical CO Sensor?

The details vary among sensor designs, but the basic principle involves converting a chemical reaction into a measurable electrical current.

Carbon monoxide reaching the sensing electrode is oxidized through an electrochemical reaction. Electrons produced by the reaction travel through the sensor's electrical circuit while ions move through the electrolyte to complete the electrochemical process.

The resulting current is related to the amount of carbon monoxide reacting at the electrode.

Because the signal is very small, electronic circuits amplify and process it before deciding whether alarm conditions have been reached.

This allows the detector to continuously monitor CO concentrations without relying on smell, visible smoke or changes in temperature.

Does a CO Alarm Sound as Soon as It Detects Any Carbon Monoxide?

Usually not.

Small amounts of carbon monoxide can exist temporarily without representing the same immediate danger as a high concentration. Carbon monoxide risk depends on both concentration and duration of exposure.

Residential alarms are therefore designed to respond according to established alarm thresholds and time requirements rather than necessarily sounding the instant any detectable CO is present.

At higher concentrations, the permitted time before an alarm sounds becomes much shorter. At lower concentrations, an alarm may take longer to activate or may not activate if the level remains below its designed alarm criteria.

This behavior is intentional. It helps the device provide warnings for hazardous exposures while reducing unnecessary alarms from brief or very low CO levels.

What Does PPM Mean?

Carbon monoxide concentration is commonly measured in parts per million, abbreviated ppm.

One ppm means approximately one molecule of carbon monoxide for every million molecules of air, when describing the gases by volume under the same conditions.

A reading of 100 ppm therefore represents a higher CO concentration than a reading of 20 ppm.

Some carbon monoxide alarms include digital displays that show measured concentrations. The way these displays handle very low readings varies among models, so a display showing zero does not necessarily mean the sensor has never encountered any carbon monoxide.

Why Does Exposure Time Matter?

The effects of carbon monoxide depend on how much CO is present and how long a person breathes it.

A relatively high concentration can become dangerous quickly. A lower concentration may require a longer exposure before producing comparable levels of carbon monoxide in the bloodstream.

For this reason, carbon monoxide alarms use time-and-concentration relationships when determining when to sound.

The alarm is therefore not simply functioning like an on-off switch tied to one universal ppm number.

Why Doesn't a CO Detector Work Like a Smoke Detector?

Smoke and carbon monoxide are physically different hazards.

Smoke consists of airborne particles, droplets and gases produced during combustion. Traditional smoke alarms detect particles using photoelectric or ionization sensing methods.

Carbon monoxide is an individual gas molecule. It does not create a visible cloud and cannot be reliably detected by the optical system used in an ordinary photoelectric smoke alarm.

A CO alarm therefore requires a sensor designed specifically to respond to carbon monoxide.

Combination smoke and carbon monoxide alarms contain separate sensing capabilities for the two hazards, even though they share one housing and warning system.

Can Carbon Monoxide Be Seen or Smelled?

No.

Pure carbon monoxide has no color, smell or taste that people can use as a warning.

A person may sometimes smell exhaust, smoke or other combustion products when carbon monoxide is present, but those odors come from other substances. Carbon monoxide itself remains odorless.

The absence of an unusual smell therefore does not establish that an area is free of carbon monoxide.

This is one of the primary reasons electronic CO alarms are important in buildings containing fuel-burning equipment or attached garages.

What Causes Carbon Monoxide to Build Up Indoors?

Carbon monoxide can accumulate when fuel burns incompletely or combustion exhaust cannot escape properly.

A malfunctioning or poorly vented furnace, boiler, fireplace or other fuel-burning appliance can release CO indoors. Blocked chimneys or vents can prevent combustion gases from leaving a building.

Portable generators, charcoal grills and fuel-burning equipment can produce dangerous amounts of carbon monoxide when operated in enclosed or partially enclosed spaces.

Vehicles running in garages can also create hazardous concentrations, particularly when exhaust gases enter occupied portions of a building.

Carbon monoxide problems are therefore often related to both combustion and ventilation.

Does Carbon Monoxide Rise or Sink?

A common misconception is that carbon monoxide always rises to the ceiling or sinks toward the floor.

Carbon monoxide has a molecular weight close to that of ordinary air. Once released into a room, it tends to mix with the surrounding air through normal air movement, convection and ventilation.

Its distribution is therefore not governed by a simple rule that it always collects exclusively at the top or bottom of a room.

This is why placement recommendations are based primarily on protecting occupied and sleeping areas and following the alarm manufacturer's instructions rather than on a belief that CO always rises or always sinks.

Where Should Carbon Monoxide Alarms Be Installed?

Exact requirements vary by building code, jurisdiction and alarm manufacturer.

CO alarms are commonly recommended outside sleeping areas and on levels of a home where people may be exposed to carbon monoxide. Some codes require additional locations depending on the building and its fuel-burning equipment.

An alarm should not automatically be placed directly beside every combustion appliance. Very specific distances from appliances, vents, windows, bathrooms or other locations may be recommended to reduce nuisance conditions and improve reliable detection.

The manufacturer's installation instructions and applicable local fire and building codes should determine exact placement.

How Are Carbon Monoxide Alarms Powered?

CO alarms may operate from replaceable batteries, sealed long-life batteries, household electrical power or a combination of electrical power and battery backup.

Plug-in models are also common.

The power source does not fundamentally change how an electrochemical sensor detects carbon monoxide. It supplies the electricity needed by the sensor electronics, processor, indicators and audible alarm.

Battery backup can allow some models to continue operating during a power outage, which can be particularly important because outages sometimes lead people to use generators or alternative heating equipment that can create additional CO hazards.

Why Does a Carbon Monoxide Alarm Chirp?

A periodic chirp usually indicates a condition different from an emergency carbon monoxide alarm.

Depending on the model, chirping may indicate a low battery, sensor fault, loss of electrical power or end-of-life condition.

Carbon monoxide alarms typically use specific audible patterns and indicator lights to distinguish an emergency alarm from maintenance warnings.

Because these patterns vary among manufacturers and models, the device's instructions should be checked rather than assuming every chirp means the same thing.

An alarm indicating the end of its service life should be replaced rather than simply given a new battery.

Why Do CO Detectors Need to Be Replaced?

Carbon monoxide sensors do not remain reliable indefinitely.

Electrochemical sensors gradually age as their chemical components change over time. Electronic components can also deteriorate.

Manufacturers therefore specify a service life for each alarm. Depending on the design, modern CO alarms may have useful lives of several years or longer, but there is no single replacement interval that applies to every model.

Many newer alarms include an automatic end-of-life warning.

The manufacture date, replacement date and manufacturer instructions should be checked to determine when a particular unit needs replacement.

What Does the Test Button Actually Test?

Pressing the test button primarily checks the alarm's electronics, power supply and audible warning system according to the device's design.

It should not be assumed that the button creates carbon monoxide or reproduces an actual CO exposure.

Some models perform electronic checks of sensor-related circuitry as part of their self-testing functions, but capabilities vary.

Using an unsafe source of combustion gas to test a CO alarm is not an appropriate substitute for the manufacturer's testing procedure.

Regular testing should be performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Can Other Chemicals Trigger a CO Sensor?

Electrochemical sensors are designed to be selective for carbon monoxide, but no practical chemical sensor is perfectly isolated from every possible environmental influence.

Certain chemicals, extreme environmental conditions or contamination can interfere with some sensors depending on their design.

Manufacturers design alarms to reduce these effects, and modern devices may use additional electronic processing to improve reliability.

This is another reason CO alarms should be installed and maintained according to their instructions rather than exposed to solvents, cleaners, excessive moisture or other conditions for which they were not designed.

What Should You Do If a Carbon Monoxide Alarm Sounds?

A carbon monoxide alarm should be treated as a potentially serious warning rather than assumed to be a malfunction.

People should move to fresh air and follow the emergency instructions supplied with the alarm and guidance from local emergency authorities. Emergency services can evaluate possible exposure and help determine whether dangerous carbon monoxide is present.

Anyone experiencing symptoms consistent with carbon monoxide poisoning should seek appropriate emergency medical assistance.

A building should not simply be assumed safe because the alarm eventually stops sounding. The source of a confirmed or suspected carbon monoxide problem needs to be identified and corrected.

Can a CO Alarm Prevent Carbon Monoxide From Forming?

No.

A carbon monoxide alarm is a warning device, not a prevention system.

It does not repair malfunctioning appliances, improve ventilation or remove carbon monoxide from the air. Its purpose is to alert occupants when potentially hazardous exposure is detected.

Preventing carbon monoxide problems also requires proper installation, inspection and maintenance of fuel-burning appliances, chimneys, vents and other combustion equipment, along with safe use of generators and vehicles.

The Bottom Line

A carbon monoxide detector protects people by continuously monitoring the air for an invisible and odorless toxic gas.

Most modern residential CO alarms use an electrochemical sensor. Carbon monoxide entering the sensor participates in a chemical reaction that produces a small electrical signal. The alarm's electronics use that signal to determine the concentration of carbon monoxide and whether the combination of concentration and exposure time has reached its alarm criteria.

Unlike smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms detect gas molecules rather than smoke particles. They also do not necessarily sound immediately when they encounter a tiny amount of CO because health risk depends on both concentration and duration.

Proper placement, regular testing and replacement at the end of the alarm's service life are essential. The device cannot prevent carbon monoxide from being produced, but it can provide the warning people need when a dangerous gas that cannot be seen, smelled or tasted begins accumulating around them.
