What Is Contempt of Congress?

August 15, 202610 min read

What Is Contempt of Congress?

Contempt of Congress is a legal and congressional enforcement process used when a person refuses to comply with a lawful congressional demand, such as a subpoena requiring testimony or documents.

Congress relies on subpoenas and other investigative tools to conduct oversight, gather information, and support its legislative responsibilities. When someone refuses to comply without a legally valid reason, Congress may use contempt procedures to try to enforce the demand or punish the refusal.

Being held in contempt of Congress does not automatically mean someone has been convicted of a crime. Congress has several different methods for responding to noncompliance, and each works differently.

Why Can Congress Issue Subpoenas?

Congress has broad investigative authority connected to its legislative responsibilities.

Congressional committees may investigate government programs, executive branch actions, private industries, national problems, and other subjects that could inform legislation or oversight.

As part of those investigations, committees may issue subpoenas requiring people to:

  • Appear and testify.
  • Produce documents.
  • Provide records or other information.

The authority to issue a subpoena depends on the rules of the House or Senate and the rules governing the particular committee.

What Can Lead to Contempt of Congress?

Contempt most commonly arises when someone who has been properly subpoenaed:

  • Fails to appear for required testimony.
  • Refuses to provide documents required by the subpoena.
  • Appears but refuses to answer questions that Congress is legally entitled to ask.

Not every disagreement with Congress amounts to contempt.

A witness may raise legal objections, constitutional protections, privileges, or other defenses. Whether those objections justify refusing to comply can become the subject of negotiations, congressional proceedings, litigation, or other legal action.

Is a Congressional Request the Same as a Subpoena?

No.

Congress frequently asks people and agencies to voluntarily provide information before issuing subpoenas.

A request letter generally does not carry the same compulsory legal authority as a subpoena. A subpoena is a formal demand issued under congressional authority and may be enforced through contempt or court proceedings if the recipient does not comply.

This distinction is important because refusing an ordinary congressional request does not automatically create criminal contempt liability.

What Are the Three Types of Congressional Contempt?

Congress has traditionally relied on three main mechanisms for enforcing its investigative demands: criminal contempt, civil enforcement, and inherent contempt.

What Is Criminal Contempt of Congress?

Federal law, 2 U.S.C. § 192, makes certain willful failures to comply with congressional subpoenas a misdemeanor. It applies when a person who has been summoned by Congress willfully fails to appear, refuses to produce required documents, or refuses to answer a pertinent question without sufficient legal justification.

A conviction can carry a fine and imprisonment of up to 12 months.

The process generally involves several stages.

A congressional committee may first vote to recommend contempt. When Congress is in session, the matter is generally reported to the full House or Senate. The chamber may then vote on whether to approve a criminal contempt resolution and certify the matter for referral under federal law.

A congressional contempt vote itself is not a criminal conviction. Criminal punishment requires a separate prosecution through the federal court system.

Does the Justice Department Have to Prosecute a Contempt Referral?

2 U.S.C. § 194 establishes the statutory process for certifying a criminal contempt matter for referral to the appropriate U.S. attorney.

Under that process, when the required findings are reported to the House or Senate, the matter may be certified by the Speaker of the House or the President of the Senate to the appropriate U.S. attorney for presentation to a grand jury.

A referral does not itself guarantee that criminal charges will be filed. The Justice Department has maintained that prosecutors retain traditional prosecutorial discretion over whether and how to pursue criminal cases.

Disputes become particularly complicated when a contempt citation involves an executive branch official acting under a presidential claim of executive privilege. The Justice Department has long taken the position that the criminal contempt statute cannot constitutionally be used in certain circumstances against executive branch officials who are carrying out a presidential assertion of executive privilege.

Those questions can involve difficult separation-of-powers disputes between Congress and the executive branch.

What Is Civil Enforcement?

Instead of seeking criminal punishment, Congress may sometimes ask a federal court to enforce a subpoena.

In a civil enforcement case, Congress seeks a court order requiring the person or agency to comply with the subpoena.

If the court determines that the subpoena is enforceable, it may order compliance. Continued refusal to obey the court's order could lead to additional legal consequences through the judicial system.

Civil enforcement is primarily intended to obtain the information Congress requested rather than punish the witness for past noncompliance.

The procedures and legal authority available for civil enforcement can differ between the House and Senate and may depend on the circumstances of the dispute.

What Is Inherent Contempt?

Inherent contempt is Congress's historically recognized power to enforce its own authority without relying initially on criminal prosecution by the executive branch.

Historically, a chamber of Congress could direct its Sergeant at Arms to take a person into custody and bring that person before the chamber. Congress could then conduct its own contempt proceeding and potentially detain the individual to compel compliance or punish obstruction.

Congress used versions of this power in the 19th and early 20th centuries but has rarely relied on physical detention through inherent contempt in modern times.

The scope of inherent contempt, particularly when used against executive branch officials, remains the subject of significant constitutional disagreement.

Modern Congress generally relies instead on criminal referrals, civil litigation, negotiations, and other enforcement tools.

Can Congress Arrest Someone?

Historically, yes, through its inherent contempt authority.

Congress has asserted the power to direct its Sergeant at Arms to take individuals into custody for contempt of congressional authority.

That does not mean members of Congress themselves act as police officers, nor does it mean that every modern contempt vote results in an arrest.

Physical detention under inherent contempt has become extremely uncommon, and Congress generally uses other enforcement methods today.

Is a Committee Contempt Vote the Same as a Full House or Senate Vote?

No.

A committee may vote to recommend that a person be held in contempt, but that action is generally one step in the process rather than the final action of the entire House or Senate.

For criminal contempt proceedings while Congress is in session, the matter generally must be reported to the full chamber before it can be certified for referral under the federal contempt statute.

This distinction is important because news reports may describe a committee as having "held someone in contempt" even though additional action by the full House or Senate is still required for a criminal contempt referral.

Can Someone Refuse to Answer Congress by Pleading the Fifth?

Sometimes.

The Fifth Amendment protects people from being compelled to give testimony that could incriminate them in a criminal case.

A witness before Congress may invoke the Fifth Amendment when answering a particular question could reasonably create a risk of self-incrimination.

That does not mean a witness can automatically refuse every question simply by mentioning the Fifth Amendment. The privilege must apply to the testimony being sought, and disputes can arise over whether the claim is legally valid.

Congress also has limited authority under federal law to grant immunity in certain circumstances, which can affect a witness's ability to rely on the privilege against self-incrimination.

What About Executive Privilege?

Executive privilege may allow the president or executive branch to withhold certain confidential information from Congress or the courts in particular circumstances.

It is not an automatic shield against every congressional subpoena.

Disputes involving executive privilege often result in negotiations between Congress and the executive branch over what information will be provided and under what conditions. If those negotiations fail, the dispute may lead to contempt proceedings or litigation.

Because both Congress and the president have constitutional responsibilities, courts and government lawyers have treated these conflicts as separation-of-powers disputes rather than simple cases of one side automatically controlling the other.

Is Contempt of Congress the Same as Contempt of Court?

No.

Contempt of court involves disobeying or obstructing a court or violating a judicial order.

Contempt of Congress involves obstructing or refusing to comply with Congress in the exercise of its constitutional investigative and legislative authority.

Both concepts involve enforcement of lawful institutional authority, but they arise from different branches of government and use different procedures.

Can Someone Go to Prison for Criminal Contempt of Congress?

Yes, following a criminal prosecution and conviction.

Under 2 U.S.C. § 192, criminal contempt of Congress is a misdemeanor, and a conviction can result in a fine and imprisonment of up to 12 months.

However, a congressional vote does not itself send someone to jail. The matter must proceed through the criminal justice system before a court can impose criminal punishment.

Common Misunderstandings

Congress Voted for Contempt, So the Person Is a Convicted Criminal

No.

A congressional contempt resolution is not a criminal conviction. A conviction requires a separate prosecution and court proceeding.

The Justice Department Must Prosecute Every Referral

Not necessarily.

The contempt statute provides a certification and referral process, but the executive branch has maintained that prosecutors retain prosecutorial discretion. Additional constitutional disputes can arise when executive privilege is involved.

Ignoring Any Request From Congress Is Contempt

No.

Congress makes many voluntary requests for information. Contempt enforcement generally involves failure to comply with a valid compulsory demand, such as a subpoena, rather than simply declining an ordinary request.

Pleading the Fifth Is Automatically Contempt

No.

The Fifth Amendment can provide a lawful basis for refusing to provide potentially self-incriminating testimony when the privilege properly applies.

Contempt of Congress and Contempt of Court Are the Same

No.

They involve different branches of government and different legal processes.

A Committee Vote Automatically Means the Entire Congress Has Acted

No.

A committee contempt recommendation and an action by the full House or Senate are different stages of the process.

Why Contempt of Congress Matters

Congress depends on access to information to write laws, oversee federal agencies, investigate problems, and evaluate government programs.

Its subpoena and contempt powers help prevent witnesses from simply ignoring lawful congressional investigations.

At the same time, those powers operate within constitutional limits. Witnesses may have legitimate privileges or legal defenses, courts may become involved, and disputes between Congress and the executive branch can raise difficult separation-of-powers questions.

Understanding contempt of Congress therefore requires distinguishing between Congress's decision that someone has failed to comply, the methods Congress can use to enforce compliance, and any later criminal or judicial proceedings.

Conclusion

Contempt of Congress is a process Congress can use when a person refuses to comply with a lawful congressional demand such as a subpoena. Federal law provides a criminal contempt offense under 2 U.S.C. § 192 and a certification and referral process under 2 U.S.C. § 194. Congress may also seek civil enforcement or rely on its historically recognized inherent contempt authority.

A contempt vote is not itself a criminal conviction, and different legal procedures apply depending on the enforcement method Congress chooses. Constitutional protections, privileges, prosecutorial discretion, and separation-of-powers disputes can also affect how a contempt case ultimately proceeds.
