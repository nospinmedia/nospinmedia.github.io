What Causes a Meteor Shower?

September 3, 202614 min read

What Causes a Meteor Shower?

A meteor shower occurs when Earth passes through a stream of dust and small pieces of debris orbiting the Sun. These debris streams are usually associated with comets, although some meteor showers are connected to asteroid-like objects.

When the particles enter Earth's atmosphere at high speed, they interact with the atmosphere and produce brief streaks of light called meteors.

Because Earth encounters many of the same debris streams at approximately the same point in its orbit each year, major meteor showers return on predictable annual schedules.

Where Does the Debris in a Meteor Shower Come From?

Most major meteor showers are associated with comets.

As a comet travels around the Sun, heating can cause frozen material to release gas and carry dust and rocky particles away from the comet. Over repeated trips around the Sun, debris can become distributed along the comet's orbit.

The result can be a long stream of particles traveling around the Sun on an orbit related to that of the parent comet.

If Earth's orbit intersects that debris stream, Earth encounters some of those particles and a meteor shower can occur.

Is Earth Being Hit by the Comet During a Meteor Shower?

No.

Earth normally encounters debris previously released by the parent object, not the comet itself.

The parent comet may be millions of miles away when the meteor shower occurs.

For example, the annual Perseid meteor shower is associated with debris from Comet 109P/Swift-Tuttle. Earth crosses the comet's debris stream each year even though Swift-Tuttle itself takes about 133 years to orbit the Sun.

Do All Meteor Showers Come From Comets?

No.

Comets are responsible for many well-known meteor showers, but there are important exceptions.

The Geminid meteor shower is associated with 3200 Phaethon, an unusual asteroid-like object sometimes described as a possible "rock comet." Phaethon has displayed some comet-like behavior even though it is classified as an asteroid.

Another major example is the Quadrantid meteor shower, which is associated with 2003 EH1, an asteroid-like object that may be the inactive or extinct nucleus of a former comet.

Together, the Geminids and Quadrantids show that major meteor showers are not produced exclusively by currently active icy comets.

Why Do Meteor Showers Happen Around the Same Dates Every Year?

Earth follows a predictable orbit around the Sun.

If a stream of meteoroids crosses Earth's orbit, Earth returns to that intersection at approximately the same time each year.

That is why familiar showers have regular annual seasons. The Perseids occur during the Northern Hemisphere's summer, the Leonids occur in November, and the Geminids occur in December.

The exact peak time and observed activity can vary from year to year because the debris is not distributed perfectly evenly and because the positions of Earth and the debris stream change slightly.

What Is a Meteoroid?

A meteoroid is a relatively small natural object traveling through space.

Meteor-shower meteoroids can be extremely small. Many are comparable in size to grains of sand or small pebbles.

Despite their small size, they can produce visible streaks because they enter Earth's atmosphere at extremely high speeds.

What Is a Meteor?

A meteor is the visible phenomenon produced when a meteoroid enters Earth's atmosphere at high speed.

The bright streak commonly called a shooting star or falling star is a meteor.

It is not actually a star.

Most visible meteors occur high in Earth's atmosphere and last only a fraction of a second or a few seconds.

What Is a Meteorite?

A meteorite is material from a meteoroid that survives its passage through the atmosphere and reaches the ground.

The three related terms therefore describe different stages or phenomena:

  • Meteoroid: The object while it is in space.
  • Meteor: The visible atmospheric phenomenon produced during entry.
  • Meteorite: Material that survives and reaches Earth's surface.

Not every meteor produces a meteorite.

Do Meteor Showers Usually Produce Meteorites?

Generally, no.

Most particles in typical meteor showers are small and are destroyed high in the atmosphere.

Meteorites found on Earth are more commonly associated with larger and more durable meteoroids rather than the tiny particles responsible for most annual meteor showers.

This means watching a meteor shower does not normally mean pieces of the shower are about to begin falling onto the ground around observers.

Why Does a Meteor Glow?

A common explanation is that meteors glow simply because of friction with the atmosphere, but the actual process is more complicated.

A meteoroid enters the atmosphere at tremendous speed. Its motion strongly compresses and heats the air in front of and around it. Collisions involving atmospheric molecules and material from the meteoroid produce excited and ionized atoms and molecules.

The meteoroid also loses material through a process called ablation as its surface is heated and eroded.

The glowing atmospheric gas and meteoroid material create the bright trail that observers see as a meteor.

Because the entry speeds are so high, even very small particles can create easily visible meteors.

What Is the Radiant of a Meteor Shower?

During a meteor shower, the meteors appear to originate from a particular region of the sky called the radiant.

The particles are actually entering Earth's atmosphere along roughly parallel paths. Perspective makes those paths appear to diverge from a single point in the sky.

A similar perspective effect makes parallel railroad tracks appear to converge toward a point in the distance.

The radiant therefore does not mean that meteors are physically being launched from a star or constellation.

How Are Meteor Showers Named?

Meteor showers are commonly named for the constellation, or a nearby star or region of the sky, where their radiant appears.

For example:

  • Perseids appear to radiate from the constellation Perseus.
  • Geminids appear to radiate from Gemini.
  • Leonids appear to radiate from Leo.

The stars in those constellations have nothing to do with producing the meteors. The constellations simply provide convenient reference points for identifying the apparent direction from which the meteors arrive.

Do You Have to Look Directly at the Radiant?

No.

Meteors belonging to a shower can appear across large portions of the sky.

In fact, staring directly at the radiant is not necessarily the best way to watch a meteor shower. Meteors farther from the radiant can produce longer visible trails across the sky.

A broad, unobstructed view of the sky is generally more useful than concentrating on one small area.

Why Are Some Meteor Showers Better Than Others?

Meteor-shower activity depends on several factors.

These include:

  • How dense the debris stream is.
  • Where Earth passes through the stream.
  • The size and distribution of its particles.
  • The altitude of the radiant in the observer's sky.
  • The observer's latitude.
  • Cloud cover.
  • Light pollution.
  • Moonlight.
  • The exact time of the shower's peak.

A strong astronomical shower can therefore produce disappointing viewing conditions if the sky is cloudy or bright with moonlight.

What Is the Zenithal Hourly Rate?

Meteor-shower forecasts often include a number called the zenithal hourly rate, or ZHR.

The ZHR is a standardized estimate of how many meteors an observer could see per hour under ideal conditions, including a dark sky and the shower's radiant positioned at the zenith, or directly overhead.

It is not a guarantee of how many meteors an individual observer will actually see.

For example, a shower with a ZHR of 100 does not mean every observer will see 100 meteors during an hour.

Moonlight, city lights, clouds, the altitude of the radiant, and other conditions can reduce the actual observed rate substantially.

What Is a Meteor Shower Peak?

A meteor shower can remain active for days or even weeks, but activity usually increases and decreases as Earth moves through different portions of the debris stream.

The peak is the period when meteor activity is expected to be greatest.

Some showers have relatively broad peaks, while others can rise and fall quickly.

The predicted peak also represents Earth's encounter with a particular region of the debris stream, so observers in different parts of the world may have different viewing conditions when that peak occurs.

What Is a Fireball?

A fireball is an exceptionally bright meteor.

Fireballs are produced by larger particles or by objects whose atmospheric entry produces unusually bright light.

Some meteor showers are particularly well known for producing fireballs.

A very bright meteor may also be described as a bolide, although definitions and usage of that term vary among scientific organizations and observers.

What Is a Meteor Storm?

A meteor storm is an unusually intense meteor shower in which meteor rates become dramatically higher than normal.

Meteor storms can occur when Earth encounters an especially dense concentration of debris within a meteoroid stream.

The Leonids are famous for producing historic meteor storms. During exceptional Leonid events, observers have reported enormous numbers of meteors compared with the rates seen during an ordinary annual shower.

Meteor storms are much less common than ordinary meteor showers and cannot be expected every year.

Are Meteor Showers Dangerous?

Ordinary meteor showers pose extremely little danger to people on the ground.

Most shower particles are tiny and are destroyed high in Earth's atmosphere.

Earth encounters small pieces of interplanetary material continuously, including particles unrelated to recognized meteor showers.

The dramatic appearance of dozens of meteors in the sky does not mean dozens of large objects are approaching the ground.

What Is a Sporadic Meteor?

Not every meteor belongs to a recognized meteor shower.

A sporadic meteor is a meteor that is not associated with a particular identified shower.

Small particles are distributed throughout the solar system, and Earth encounters them throughout the year.

This is why someone can occasionally see a shooting star even when no major meteor shower is active.

During a shower, observers may see both shower meteors and unrelated sporadic meteors during the same night.

When Is the Best Time to Watch a Meteor Shower?

The best viewing time depends on the particular shower.

Many major meteor showers are most productive during the hours after midnight and before dawn because Earth's rotation turns the observer's location toward the direction in which Earth is moving through space, allowing that side of Earth to encounter more particles directly.

However, this is not universal. Some showers or particular types of meteors can be well observed earlier in the evening.

For a specific meteor shower, the predicted peak time and radiant position provide the best guidance.

How Do You Watch a Meteor Shower?

Meteor showers generally require no special equipment.

For the best chance of seeing meteors:

  • Find a location with a wide view of the sky.
  • Move away from bright city lights when practical.
  • Check the weather forecast and cloud cover.
  • Consider how bright the Moon will be.
  • Allow roughly 20 to 30 minutes for your eyes to adapt to darkness.
  • Avoid repeatedly looking at bright phone screens or other lights.
  • Give yourself plenty of observing time rather than expecting meteors to appear continuously.

Meteor activity can come in bursts separated by quiet periods, so patience helps.

Do You Need a Telescope or Binoculars?

No.

The unaided eye is generally the best tool for watching a meteor shower because it provides a much wider field of view than binoculars or a telescope.

Meteors can appear almost anywhere in the sky and move quickly. A telescope's narrow field of view makes it likely that the observer will miss them.

Binoculars and telescopes can be useful for many astronomical objects, but they usually do not improve ordinary meteor-shower viewing.

Do You Need Special Eye Protection?

No.

Meteor showers are nighttime events involving brief streaks of light in the atmosphere and do not require eclipse glasses or other special eye protection.

This is very different from observing a solar eclipse, where looking toward the Sun requires proper solar viewing protection except during the brief total phase of a total solar eclipse.

How Does Moonlight Affect a Meteor Shower?

Bright moonlight can wash out dim meteors in much the same way that artificial light pollution does.

A shower occurring near a new moon can therefore provide better viewing conditions than the same shower occurring when a bright Moon is above the horizon.

A strong shower still occurs even if moonlight makes it difficult to see. The Moon affects what observers can detect, not whether Earth is actually encountering the meteoroid stream.

How Does Light Pollution Affect Meteor Viewing?

Artificial lighting brightens the night sky and makes faint meteors harder to see.

Observers under dark rural skies can often see substantially more meteors than observers in brightly illuminated urban areas during the same shower.

Bright fireballs may still be visible from cities, but many fainter meteors can disappear against the background glow.

Can Meteor Showers Be Predicted?

Yes.

Because Earth's orbit and known meteoroid streams can be modeled, astronomers can predict when established annual meteor showers will occur and approximately when activity will peak.

Predicted meteor rates are less certain than simply predicting that a shower will occur. Debris streams can contain dense filaments, gaps, and particles released during different passages of their parent objects.

Occasionally, orbital calculations indicate that Earth may encounter an unusually dense trail and experience enhanced activity or a possible meteor storm.

Why Can the Same Meteor Shower Change From Year to Year?

Earth does not necessarily encounter exactly the same part of a debris stream every year.

The gravitational influence of planets can gradually alter debris orbits, and a comet can add new material during later passages around the Sun.

Different parts of a stream can also have different particle densities.

At the same time, observing conditions change. A shower that occurs under a dark, moonless sky one year may coincide with a bright Moon the next.

This means the same annual meteor shower can appear spectacular one year and relatively modest another.

Common Misunderstandings

Shooting Stars Are Falling Stars

No.

Stars are enormous objects located far beyond Earth's atmosphere. A shooting star is the common name for a meteor produced when a small piece of space material enters the atmosphere.

Meteor Showers Happen Because Many Meteors Suddenly Fall Toward Earth

Not exactly.

Earth is moving through a preexisting stream of particles orbiting the Sun. From Earth's perspective, those particles enter the atmosphere and produce the shower.

Every Meteor Shower Comes From a Comet

No.

Most major showers are associated with comets, but there are exceptions. The Geminids are associated with 3200 Phaethon, while the Quadrantids are associated with 2003 EH1, an asteroid-like object that may be the remnant of a former comet.

Meteors Come From the Constellation at the Radiant

No.

The radiant is a perspective effect. The distant stars forming the constellation are unrelated to the meteoroid stream.

Meteor Showers Usually Drop Meteorites on the Ground

No.

Most shower particles are small and are destroyed in the atmosphere.

You Need a Telescope to Watch a Meteor Shower

No.

The unaided eye is usually better because it provides a much wider view of the sky.

A Forecast of 100 Meteors Per Hour Means You Will See 100

No.

Published rates such as ZHR represent standardized or idealized observing conditions. Actual observed rates can be much lower.

A Bright Moon Makes the Meteor Shower Weaker

Not physically.

Moonlight makes faint meteors harder to see, but it does not prevent Earth from passing through the debris stream.

Why Meteor Showers Matter

Meteor showers provide scientists with opportunities to study small particles left behind by comets and other solar-system objects without having to travel to those objects directly.

Meteor observations can help researchers investigate the structure and evolution of meteoroid streams, the history of their parent bodies, and how small particles interact with Earth's atmosphere.

For observers, meteor showers are also among the most accessible astronomical events. They generally require no telescope, binoculars, or specialized eye protection—just a reasonably dark sky, suitable weather, and time to watch.

They also provide a visible reminder that Earth is moving through a solar system containing enormous numbers of small particles and debris streams left behind by objects traveling around the Sun.

Conclusion

A meteor shower occurs when Earth passes through a stream of small particles orbiting the Sun, usually debris associated with a comet and sometimes with an asteroid-like object.

As those particles enter Earth's atmosphere at high speed, atmospheric interactions and ablation produce the streaks of light called meteors.

Annual showers return around similar dates because Earth repeatedly crosses the same debris streams as it travels around the Sun. The meteors appear to radiate from a common point because of perspective, not because they originate in the stars of a constellation.

Most meteor-shower particles are tiny and never reach the ground. Despite their dramatic appearance, meteor showers are generally harmless, predictable astronomical events that can be enjoyed without telescopes or special eye protection.
