How Does Air Quality (AQI) Work?

The Air Quality Index (AQI) is a scale used to show how clean or polluted the outdoor air is and what potential health effects people may experience.

Rather than reporting raw pollution measurements, the AQI converts air pollution data into a simple number and color category that is easier for the public to understand. Higher AQI values indicate worse air quality and greater potential health risks.

Who Calculates the AQI?

In the United States, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) developed the Air Quality Index.

The AQI is calculated using air pollution measurements collected by federal, state, local, and tribal monitoring networks across the country. Weather agencies, health departments, and many news organizations use these official AQI values when issuing air quality reports.

What Pollutants Does the AQI Measure?

The AQI is based on five major categories of air pollutants regulated under the Clean Air Act:

  • Ground-level ozone.
  • Particle pollution (PM2.5 and PM10).
  • Carbon monoxide (CO).
  • Sulfur dioxide (SO₂).
  • Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂).

Particle pollution includes both PM2.5 (fine particles measuring 2.5 micrometers or smaller) and PM10 (coarser particles measuring up to 10 micrometers), which differ primarily in their size.

For any given location, the AQI reflects the pollutant with the highest health risk at that time.

Why Is PM2.5 So Important During Wildfires?

During wildfire smoke events, the AQI is often driven by PM2.5, which consists of tiny airborne particles measuring 2.5 micrometers or smaller.

Because these particles are so small, they can travel deep into the lungs and may enter the bloodstream. Wildfire smoke frequently causes PM2.5 levels to rise much more quickly than other pollutants, making it one of the primary concerns during smoke events.

What Do the AQI Numbers Mean?

The AQI is divided into six categories:

  • 0–50 (Green): Good — Air quality poses little or no health risk.
  • 51–100 (Yellow): Moderate — Air quality is generally acceptable, although unusually sensitive individuals may experience minor effects.
  • 101–150 (Orange): Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups — Children, older adults, pregnant people, and individuals with heart or lung conditions may be affected.
  • 151–200 (Red): Unhealthy — Everyone may begin experiencing health effects, with sensitive groups at greater risk.
  • 201–300 (Purple): Very Unhealthy — Health warnings indicate an increased risk for the general public.
  • 301–500 (Maroon): Hazardous — Emergency conditions where everyone is more likely to experience significant health effects.

Who Is Most at Risk?

Poor air quality can affect anyone, but higher-risk groups include:

  • Children.
  • Older adults.
  • Pregnant people.
  • People with asthma.
  • People with chronic lung diseases such as COPD.
  • People with heart disease.
  • Individuals who work or exercise outdoors for extended periods.

These groups may be advised to reduce outdoor activity when AQI levels become elevated.

How Can People Reduce Their Exposure?

When air quality worsens, public health officials may recommend:

  • Limiting prolonged outdoor activity.
  • Keeping windows and doors closed when appropriate.
  • Using air conditioning or air filtration systems if available.
  • Running portable air purifiers with HEPA filters.
  • Wearing a properly fitted N95 or similar respirator during severe smoke events if outdoor exposure cannot be avoided.
  • Following guidance from local health officials.

The appropriate response depends on the AQI level and individual health conditions.

Why Is AQI Frequently in the News?

The Air Quality Index is commonly mentioned during:

  • Wildfires.
  • Canadian wildfire smoke events.
  • Urban smog episodes.
  • Heat waves.
  • Industrial pollution incidents.
  • Dust storms.
  • Public health advisories.

News reports often reference AQI values to help people understand whether outdoor activities are considered safe.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that the AQI measures only wildfire smoke. In reality, it reflects several different types of air pollution, and the highest pollutant determines the reported AQI.

Another misunderstanding is that an AQI of 100 means the air is 100% polluted. The AQI is an index that represents health risk, not a percentage of pollution.

Finally, air quality can vary significantly within the same city depending on weather conditions, terrain, traffic, industrial activity, and the location of nearby wildfires.

Conclusion

The Air Quality Index (AQI) is a standardized system developed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency to communicate outdoor air quality and potential health risks. By converting pollution measurements into simple numerical and color-coded categories, the AQI helps individuals, families, schools, and communities make informed decisions during wildfire smoke events, pollution episodes, and other environmental conditions.
