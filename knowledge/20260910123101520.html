How Does an Electric Vehicle Work?

October 3, 202619 min read

A battery-electric vehicle, or EV, uses electricity stored in a large rechargeable battery to power one or more electric motors that drive the wheels. Unlike a gasoline or diesel vehicle, a fully electric vehicle does not need an internal-combustion engine to produce propulsion.

The basic energy path is straightforward: electricity is stored in the battery, power electronics control how that electricity reaches the motor, and the motor converts electrical energy into mechanical rotation that moves the vehicle.

When the vehicle slows down, the process can partially operate in reverse. The motor can act as a generator and return some of the vehicle's energy to the battery through regenerative braking.

Although the basic principle is simple, a modern EV contains sophisticated systems for controlling thousands of battery cells, converting electrical power, managing temperature, charging safely and coordinating the enormous amounts of power needed to accelerate a vehicle.

What Stores the Energy in an EV?

Most modern electric vehicles use a large lithium-ion battery pack mounted low in the vehicle, often underneath the passenger compartment.

The pack contains many individual electrochemical cells. Cells are electrically connected and physically assembled into larger structures, although the exact arrangement varies by manufacturer and battery design.

A single cell operates at only a few volts, so many cells must be combined to create the hundreds of volts used by a modern EV powertrain.

The battery stores energy chemically. When the vehicle needs power, electrochemical reactions inside the cells produce an electric current that can be delivered to the vehicle's electrical systems and propulsion motors.

When the battery is charged, external electrical energy drives the battery chemistry back toward a higher-energy state so the energy can be used later.

How Much Electricity Does an EV Battery Store?

EV battery capacity is usually described in kilowatt-hours, abbreviated kWh.

A kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy. It represents the amount of energy used by one kilowatt of power operating for one hour.

For example, a battery with 80 kWh of usable energy could theoretically provide 80 kilowatts for one hour, 40 kilowatts for two hours or 8 kilowatts for ten hours.

Real vehicle operation is more complicated because energy consumption constantly changes with speed, acceleration, temperature, terrain, climate control and other factors.

Battery capacity should not be confused with power. Capacity describes how much energy can be stored, while kilowatts describe how quickly energy is being transferred or used.

How Does Electricity Make the Wheels Turn?

The battery supplies electrical energy to one or more traction motors.

Inside an electric motor, magnetic fields interact to produce rotational force called torque. That rotation is transferred through gears and shafts to the wheels.

Different EV manufacturers use different motor designs, including permanent-magnet motors and induction motors, as well as other configurations.

Regardless of the exact design, the motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy.

Electric motors can produce substantial torque from very low speeds, which is one reason EVs can accelerate quickly without needing a traditional multi-speed transmission.

What Does the Inverter Do?

The high-voltage battery stores and supplies direct current, or DC electricity.

Many EV traction motors operate using controlled alternating current, or AC.

A power-electronic device called an inverter converts the battery's DC electricity into the controlled electrical waveforms required by the motor.

The inverter does much more than simply change DC into AC. By precisely controlling voltage, current and frequency, it controls the motor's torque and speed.

When regenerative braking occurs, the inverter helps manage power flowing in the opposite direction, converting electrical energy produced by the motor into a form that can recharge the battery.

Modern inverters use high-power semiconductor switches that can control large amounts of electricity extremely quickly and efficiently.

What Happens When You Press the Accelerator?

The accelerator pedal in an EV does not mechanically open a throttle to feed fuel into an engine.

Instead, sensors measure the driver's accelerator request and send that information to the vehicle's control systems.

The vehicle considers the requested acceleration along with factors such as battery condition, traction, motor limits, temperature and stability-control requirements.

The power electronics then supply the appropriate electrical current to the motor or motors.

The motor produces torque, which is transferred to the wheels.

Because electrical torque can be controlled almost instantly, EV acceleration can feel particularly smooth and responsive.

Does an EV Need a Transmission?

Most battery-electric vehicles do not use the type of multi-speed automatic transmission found in conventional gasoline vehicles.

Electric motors can operate efficiently across a wide range of rotational speeds and can produce strong torque at low speeds.

Most EVs therefore use a single-speed reduction gear between the motor and the wheels. The reduction gearing allows the motor to spin much faster than the wheels while multiplying torque at the wheels.

There are exceptions, including EVs that use more than one gear for particular performance or efficiency goals, but a complicated multi-speed transmission is generally unnecessary.

This reduces the number of moving mechanical components in the drivetrain.

How Does an EV Go in Reverse?

A conventional vehicle typically needs mechanical transmission gearing to reverse the direction of the wheels.

An electric motor can reverse its direction electronically.

The vehicle's power electronics change how the motor is energized, causing it to rotate in the opposite direction.

As a result, most EVs do not need a separate mechanical reverse gear in the traditional sense.

The vehicle's software still limits reverse speed and controls torque for safe operation.

What Is Regenerative Braking?

One of the major differences between electric and conventional vehicles is regenerative braking.

When an EV accelerates, electrical energy from the battery drives the motor and increases the vehicle's kinetic energy.

When the driver slows down, the motor can operate as a generator. Instead of consuming electricity to create motion, the rotating wheels drive the motor, causing it to generate electrical energy.

The inverter directs that energy back toward the battery.

The process creates resistance to the vehicle's motion, slowing the vehicle while recovering some energy that would otherwise be lost as heat in the brakes.

Regenerative braking cannot recover all of the energy originally used to accelerate because every energy conversion has losses, but it can substantially improve efficiency, particularly in stop-and-go driving.

Does an EV Still Have Regular Brakes?

Yes.

Electric vehicles still have conventional friction brakes, usually using brake discs and pads similar to those found on other passenger vehicles.

Regenerative braking cannot handle every braking situation.

Friction brakes are needed for hard stops, emergency braking, low-speed stopping and situations where regenerative braking is limited by battery temperature, state of charge, traction or other conditions.

Modern EVs can blend regenerative and friction braking automatically so the driver experiences predictable braking behavior.

Because regenerative braking handles some routine slowing, friction brake components may experience less wear than they would in a comparable conventional vehicle.

However, reduced use can create other maintenance considerations, such as corrosion in environments where road salt and moisture are common.

What Is One-Pedal Driving?

Some EVs provide strong regenerative braking when the driver releases the accelerator pedal.

This can allow the driver to control much of the vehicle's acceleration and deceleration using one pedal. Pressing the accelerator makes the vehicle move faster, while gradually releasing it causes the vehicle to slow significantly.

Some systems can bring the vehicle all the way to a stop without requiring the brake pedal during ordinary driving.

This feature is commonly called one-pedal driving.

It does not eliminate the brake pedal or conventional braking system. The brake pedal remains available whenever stronger or more immediate braking is needed.

What Happens When You Plug In an EV?

Charging transfers electrical energy from an external source into the vehicle's battery.

There are two major approaches: AC charging and DC fast charging.

With AC charging, the charging station or electrical outlet supplies alternating current to the vehicle. An onboard charger inside the EV converts that AC electricity into DC electricity suitable for charging the battery.

With DC fast charging, large power-conversion equipment outside the vehicle converts AC grid electricity into DC. The charging station then delivers high-voltage DC power more directly to the vehicle's battery system, bypassing most of the vehicle's onboard AC charger.

This allows DC fast chargers to deliver much more power than typical onboard AC chargers.

What Are Level 1 and Level 2 Charging?

In North America, common AC charging is often described as Level 1 or Level 2.

Level 1 charging generally uses a standard 120-volt household electrical circuit. It provides relatively low charging power and can require a long time to replenish a large EV battery.

Level 2 charging uses a higher-voltage AC supply, commonly 208 or 240 volts. It can deliver substantially more power and is widely used for home, workplace and public charging.

Actual charging speed depends on the electrical circuit, charging equipment and the maximum AC charging power accepted by the vehicle.

A charging station capable of supplying more power than the vehicle's onboard charger can accept will not force the vehicle to charge beyond its designed limit.

Why Is DC Fast Charging So Much Faster?

A DC fast charger contains powerful electrical equipment capable of converting large amounts of grid electricity and delivering high-voltage DC directly to the vehicle.

Depending on the vehicle and charger, charging power can reach hundreds of kilowatts.

The EV and charging station continuously communicate to determine how much power can safely be delivered.

Charging speed depends on battery temperature, state of charge, battery design, charger capability and other limits established by the vehicle.

The maximum number advertised for a charger or vehicle is therefore not necessarily the power that will be maintained throughout the entire charging session.

Why Does Fast Charging Slow Down as the Battery Fills?

A battery cannot safely accept its maximum charging power at every state of charge.

When the battery is relatively empty and at an appropriate temperature, it may accept very high charging power.

As the battery becomes fuller, the battery-management system gradually reduces charging current to keep cell voltages and temperatures within safe limits.

This produces a charging curve rather than one constant charging rate.

For this reason, charging from a low state of charge to around 80 percent can sometimes take much less time than charging from 80 percent all the way to 100 percent.

The exact behavior varies by vehicle and battery chemistry.

What Is Battery Preconditioning?

Lithium-ion batteries have temperature ranges in which they can accept high charging power most effectively.

If a battery is very cold, the vehicle may need to warm it before rapid charging. If it is too hot, the thermal-management system may need to cool it.

Many EVs can automatically precondition the battery before arriving at a fast charger, particularly when the charger is entered as a destination in the vehicle's navigation system.

Bringing the battery toward an appropriate temperature can improve charging speed and help protect the cells from damaging conditions.

What Is the Battery-Management System?

An EV battery pack is controlled by a battery-management system, commonly called the BMS.

The BMS monitors conditions throughout the battery pack, including cell voltages, temperatures, current and state of charge.

It helps keep individual cells within safe operating limits and can balance cells so portions of the battery do not become excessively different in their state of charge.

The BMS also estimates values such as remaining charge and available power.

If conditions become unsafe, the system can reduce charging or propulsion power and, in severe circumstances, disconnect the high-voltage battery.

Battery management is essential because an EV pack contains a large amount of stored energy and thousands of individual electrochemical cells may need to operate together as one system.

Does the Battery Really Go From Zero to 100 Percent?

The percentage shown to the driver does not necessarily represent the absolute electrochemical limits of the battery cells.

Manufacturers can reserve some battery capacity above or below the portion normally available to the driver. These reserves are sometimes called buffers.

The vehicle's displayed zero percent can therefore occur before the cells reach a truly destructive level of discharge, while displayed 100 percent can be below the absolute maximum voltage the chemistry could theoretically reach.

The exact size and operation of these buffers vary by manufacturer and vehicle.

They help protect the battery and can contribute to long-term durability.

Why Does an EV Battery Need Heating and Cooling?

Temperature has a major effect on lithium-ion battery performance, charging and lifespan.

High temperatures can accelerate chemical degradation. Very low temperatures reduce the battery's ability to deliver and accept power and can make some charging conditions harmful.

Many modern EVs therefore use active thermal-management systems.

Liquid coolant may circulate through or near the battery pack. Pumps, radiators, chillers, heaters and heat pumps can be used to control battery temperature depending on the vehicle design.

The vehicle's software continuously manages these systems while driving, charging and sometimes while parked.

Why Does an EV Lose Range in Cold Weather?

Cold weather affects an EV in several ways.

The battery's internal chemical processes become less effective at low temperatures, increasing resistance and temporarily reducing available performance.

The vehicle also needs energy to heat the passenger cabin and sometimes the battery itself.

A gasoline vehicle produces large amounts of waste heat from its engine, which can be used for cabin heating. An EV's drivetrain is much more efficient and therefore produces less waste heat, so cabin heating may require energy taken directly from the battery.

Vehicles equipped with heat pumps can move heat more efficiently than simple resistance heaters under many conditions, although heat-pump performance also depends on outside temperature and system design.

Cold-weather range loss is therefore caused by a combination of battery conditions and additional energy consumption.

Why Does Highway Driving Often Use More Energy?

At higher speeds, aerodynamic drag becomes increasingly important.

The force needed to push a vehicle through the air rises rapidly with speed, and the power required to overcome aerodynamic drag increases even more strongly because the vehicle is also covering distance faster.

This is why an EV may achieve excellent efficiency during moderate-speed city driving but consume considerably more energy per mile at high highway speeds.

Regenerative braking also provides more opportunities to recover energy during stop-and-go driving, while steady highway travel involves relatively little braking.

Gasoline vehicles can show a different city-versus-highway pattern because their engines and transmissions have different efficiency characteristics.

How Efficient Is an Electric Motor?

Electric motors can convert a large percentage of the electrical energy they receive into useful mechanical energy.

Internal-combustion engines lose a much larger portion of fuel energy as heat through exhaust gases, cooling systems and internal friction.

An EV still has energy losses in the battery, inverter, motor, gears, tires and other systems, so it is not 100 percent efficient.

Electric drivetrains nevertheless convert stored energy into vehicle motion much more efficiently than typical internal-combustion powertrains.

This efficiency is one reason an EV can travel substantial distances using an amount of electrical energy whose raw energy content is much lower than the energy contained in the gasoline consumed by a comparable conventional vehicle.

Why Are EVs So Quick From a Stop?

Electric motors can produce high torque at very low rotational speeds.

The inverter can also change motor torque extremely quickly.

A gasoline engine generally needs to increase its rotational speed to reach its strongest power range, and a transmission must select appropriate gear ratios as vehicle speed changes.

An EV can often deliver strong motor torque almost immediately through a fixed reduction gear.

This does not mean every EV is designed for high performance. Manufacturers can limit motor output for efficiency, traction, battery protection, cost or other reasons.

But the characteristics of electric motors make rapid, smooth acceleration relatively easy to achieve.

How Do All-Wheel-Drive EVs Work?

Many electric vehicles create all-wheel drive by installing separate motors for the front and rear axles.

Because the motors can be controlled electronically, the vehicle can rapidly adjust how much torque is delivered to each axle.

Some high-performance EVs use even more motors, allowing additional control over torque distribution.

This arrangement can eliminate the need for a mechanical driveshaft connecting the front and rear axles.

Software, traction-control systems and motor inverters coordinate the motors to provide the desired combination of acceleration, stability and efficiency.

Does an EV Have a Normal 12-Volt Battery?

Usually, yes, although some vehicles use another low-voltage standard.

The large high-voltage traction battery powers the motors, but many conventional automotive systems operate from a separate low-voltage electrical system.

Lights, computers, locks, control modules and safety equipment can depend on this low-voltage system.

Instead of an engine-driven alternator, an EV normally uses a DC-to-DC converter to take energy from the high-voltage traction battery and supply the low-voltage electrical system.

A small low-voltage battery allows important electronics to operate when the main high-voltage system is disconnected.

A weak low-voltage battery can therefore prevent an EV from starting its high-voltage systems even when the large traction battery contains plenty of energy.

What Happens When You Turn an EV On?

Starting an EV is different from cranking a gasoline engine.

The vehicle's low-voltage electronics wake up and perform safety checks. If conditions are acceptable, high-voltage contactors connect the traction battery to the vehicle's propulsion electrical system.

The vehicle can then become ready to drive.

There may be almost no sound because there is no engine that needs to begin idling.

When the vehicle is stopped, the electric motor does not need to keep spinning simply to remain ready for acceleration.

Is the High-Voltage Battery Always Connected?

No.

EV battery packs typically contain large electrically controlled switches called contactors that can disconnect the high-voltage battery from much of the vehicle.

The system can open these contactors when the vehicle is shut down or when a serious electrical fault or crash condition is detected.

EVs also use insulation monitoring, fuses, crash sensors and other protective systems to reduce electrical hazards.

High-voltage components and cables are generally clearly identified and designed so ordinary vehicle occupants do not come into contact with dangerous voltages during normal use.

What Happens During a Crash?

Electric vehicles include safety systems designed to respond to collisions.

Depending on the severity and nature of the crash, the vehicle can disconnect the high-voltage battery from parts of the electrical system. Structural protection around the battery is designed to reduce the likelihood of cells being crushed or penetrated.

Like gasoline vehicles, EVs can catch fire after severe damage. Lithium-ion battery fires present different challenges because damaged cells can enter thermal runaway, in which internal heat causes reactions that release additional heat and can spread to neighboring cells.

Manufacturers, emergency responders and safety organizations have developed procedures specifically for dealing with damaged high-voltage batteries.

Do EV Batteries Wear Out?

Yes, but battery degradation is gradual rather than a simple switch from good to unusable.

Lithium-ion batteries slowly lose capacity because of chemical and physical changes caused by charging, discharging, temperature and calendar age.

An older EV may therefore still operate normally but have less driving range than it did when new.

Battery-management and thermal-management systems are designed partly to slow this degradation by keeping cells within controlled voltage and temperature ranges.

A detailed explanation of self-discharge and battery aging is a separate subject from how an EV powertrain works.

Does an EV Need Oil Changes?

A battery-electric vehicle does not have engine oil because it has no internal-combustion engine.

It also eliminates many engine-related maintenance items such as spark plugs, engine air-fuel systems and exhaust components.

However, an EV is not maintenance-free.

It still has tires, suspension, steering, brakes, cabin filters, cooling systems, air conditioning and other components requiring inspection or service.

Reduction gears and other drivetrain components may also contain lubricants, although service requirements vary by vehicle.

Tire wear can be significant because EVs can be heavy and capable of producing substantial torque.

Does an EV Produce Any Emissions?

A battery-electric vehicle produces no tailpipe exhaust because it does not burn gasoline or diesel fuel while driving.

However, that does not mean its total environmental impact is zero.

Producing the vehicle and battery requires energy and materials. Generating the electricity used for charging can also produce emissions depending on the power sources supplying the electrical grid.

Tires, road wear and other vehicle-related environmental effects still exist as well.

Evaluating total environmental impact therefore requires considering the vehicle's entire life cycle and the electricity used to operate it, rather than looking only at the absence of a tailpipe.

Can an EV Power a House?

Some electric vehicles support bidirectional charging, meaning electrical energy can flow out of the traction battery as well as into it.

Depending on the vehicle and equipment, this capability may be used to power electrical devices, provide backup electricity to a home or potentially exchange power with the electrical grid.

Terms such as vehicle-to-load, vehicle-to-home and vehicle-to-grid describe different applications of bidirectional power.

Not every EV supports these functions, and powering a building requires compatible electrical equipment and appropriate safety controls.

The Bottom Line

An electric vehicle converts stored electrical energy directly into motion.

Electricity stored in a high-voltage battery flows through power electronics that precisely control one or more electric motors. The motors turn through reduction gearing to drive the wheels. Because electric motors operate effectively across a wide range of speeds, most EVs do not require conventional multi-speed transmissions.

When the vehicle slows, regenerative braking allows the motor to operate as a generator and recover some of the vehicle's kinetic energy, returning electricity to the battery instead of wasting all of that energy as heat in friction brakes.

Charging reverses the energy flow. AC charging uses an onboard charger to convert grid electricity into the DC electricity needed by the battery, while DC fast chargers perform much of that conversion outside the vehicle and can deliver substantially higher power.

A battery-management system monitors the cells, while heating and cooling equipment keeps the battery within appropriate temperature ranges. Inverters, DC-to-DC converters, high-voltage contactors and multiple electronic control systems coordinate the flow of energy throughout the vehicle.

The result is a drivetrain with fewer major moving parts than an internal-combustion powertrain and a much more direct path from stored energy to the wheels: battery to power electronics to electric motor to motion.
