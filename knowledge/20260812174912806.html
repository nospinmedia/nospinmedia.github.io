What Is a Filibuster?

August 27, 202613 min read

What Is a Filibuster?

A filibuster is a Senate tactic or procedural situation that can delay or block action by preventing the Senate from ending debate and moving toward a final vote.

The filibuster is mainly associated with the U.S. Senate. It is not created by the Constitution, and it does not mean that every bill legally requires 60 votes to pass.

Instead, Senate rules generally allow extended debate. For most legislation, ending that debate through a procedure called cloture requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn. With 100 senators, that normally means 60 votes.

This creates an important distinction: many bills can ultimately pass with a simple majority, but supporters may first need 60 votes to overcome a filibuster and reach the point where a final vote can occur.

Why Does the Senate Have a Filibuster?

The Constitution does not establish a filibuster or require a 60-vote threshold for ordinary legislation.

The filibuster developed from the Senate's rules and traditions allowing extended debate.

Unlike the House of Representatives, which uses rules that tightly limit debate on most legislation, the Senate historically allowed individual senators much greater ability to continue debate and delay action.

Over time, senators learned to use those procedures to obstruct or postpone measures they opposed.

The modern filibuster is therefore a product of Senate procedure and institutional history rather than a specific constitutional command.

Does a Senator Have to Keep Talking?

Usually not under modern Senate practice.

The best-known image of a filibuster is a senator standing on the Senate floor and speaking continuously for hours. These talking filibusters have occurred and remain possible in some circumstances.

But most modern filibusters do not require a senator to continuously speak on the floor.

Instead, the practical issue is often whether supporters of a bill have enough votes to invoke cloture and overcome the possibility of continued debate or procedural delay.

Because Senate leaders know that 60 votes may be required to end debate, legislation can effectively be blocked without opponents having to maintain a continuous speech.

What Is Cloture?

Cloture is the Senate procedure used to limit debate and move a matter toward a final vote.

The Senate first adopted a formal cloture rule in 1917 after repeated disputes over unlimited debate.

Under the original rule, cloture generally required two-thirds of senators present and voting.

In 1975, the Senate changed the threshold for most matters to three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn.

Under current Senate Rule XXII, that normally means 60 votes when all 100 Senate seats are filled.

Cloture does not necessarily produce an immediate vote on final passage. The Senate's rules can still allow additional debate and procedural steps after cloture is invoked, although the amount of remaining consideration is limited.

Does Every Bill Need 60 Votes to Pass the Senate?

No.

This is one of the most common misunderstandings about the filibuster.

Most ordinary legislation can pass the Senate with a simple majority once it reaches a final passage vote, assuming constitutional requirements are otherwise satisfied.

The 60-vote threshold usually concerns ending debate, not the final vote on the bill itself.

For example, a bill might have 55 senators willing to vote for final passage. That would be enough to pass the bill if a final vote occurred. But if opponents prevent the Senate from ending debate and supporters cannot obtain the 60 votes normally required for cloture, the bill may never reach that final vote.

That is why news reports often describe legislation as "needing 60 votes" even though the more precise explanation is that 60 votes may be needed to overcome a filibuster.

What Is a Talking Filibuster?

A talking filibuster occurs when one or more senators hold the floor and continue speaking to delay Senate action.

Senators have historically used extended speeches to postpone votes, draw public attention to an issue, or pressure colleagues to negotiate.

Some famous filibusters lasted many hours.

Talking filibusters are highly visible, but they are not the only way Senate procedures can delay action. Modern filibusters more often involve the threat or use of procedural obstruction that requires supporters to assemble enough votes for cloture.

Why Doesn't the House Have the Same Kind of Filibuster?

The House of Representatives operates under different procedural rules.

Because the House has hundreds of members, it developed mechanisms that strictly control how long legislation can be debated and which amendments may be offered.

The House Rules Committee and other procedures allow the chamber to set time limits on debate and move legislation toward a vote.

As a result, the modern Senate-style filibuster does not exist in the House.

How Did the Filibuster Develop?

The early Senate did not have today's cloture rule.

In 1806, the Senate eliminated a procedural motion known as the previous question motion. That change left the chamber without a simple general procedure for cutting off debate.

The elimination of that motion was not an explicit decision by the Founders to create the modern filibuster. Extended obstruction developed gradually afterward as senators took advantage of the Senate's permissive debate rules.

The term filibuster became associated with legislative obstruction in the United States during the 19th century.

By the early 20th century, repeated filibusters created pressure for a formal way to end debate.

In 1917, the Senate adopted Rule XXII, establishing the cloture procedure. The original rule required a two-thirds vote to end debate.

In 1975, the Senate lowered the cloture threshold for most matters to three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn, normally 60 senators today.

Is the Filibuster in the Constitution?

No.

The Constitution specifies supermajority requirements for certain actions, such as overriding a presidential veto, ratifying treaties, convicting an impeached official, and proposing constitutional amendments.

It does not impose a 60-vote requirement for ordinary Senate legislation.

The modern filibuster and cloture procedures arise from Senate rules and precedents adopted under the chamber's constitutional authority to determine its own rules of proceedings.

What Is the Nuclear Option?

The nuclear option is an informal name for a Senate procedural method used to establish a new precedent about how Senate rules are interpreted or applied using a simple majority vote.

It does not involve physically rewriting the Senate rules through the ordinary formal rule-change process.

Instead, a senator raises a procedural point, the presiding officer rules on it, and the Senate can vote on whether to uphold or overturn that ruling. A majority can thereby establish a new Senate precedent governing future proceedings.

This method has been used to reduce the practical cloture threshold for presidential nominations.

What Happened to the Filibuster for Presidential Nominations?

The Senate has substantially limited the filibuster for nominations.

In 2013, the Senate established a precedent allowing cloture on most presidential nominations, including lower federal court nominations, with a simple majority rather than the previous three-fifths threshold. Supreme Court nominations were excluded from that change.

In 2017, the Senate established another precedent extending the simple-majority cloture standard to Supreme Court nominations.

As a result, presidential nominations generally are no longer subject to the ordinary 60-vote cloture threshold that applies to most legislation.

The Senate still votes on whether to confirm nominees, typically by a simple majority.

Does the Filibuster Apply to Supreme Court Nominees?

Not under current Senate precedent.

Since the 2017 precedent change, cloture on Supreme Court nominations can be invoked by a simple majority.

This means a Senate majority can generally advance and confirm a Supreme Court nominee without obtaining 60 votes.

What Is Budget Reconciliation?

Budget reconciliation is a special congressional process for legislation involving certain federal spending, revenue, and debt-limit matters.

Reconciliation legislation receives expedited treatment in the Senate, including limits on debate. Because debate is limited, the ordinary legislative filibuster cannot be used in the same way against a reconciliation bill.

As a result, reconciliation legislation can often pass the Senate with a simple majority.

This is why major tax and spending measures sometimes pass with 51 votes—or 50 senators plus the vice president's tie-breaking vote—even when supporters could not obtain 60 votes for ordinary legislation.

Can Anything Be Put in a Reconciliation Bill?

No.

Reconciliation is subject to significant procedural restrictions.

One of the most important is the Byrd Rule, named for Senator Robert Byrd.

The Byrd Rule restricts provisions considered extraneous to the budgetary purposes of reconciliation. Among other tests, provisions can face objections when their budgetary effects are merely incidental to broader policy changes or when they otherwise fail the rule's requirements.

The Senate parliamentarian advises the presiding officer on whether provisions comply with reconciliation rules.

A provision that violates the Byrd Rule can be removed unless the Senate waives the objection, which generally requires 60 votes.

Reconciliation therefore provides a path around the ordinary legislative filibuster for qualifying budget legislation, not a way for a Senate majority to pass any policy it wants with 51 votes.

Are There Other Exceptions to the Filibuster?

Yes.

Congress has created expedited procedures for certain categories of Senate business that limit debate or otherwise prevent the ordinary filibuster from operating normally.

Examples can include particular resolutions under federal statutes and specialized congressional-review procedures.

The precise rules depend on the statute or Senate procedure governing the matter.

This is why it is inaccurate to assume that every question before the Senate automatically faces the same 60-vote threshold.

Can 51 Senators Eliminate the Legislative Filibuster?

A Senate majority could potentially establish new precedents changing how filibuster-related procedures operate, similar to what occurred with nominations in 2013 and 2017.

However, saying that 51 senators can simply rewrite Senate Rule XXII is misleading.

Rule XXII itself imposes a higher cloture threshold for proposals to amend the Senate rules: two-thirds of senators present and voting.

The so-called nuclear option takes a different route. Instead of formally rewriting the rule through the normal rule-change process, a majority establishes a new interpretation or precedent concerning how Senate procedure operates.

Whether and how a future Senate might use that process to change the legislative filibuster would depend on the procedural steps taken and the decisions of the Senate at that time.

Can the Vice President Break a Filibuster Tie?

The vice president can cast a tie-breaking vote when the Senate is evenly divided on matters where a simple majority decides the question.

But the vice president cannot turn fewer than the required number of cloture votes into the three-fifths threshold established by Rule XXII.

For example, 59 senators voting for cloture on ordinary legislation would still be short of the 60 votes normally required when all 100 senators are duly chosen and sworn. A vice-presidential tie-breaking vote does not substitute for the rule's three-fifths requirement.

The vice president can become decisive after cloture if final passage produces a 50-50 tie on legislation requiring only a simple majority.

What Happens After Cloture Is Invoked?

Invoking cloture limits further consideration but does not always produce an immediate final vote.

Senate rules provide for a limited amount of additional debate and allow certain procedural actions after cloture.

The exact amount of post-cloture time and the procedures available can depend on the type of matter under consideration.

Senators frequently reach unanimous-consent agreements to shorten this process and schedule votes more quickly.

What Is Unanimous Consent?

Much of the Senate's everyday work occurs through unanimous-consent agreements.

Rather than formally using every procedural step available under Senate rules, senators often agree to waive certain requirements, limit debate, schedule votes, or structure consideration of legislation and nominations.

If no senator objects, the agreement allows the Senate to proceed efficiently.

If a senator objects, Senate leaders may have to use more time-consuming procedures, potentially including cloture.

This helps explain why not every Senate bill produces a visible filibuster or cloture vote even though extended debate remains available under the rules.

Why Is the Filibuster Controversial?

The filibuster has long generated debate over the proper balance between majority rule and minority rights in the Senate.

Supporters argue that it encourages negotiation, protects political minorities from rapidly changing laws, and forces broader agreement before major legislation advances.

Critics argue that it allows a minority of senators to block legislation supported by a majority, contributes to legislative gridlock, and creates a supermajority requirement for ordinary legislation that the Constitution itself does not require.

The filibuster also has a complicated history. It has been used for many different policy disputes, including prominently to obstruct civil-rights legislation during the 19th and 20th centuries.

Whether the filibuster should be preserved, modified, restored to a more traditional talking format, or eliminated remains an ongoing political and institutional debate.

Common Misunderstandings

Every Senate Bill Needs 60 Votes to Pass

No.

Most ordinary legislation can pass on a simple-majority final vote. The 60-vote threshold generally arises because three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn are normally needed to invoke cloture and end debate.

A Senator Must Talk Continuously to Maintain a Filibuster

Usually not under modern Senate practice.

Talking filibusters still exist, but modern procedural filibusters generally do not require opponents to continuously occupy the floor.

The Filibuster Is in the Constitution

No.

The Constitution does not establish a filibuster or require 60 votes for ordinary legislation. The practice developed through Senate rules and precedents.

The Founders Created the 60-Vote Rule

No.

The Senate did not adopt its first cloture rule until 1917, and the current three-fifths threshold for most matters dates to 1975.

Supreme Court Nominees Need 60 Votes

Not under current Senate precedent.

Since 2017, cloture on Supreme Court nominations can generally be invoked with a simple majority.

Reconciliation Lets the Senate Majority Pass Anything With 51 Votes

No.

Reconciliation is limited to qualifying budget legislation and is subject to rules including the Byrd Rule.

The Vice President Can Turn 59 Cloture Votes Into 60

No.

The Rule XXII cloture requirement is based on three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn, not simply a majority vote that can be resolved through the vice president's ordinary tie-breaking authority.

The House Has the Same Filibuster

No.

House rules tightly control debate, so the modern Senate-style filibuster does not operate in the House.

Why the Filibuster Matters

The filibuster can significantly change how much political support is needed to move legislation through the Senate.

A party may control the chamber with 51 or more senators but still be unable to advance ordinary legislation when fewer than 60 senators support ending debate.

At the same time, important exceptions mean that not everything requires the same coalition. Presidential nominations generally advance by majority vote under current Senate precedent, and qualifying reconciliation legislation receives expedited consideration that prevents an ordinary filibuster.

Understanding those distinctions makes Senate news much easier to interpret. When someone says a bill "needs 60 votes," the most useful question is often: Does it need 60 votes to pass, or does it need 60 votes to end debate and reach the final vote?

Conclusion

A filibuster is a Senate tactic or procedural situation that can delay or block action by preventing the chamber from ending debate and moving toward a final vote.

Under current Senate Rule XXII, cloture on most legislation requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn, normally 60 votes in a 100-member Senate. Most legislation itself can still pass with a simple majority once a final vote occurs.

The filibuster is not required by the Constitution and does not operate identically across all Senate business. Current precedents allow most presidential nominations, including Supreme Court nominations, to advance by simple-majority cloture votes, while budget reconciliation and some other expedited procedures are also not subject to the ordinary legislative filibuster.

The modern system is therefore less accurately described as a universal 60-vote rule than as a set of Senate procedures that often allow a determined minority to prevent action unless a sufficiently large majority votes to end debate.
