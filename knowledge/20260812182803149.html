Why Do We Use BC and AD to Number Years?

September 5, 202612 min read

Why Do We Use BC and AD to Number Years?

BC and AD are labels used to number years around a historical reference point traditionally associated with the birth of Jesus Christ.

BC means Before Christ. AD comes from the Latin phrase Anno Domini, meaning "in the year of the Lord."

The system developed in Christian Europe and eventually became widely used alongside the Julian and later Gregorian calendars. Its global reach grew over centuries through European government, scholarship, trade, colonization, international communication, and eventually the widespread civil use of the Gregorian calendar.

Today, BCE and CE are also widely used. They mean Before Common Era and Common Era and use exactly the same year numbers as BC and AD.

What Does AD Mean?

AD stands for the Latin Anno Domini, traditionally translated as "in the year of the Lord."

It refers to years in the era beginning with AD 1.

For example:

  • AD 1 is the first year of the traditional AD era.
  • AD 1066 is the year associated with the Norman Conquest of England.
  • AD 2026 and 2026 CE refer to the same year.

In modern writing, the letters AD are often omitted when it is obvious that a year belongs to the current era.

Does AD Mean After Death?

No.

A common misconception is that AD means After Death, referring to the death of Jesus.

It does not.

AD means Anno Domini.

If AD meant After Death, the system would also need a separate way to number the years traditionally associated with Jesus's lifetime. That is not how the system was constructed.

What Does BC Mean?

BC means Before Christ.

It identifies years before AD 1.

BC years count backward as they approach the beginning of the AD era:

500 BC → 100 BC → 10 BC → 2 BC → 1 BC → AD 1

This means that 500 BC occurred earlier than 100 BC even though 500 is the larger number.

Who Created the AD System?

The AD system is generally credited to Dionysius Exiguus, a Christian monk and scholar who lived during the sixth century.

Dionysius was working on tables used to calculate the date of Easter. In those tables, he numbered years using an era based on what he calculated to be the incarnation or birth era of Jesus rather than an older system connected to the Roman emperor Diocletian.

This Anno Domini system provided the foundation for the year numbering still familiar today.

Dionysius did not create the entire modern BC/AD convention in its finished form. His work established the AD era. The practice of systematically numbering years before that reference point developed and became common later.

Who Helped Popularize AD Dating?

The English monk and historian Bede, also known as the Venerable Bede, played an important role in spreading Christian-era dating in medieval Europe.

In the eighth century, Bede used dating based on the incarnation of Christ in his historical writings and also described events occurring before that era.

The system gradually became more common in European chronicles, religious documents, government records, and scholarship.

It did not become universal overnight. For centuries, different regions continued to use other methods of identifying years.

Was Jesus Actually Born in AD 1?

Probably not.

The AD system was devised more than five centuries after the period it was attempting to date, and Dionysius did not have access to modern historical methods or all of the evidence available to later scholars.

Historians have long debated the exact year of Jesus's birth. Chronologies based partly on the reign and death of Herod the Great have led many scholars to place the birth several years before AD 1.

This creates an apparent historical oddity: under the calendar system later constructed around his birth, Jesus may have been born in a year labeled BC.

The discrepancy does not change modern year numbering. The established calendar era continued to be used even after scholars questioned the accuracy of its original historical reference point.

Is There a Year Zero?

Not in the traditional BC/AD historical system.

The sequence is:

3 BC → 2 BC → 1 BC → AD 1 → AD 2 → AD 3

There is no traditional historical year AD 0 or 0 BC between 1 BC and AD 1.

This is important when calculating spans of time that cross the BC/AD boundary.

For example, the interval from the beginning of 1 BC to the beginning of AD 1 is one year, not two.

Why Is There No Year Zero?

The Anno Domini system was developed in a period when European year numbering did not use zero as a year between the years before and after the era's starting point.

Dionysius numbered the first year of his era as year 1.

Later historical BC/AD chronology therefore moved directly from 1 BC to AD 1.

Modern mathematics and astronomy have made zero much more useful, so some technical systems handle ancient years differently.

Do Astronomers Use a Year Zero?

Astronomers and some technical dating systems can use astronomical year numbering, which includes a year zero.

In that system:

  • Historical AD 1 corresponds to astronomical year 1.
  • Historical 1 BC corresponds to astronomical year 0.
  • Historical 2 BC corresponds to astronomical year -1.

Including zero simplifies mathematical calculations involving dates that cross the traditional BC/AD boundary.

This means readers should be careful when comparing ancient dates from historical sources with dates generated by astronomical or technical software.

What Are BCE and CE?

BCE means Before Common Era.

CE means Common Era.

They are alternative labels for the same year-numbering system used by BC and AD.

The conversion is direct:

  • 500 BC = 500 BCE
  • 44 BC = 44 BCE
  • AD 1 = 1 CE
  • AD 1066 = 1066 CE
  • AD 2026 = 2026 CE

Changing from BC/AD to BCE/CE does not change the year number or move an event to a different date.

Why Do Some People Use BCE and CE Instead of BC and AD?

BC and AD explicitly use Christian terminology.

BCE and CE provide terminology that can be used in religiously diverse, secular, academic, and international settings while preserving the established year numbers.

The terminology is particularly common in modern historical, archaeological, educational, and scientific writing.

The underlying reference point remains the same. BCE/CE changes the labels, not the chronology.

Is BCE and CE a New System?

No.

The terminology Common Era has a history extending back centuries, although its use has become much more widespread in modern times.

It should not be confused with the invention of a new calendar.

BCE and CE simply provide alternative names for years that can also be written BC and AD.

Is BC/AD the Same Thing as the Gregorian Calendar?

No.

These are related concepts, but they are not the same thing.

BC/AD is a year-numbering era.

The Gregorian calendar is a calendar system governing how years, months, days, and leap years are organized.

The AD era existed for roughly a thousand years before the Gregorian calendar was introduced.

When Was the Gregorian Calendar Introduced?

The Gregorian calendar was introduced under Pope Gregory XIII in 1582 as a reform of the Julian calendar.

The Julian calendar's leap-year system caused the calendar to drift gradually relative to the solar year. The Gregorian reform adjusted the leap-year rules and corrected accumulated calendar drift.

The reform did not restart year numbering at year 1.

Instead, it retained the existing Christian-era year numbers.

That is why the Gregorian calendar and AD numbering are often associated with each other even though they originated many centuries apart.

Did Everyone Adopt the Gregorian Calendar in 1582?

No.

Different countries adopted the Gregorian calendar at different times.

Catholic countries were among the first to adopt it, while some Protestant and Orthodox regions continued using the Julian calendar for much longer.

Britain and its colonies, for example, adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1752.

As a result, historical dates can require careful interpretation because different places sometimes used different calendars at the same time.

What Did People Use Before BC and AD?

There has never been only one way to number years.

Ancient and medieval societies used many systems, including:

  • Years of a ruler's reign.
  • Roman consular years.
  • Dating from the traditional founding of Rome.
  • Olympiads in some Greek historical traditions.
  • Local eras based on major political or religious events.
  • Dynastic calendars.
  • Religious calendars.

A document might identify a year as the fifth year of a particular king's reign rather than assign it a continuously increasing number like 2026.

Historians sometimes have to translate those systems into BC/BCE and AD/CE dates so events from different societies can be placed on a common timeline.

Did Ancient Romans Say They Lived in BC?

No.

A Roman living in what we now call 100 BC did not describe the year as "100 BC."

BC is a retrospective label applied by people using a dating system developed centuries later.

The same principle applies to many ancient dates. When historians say an event happened in 500 BC, they are translating that event into a modern chronological framework rather than necessarily reproducing the date terminology used by people at the time.

Does the Whole World Use BC and AD?

Not exclusively.

Many countries use the Gregorian calendar for civil, commercial, diplomatic, technological, or international purposes, but numerous other calendars and eras continue to be used for religious, cultural, and official purposes.

Examples include the Hebrew calendar, Islamic calendar, Persian calendar, and various Buddhist and traditional calendars.

The same physical day can therefore have different year numbers depending on the calendar system being used.

Why Did BC and AD Become So Widespread?

The system's spread was historical rather than astronomical.

Christianity became deeply embedded in European institutions, and AD dating became increasingly common in European religious, scholarly, governmental, and commercial records.

European political and economic expansion later spread European calendar conventions much more widely through trade, diplomacy, colonization, navigation, education, and administration.

In the modern era, common dating conventions became increasingly useful for international commerce, transportation, science, communications, computing, and record keeping.

The widespread use of these year numbers therefore reflects historical development and international standardization rather than a natural astronomical requirement that years must be counted from this particular starting point.

Why Does the 21st Century Begin in 2001 Instead of 2000?

The absence of a year zero affects the numbering of centuries and millennia.

The first century began with AD 1 and contained the years AD 1 through AD 100.

Therefore:

  • 1st century: AD 1–100
  • 2nd century: AD 101–200
  • 20th century: AD 1901–2000
  • 21st century: AD 2001–2100

For the same reason, the third millennium began on January 1, 2001, not January 1, 2000, when centuries and millennia are counted from the beginning of the traditional era.

The year 2000 was nevertheless an enormous cultural milestone because the leading digits of the year changed from 1 to 2.

What About Decades Such as the 1990s?

Named decades do not necessarily follow the same convention as numbered centuries.

The 1990s normally means the years 1990 through 1999 because the name describes years sharing the digits "199."

A formally counted decade beginning with the first year of the AD era would instead follow groups such as 1–10, 11–20, and so forth.

That is why arguments over whether a "new decade" begins in a year ending in 0 or 1 can depend on which definition of decade is being used.

Does AD Go Before or After the Year?

Traditional English usage often places AD before the year, as in AD 1066, while BC follows the year, as in 44 BC.

This reflects the meaning of the Latin phrase Anno Domini.

Modern style varies, however. Writers and publications may place AD after the year or use CE instead.

The placement does not change the date itself.

Common Misunderstandings

AD Means After Death

No.

AD stands for Anno Domini, not After Death.

Jesus Was Definitely Born in AD 1

No.

The exact year of Jesus's birth is uncertain, and many historical chronologies place it several years before AD 1.

There Was a Year Zero

Not in traditional historical BC/AD chronology.

1 BC was followed directly by AD 1.

BCE and CE Use Different Year Numbers

No.

BCE/CE and BC/AD use corresponding year numbers. 44 BC and 44 BCE are the same year.

The Gregorian Calendar Invented AD

No.

The Anno Domini era originated in the sixth century. The Gregorian calendar was introduced in 1582, roughly a thousand years later.

Everyone in History Used BC and AD

No.

Different societies have used many different calendars, eras, regnal years, and dating systems.

Ancient People Knew They Were Living in BC

No.

BC dates were assigned retrospectively. Someone living in what historians now call 300 BC would have used the dating conventions of their own society.

500 BC Happened After 100 BC Because 500 Is Larger

No.

BC years count down toward AD 1, so 500 BC occurred earlier than 100 BC.

The 21st Century Began in 2000

Under strict ordinal century numbering, no. Because there was no year zero, the 21st century began on January 1, 2001.

Why This Dating System Matters

BC/AD and BCE/CE provide historians and readers with a shared framework for placing events across thousands of years on a common timeline.

Understanding where that framework came from also prevents several common mistakes.

AD does not mean After Death. BCE and CE do not create different year numbers. The Gregorian calendar did not invent AD. Ancient people did not describe themselves as living in BC, and the traditional historical chronology contains no year zero.

The system is ultimately a human convention that became widely adopted through history. Other cultures have used—and continue to use—different starting points for numbering years.

Conclusion

The BC/AD year-numbering system grew from the Anno Domini era developed by the sixth-century scholar Dionysius Exiguus and later expanded and popularized in European historical writing.

BC means Before Christ, while AD means Anno Domini, or "in the year of the Lord." The system uses the traditional birth era of Jesus as its dividing point, although historians generally do not regard AD 1 as a securely established date for Jesus's actual birth.

There is no year zero in traditional BC/AD chronology: 1 BC is followed directly by AD 1.

BCE and CE provide alternative terminology while retaining exactly the same year numbers. The later Gregorian calendar also retained the existing era rather than creating it.

The system became globally influential through the historical spread of European institutions and the widespread international adoption of Gregorian civil dating, but it remains one of several ways humans have devised to organize years and historical time.
