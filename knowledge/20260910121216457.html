How Does a Heat Pump Work?

September 23, 202611 min read

A heat pump heats or cools a building by moving heat from one place to another rather than creating most of its heat directly from electricity or by burning fuel.

In cooling mode, a heat pump works much like an air conditioner: it removes heat from inside the building and releases it outdoors. In heating mode, the process is reversed. The system absorbs heat from outside and transfers it indoors.

This may sound surprising when the outdoor air is cold, but cold air still contains thermal energy. A heat pump uses a refrigerant and a refrigeration cycle to collect some of that energy and move it into the building.

What Is a Heat Pump?

A heat pump is a mechanical system that transfers thermal energy from a lower-temperature location to a higher-temperature location by using external energy, usually electricity.

Most residential heat pumps use a vapor-compression refrigeration cycle, the same basic technology used in refrigerators and air conditioners.

The major components typically include a compressor, condenser, expansion device, evaporator and a circulating refrigerant.

For systems that provide both heating and cooling, a component called a reversing valve changes the direction of refrigerant flow so the indoor and outdoor coils can exchange roles.

How Can a Heat Pump Get Heat From Cold Air?

Temperature and heat are related, but they are not the same thing.

Outdoor air does not need to feel warm to contain thermal energy. As long as matter is above absolute zero, its particles possess thermal energy.

The important requirement is that the refrigerant in the outdoor coil be colder than the surrounding air. Heat naturally flows from the warmer outdoor air into the colder refrigerant.

By controlling the refrigerant's pressure, the heat pump can make the refrigerant evaporate at temperatures below the outdoor air temperature, allowing it to absorb heat even on a cold winter day.

How Does Heating Mode Work?

In heating mode, the heat pump collects heat outdoors and releases it inside the building.

The cycle can be understood in four main stages.

Step 1: The Outdoor Coil Absorbs Heat

Cold, low-pressure refrigerant passes through the outdoor coil.

Because the refrigerant is colder than the outdoor environment, heat flows from the outside air into the refrigerant. A fan moves outdoor air across the coil to improve this heat transfer.

As the refrigerant absorbs energy, it evaporates into a low-pressure vapor.

Even when the outdoor temperature is below freezing, properly designed heat pumps can make the refrigerant cold enough to absorb available heat from the air.

Step 2: The Compressor Raises the Temperature

The refrigerant vapor travels to the compressor.

The compressor squeezes the vapor into a smaller volume, increasing its pressure and temperature. The refrigerant leaving the compressor is a hot, high-pressure vapor.

Electricity powers the compressor, and this electrical work is essential to moving heat from the colder outdoor environment into the warmer building.

Step 3: The Indoor Coil Releases Heat

The hot refrigerant flows through the indoor coil.

Because the refrigerant is hotter than the indoor air, heat transfers from the refrigerant into the building. A fan commonly moves indoor air across the coil and distributes the warmed air through ducts or directly into the room.

As the refrigerant loses heat, it condenses from vapor into a high-pressure liquid.

The heat released indoors includes both energy collected from outside and energy associated with the work performed by the compressor.

Step 4: The Refrigerant's Pressure Drops

The high-pressure liquid passes through an expansion device.

The expansion device creates a large pressure drop. At the lower pressure, the refrigerant becomes much colder, with some of it typically flashing into vapor.

The cold refrigerant then returns to the outdoor coil, where it can absorb more heat.

The cycle repeats continuously while heating is required.

How Does a Heat Pump Cool a House?

In cooling mode, the process is reversed.

The indoor coil becomes the evaporator and absorbs heat from the indoor air. The compressor raises the refrigerant's pressure and temperature, and the outdoor coil releases the collected heat into the outside air.

The heat pump is then operating essentially like a conventional air conditioner.

As the indoor coil cools the air, it can also remove humidity. Water vapor in the indoor air condenses on the cold coil and drains away, which is why air conditioning often makes indoor air feel less humid.

What Does the Reversing Valve Do?

A reversible heat pump contains a reversing valve that changes the direction refrigerant travels through important parts of the system.

This allows the indoor and outdoor coils to exchange functions.

During heating, the outdoor coil absorbs heat and the indoor coil releases it. During cooling, the indoor coil absorbs heat and the outdoor coil releases it.

This ability to reverse the refrigeration cycle is what allows one system to provide both heating and air conditioning.

Why Can a Heat Pump Be More Efficient Than Electric Resistance Heat?

Electric resistance heaters convert electrical energy directly into heat. One unit of electrical energy produces approximately one unit of heat at the point of use.

A heat pump uses electricity primarily to move existing heat rather than generating all of the delivered heat through electrical resistance.

Because it collects additional thermal energy from outside, a heat pump can deliver several units of heat indoors for each unit of electrical energy it consumes under favorable conditions.

This relationship is commonly described by the coefficient of performance, or COP. A heat pump operating at a COP of 3, for example, delivers about three units of heat for every unit of electrical energy supplied to the system.

COP is not constant. It changes with outdoor temperature, equipment design, indoor temperature, operating conditions and other factors.

Does More Than 100 Percent Efficiency Violate the Laws of Physics?

No.

Heat pumps are sometimes described as being more than 100 percent efficient, which can sound impossible.

The confusion comes from comparing a heat pump with a device that converts energy directly into heat. A heat pump is not creating all of the delivered heat from electricity. It is using electrical work to transfer additional heat from another location.

If one unit of electrical energy allows the system to collect two additional units of heat from outside, approximately three units of heat can be delivered indoors.

No energy has been created. Energy has simply been transferred from the outdoor environment into the building, with electrical energy used to make that transfer possible.

Do Heat Pumps Work Below Freezing?

Yes. Modern air-source heat pumps can operate below freezing, and equipment designed for cold climates can continue providing useful heating at temperatures well below 32 degrees Fahrenheit, or 0 degrees Celsius.

However, a heat pump generally has a harder job as outdoor temperatures fall.

The temperature difference between the outdoor environment and the heated indoor space becomes larger, and there is less readily available heat for the refrigerant to collect. The system may need to operate longer or at higher compressor speeds, and its heating capacity and efficiency can decline depending on its design.

The exact low-temperature performance varies considerably among heat pump models. A system should therefore be selected and sized for the climate where it will operate rather than assuming all heat pumps perform identically in extreme cold.

Why Does Frost Form on the Outdoor Unit?

During winter operation, the outdoor coil can become colder than the surrounding air.

If moisture in the air contacts a coil whose surface is below freezing, that moisture can freeze onto the coil and form frost.

A thin layer of frost may be normal, but heavy frost interferes with airflow and acts as insulation, reducing the coil's ability to absorb heat.

Heat pumps therefore need a way to remove accumulated frost.

What Is a Defrost Cycle?

Many air-source heat pumps periodically enter a defrost cycle when frost accumulates on the outdoor coil.

The system temporarily changes operation so that hot refrigerant warms the outdoor coil and melts the ice. During this period, the system may briefly stop delivering normal heat indoors or may use supplemental heat to maintain comfort.

Water and steam around the outdoor unit during defrost can be completely normal. Melted frost drains from the coil, and water vapor can create a visible cloud in cold air.

Once the coil is clear, the heat pump returns to normal heating operation.

Why Can the Air From a Heat Pump Feel Less Hot Than Furnace Air?

A heat pump can maintain a comfortable room temperature while supplying air that feels cooler than the air produced by a high-temperature combustion furnace.

Rather than delivering extremely hot air for short periods, many heat pumps work most efficiently by providing moderate heat for longer periods.

Modern variable-speed systems may run for extended periods at low output instead of repeatedly switching fully on and off.

Long operating times therefore do not automatically mean a heat pump is struggling. Continuous or near-continuous operation can be part of normal efficient operation, particularly during cold weather.

What Is Auxiliary or Backup Heat?

Some heat pump systems include a second source of heat for situations when the heat pump alone cannot meet the building's heating demand.

In an all-electric system, this backup may be electric resistance heating elements. Other installations may use a furnace or another heating system.

Electric resistance backup heat can provide substantial heating capacity but generally consumes more electricity for the same amount of delivered heat than the heat pump itself.

Control systems determine when supplemental heat is necessary based on temperature, heating demand and system design.

Not every heat pump installation requires the same type or amount of backup heat.

What Is a Mini-Split Heat Pump?

A mini-split, or ductless heat pump, uses the same basic refrigeration cycle but does not require a conventional central duct system.

An outdoor unit connects through refrigerant lines to one or more indoor units. Each indoor unit transfers heat directly between the refrigerant and the air in the room or zone it serves.

Mini-splits can provide both heating and cooling and are commonly used for individual rooms, additions, homes without ductwork and buildings divided into separate temperature zones.

Multi-zone systems can connect several indoor units to a single outdoor unit.

What Is a Ground-Source Heat Pump?

Not all heat pumps collect heat from outdoor air.

A ground-source heat pump, sometimes called a geothermal heat pump, exchanges heat with the ground through buried piping.

Underground temperatures several feet below the surface are generally more stable throughout the year than outdoor air temperatures. This can provide favorable conditions for heat exchange in both winter and summer.

Ground-source systems use the same broad principle of moving heat, although their installation and heat-exchange systems differ substantially from ordinary air-source equipment.

What Is a Water-Source Heat Pump?

Some heat pumps exchange heat with water rather than directly with outdoor air or the ground.

Water-source systems are commonly used in certain commercial buildings and other applications where a suitable water loop or water source is available.

Again, the underlying principle remains the same: refrigerant and mechanical equipment move thermal energy from one place to another.

Does a Heat Pump Save Energy in Every Situation?

Heat pumps can be highly energy-efficient, but actual energy use depends on many factors.

Climate, equipment efficiency, building insulation, air leakage, thermostat settings, system sizing, ductwork, installation quality and the use of supplemental heat can all affect performance.

A poorly sized or poorly installed heat pump may perform much worse than a properly designed system.

Energy cost is also different from energy efficiency. A highly efficient heat pump may or may not have the lowest operating cost in every location because electricity and fuel prices vary.

Is a Heat Pump the Same as a Refrigerator?

They operate on the same basic physical principle.

A refrigerator moves heat from inside an insulated cabinet into the surrounding room. A heat pump moves heat between a building and an outside heat source or heat sink.

A reversible heat pump can change direction, moving heat outdoors for cooling or indoors for heating.

In that sense, a refrigerator, air conditioner and heat pump are closely related machines designed for different purposes.

The Bottom Line

A heat pump works by using electricity and a circulating refrigerant to move heat rather than generating all of its heat directly.

During winter, cold refrigerant absorbs thermal energy from the outdoor environment. The compressor raises the refrigerant's pressure and temperature, allowing that energy to be released inside the building. During summer, the cycle reverses and carries heat from indoors to outdoors.

Because a heat pump transfers heat that already exists, it can deliver considerably more heating energy than the electrical energy consumed by the system itself. Modern cold-climate heat pumps can continue operating below freezing, although their capacity and efficiency depend on temperature and equipment design.

Whether it is a central system, a ductless mini-split or a ground-source installation, the fundamental idea is the same: a heat pump uses the refrigeration cycle to move thermal energy in the direction needed to heat or cool a building.
