What Happens to Your Ballot After You Vote?

October 19, 202611 min read

Casting a ballot may take only a few minutes, but the process of turning that vote into an official election result involves several steps. Election officials must protect ballots, count votes accurately, account for eligible ballots, and verify the results before declaring them official.

In the United States, elections are primarily administered by state and local governments. That means the exact procedures vary depending on where someone votes. However, the basic principles are similar: protect the ballots, maintain accurate records, count every eligible vote, and verify the results.

What Happens When You Cast Your Ballot?

The first step depends on the voting system used in your community.

In many jurisdictions, voters mark a paper ballot and feed it into an optical scanner. The scanner reads the marked choices and records the votes electronically. The paper ballot is deposited into a secured container and retained as a physical record.

Other jurisdictions use electronic ballot-marking devices that allow voters to make selections on a screen and produce a paper ballot or record. Depending on the system, the voter may review that paper record before it is scanned or otherwise cast.

Some locations use different voting technologies, but most American voters cast ballots that produce a paper record.

Once a ballot has been accepted, the voting system or election officials account for it as part of the election. The ballot itself is normally kept separate from the voter's identity to preserve the secrecy of the vote.

How Are Ballots Protected?

Election officials use physical security measures, documentation, and controlled access to protect ballots throughout the election.

Ballot containers may be locked or sealed with tamper-evident seals. Authorized election workers document when materials are transferred, who handled them, and where they were stored.

This record is called the chain of custody.

For example, when ballots are transported from a polling location to a central election office, workers may record the container identification, seal numbers, departure and arrival information, and the people responsible for transportation.

At the receiving location, officials can inspect the seals and compare the materials against the documentation.

These procedures help officials identify missing materials, unexplained discrepancies, or possible unauthorized access. They do not guarantee that errors are impossible, but they create records that can be examined when questions arise.

How Are Votes Counted?

Many jurisdictions use optical scanners to count paper ballots. These machines identify marks in designated voting areas and calculate totals for each contest.

Some scanners count ballots at polling places as voters cast them. Other jurisdictions transport ballots to a central facility where they are scanned and counted.

Election officials generally test voting equipment before an election to confirm that it records and counts predetermined test votes correctly. These checks are commonly called logic and accuracy tests.

After voting ends, election workers follow procedures for closing voting equipment, recording vote totals, and securing ballots and electronic records.

The results from individual polling places or counting locations are then combined to produce preliminary totals for the larger election jurisdiction.

The exact method of transferring results depends on state requirements and the voting technology involved.

What Happens to Mail-In and Absentee Ballots?

Mail-in and absentee ballots follow a somewhat different process because election officials must determine whether a returned ballot meets the applicable legal requirements before counting it.

Depending on state law, officials may examine the ballot return envelope, verify identifying information, check a signature, or confirm that the voter has not already cast another ballot in the same election.

Some states allow election workers to begin processing mail ballots before Election Day. Others restrict when envelopes can be opened, ballots prepared, or votes counted.

After the required checks, the ballot is separated from identifying information according to the jurisdiction's procedures. This protects voter privacy while allowing the ballot to be counted.

A ballot that arrives after Election Day is not automatically invalid everywhere. Some states require receipt by Election Day, while others accept qualifying ballots received later if they meet specified mailing deadlines.

These differences are one reason vote totals may continue changing after polls close.

What If a Ballot Cannot Be Read by a Scanner?

Not every ballot can be counted automatically.

A voter might make an unclear mark, damage a ballot, or write in a candidate's name. A scanner may flag a ballot or contest for additional review.

Election workers then follow established rules for determining voter intent. This review is sometimes called ballot adjudication.

For example, if a voter clearly marks one candidate but leaves a stray mark elsewhere, election officials may need to determine whether the ballot contains a valid selection under state law.

If a ballot is damaged and cannot be scanned, some jurisdictions permit trained election workers to create a replacement ballot that accurately reproduces the voter's choices. The original and replacement are documented and preserved according to election procedures.

Election officials cannot simply choose how they think someone intended to vote. They must follow the applicable legal standards, and an unclear vote may remain uncounted for a particular contest if voter intent cannot be determined.

A ballot can still contain valid votes in other contests even when one selection cannot be counted.

What Happens to Provisional Ballots?

A provisional ballot allows someone to cast a ballot when their eligibility cannot immediately be confirmed at the polling place.

This might happen because the person's name does not appear on the voter list, their registration information needs verification, or another eligibility question must be resolved.

Provisional ballots are generally kept separate from regular ballots until election officials determine whether they can legally be counted.

Officials review registration records and other required information. Depending on state law and the circumstances, the entire ballot, certain eligible contests, or none of the ballot may be counted.

This review can take additional time after Election Day.

Why Are Election-Night Results Unofficial?

The numbers shown on television or election websites on election night are preliminary results.

They may include most ballots cast in person, but some eligible ballots may still need to be counted. Mail ballots, provisional ballots, military and overseas ballots, and ballots requiring additional review can affect the totals.

Election officials also need time to check records and resolve discrepancies.

When a news organization announces that a candidate has won, it is generally making a projection based on available information. That announcement is not the legal certification of the election.

Even a report showing that every precinct has submitted results does not necessarily mean every eligible ballot has been counted.

Election-night totals can change as additional ballots are processed and corrections are made. A change in the totals does not, by itself, indicate that anything improper occurred.

What Is the Election Canvass?

After voting, election officials conduct a formal review known as the canvass.

The canvass is the process of collecting, reviewing, and reconciling election records to establish accurate results.

One important part is ballot reconciliation. Officials compare information such as the number of voters recorded as having voted, the number of ballots issued, the number of ballots cast, and the number of ballots counted.

For example, if records show that 1,000 voters cast ballots at a polling location, election officials should be able to account for the corresponding ballots and explain any differences in the records.

Officials also review matters such as spoiled ballots, provisional ballots, eligible write-in votes, and documented equipment or administrative problems.

When discrepancies appear, election workers investigate and document how they were resolved.

The canvass is not simply another announcement of vote totals. It is an important verification process intended to ensure that the official results accurately reflect the eligible votes cast.

How Are Election Results Audited?

Many states require post-election audits to check whether voting equipment and counting procedures produced accurate results.

An audit is different from the ordinary counting process. It independently examines election records or paper ballots to test the accuracy of the reported totals.

One common method involves manually counting votes on a selected sample of paper ballots and comparing those results with the machine totals.

Some jurisdictions use risk-limiting audits. These are statistical procedures designed to provide a specified level of confidence that the reported election outcome is correct. The number of ballots examined can increase if the initial sample does not provide enough evidence.

Other states use different audit methods, including fixed-percentage reviews or audits of selected contests and precincts.

Audit requirements, timing, and methods vary by state. Some audits occur before certification, while others occur afterward.

An audit may reveal discrepancies that require additional examination. It is a verification tool, not a guarantee that every possible problem will be discovered.

What Is the Difference Between an Audit and a Recount?

An audit checks the accuracy of election results or procedures using a defined verification method. It may examine only a sample of ballots.

A recount involves counting votes again in a particular contest to verify or determine the correct total.

Recounts may be required when a race is extremely close, requested by an eligible candidate or voter, or ordered by a court, depending on state law.

Some recounts are conducted by hand, while others use voting equipment or a combination of methods.

A recount can confirm the original results or identify corrections. Whether those corrections change the outcome depends on the votes involved.

The legal requirements for requesting, conducting, and completing recounts differ among states.

When Do Election Results Become Official?

Election results become official through a legal process called certification.

Certification generally occurs after election officials complete the required counting, reconciliation, and other procedures established by law.

The responsible officials or governing board formally attest to the results. Depending on the state and type of election, certification may occur at the local level, the state level, or both.

Different states assign certification responsibilities to different officials, including election boards, secretaries of state, and other designated authorities.

Certification deadlines also vary. Some elections can be certified relatively quickly, while others require more time because of outstanding ballots, audits, recounts, or legal proceedings.

Certification is an official legal action. It is separate from media projections and election-night reporting.

Certain legal challenges or recount procedures may continue after an initial certification, depending on the applicable law.

What Happens to the Paper Ballots After the Election?

Paper ballots are not normally discarded once the results are announced.

Election officials must preserve election materials for legally required retention periods.

For federal elections, federal law generally requires officials to retain records and papers relating to applications, registration, and other acts connected with voting for 22 months after the election. State laws may impose additional requirements or longer retention periods.

Ballots and other election records are stored under security procedures so they remain available for authorized audits, recounts, investigations, or legal proceedings.

After the required retention period expires, election materials may be disposed of according to applicable law and records-management procedures, unless a legal obligation requires continued preservation.

Can Someone Find Out How You Voted?

American elections use the secret ballot to protect voters from coercion and retaliation.

Election officials maintain records showing who participated in an election, but those records do not ordinarily reveal which candidates or ballot measures a person selected.

A person's voting history may indicate that they voted in a particular election. It does not reveal the choices they made on their ballot.

Mail ballot tracking systems may show whether a ballot was sent, received, accepted, or otherwise processed. They are not designed to disclose the voter's selections.

Ballot secrecy is an important reason election systems separate identifying voter information from the ballots being counted.

What Happens If Officials Discover a Problem?

Election procedures include ways to identify, investigate, and address mistakes or irregularities.

Problems might involve missing documentation, equipment malfunctions, inconsistent ballot totals, or questions about whether certain ballots qualify for counting.

Election officials review the available evidence and follow applicable legal procedures. Depending on the issue, they may correct records, examine ballots, conduct additional counting, perform an audit, or refer the matter for investigation.

Some disputes are resolved through administrative procedures. Others may require a court to determine how election law applies.

Not every discrepancy indicates fraud. Human errors, damaged ballots, reporting mistakes, and differences in processing schedules can produce discrepancies. At the same time, documented evidence of misconduct or unlawful activity must be investigated under the appropriate procedures.

The purpose of verification and oversight is to identify problems and establish an accurate, legally valid result.

The Bottom Line

A ballot does not simply disappear into a machine when someone votes.

Election officials use procedures to secure ballots, document their handling, count votes, and compare the totals against election records. Additional checks, including canvassing, audits, and recounts when applicable, help verify the accuracy of the results.

Only after the required legal steps are completed are election results formally certified.

The process can take days or weeks, depending on the election and state laws. That time allows election officials to account for eligible ballots, resolve discrepancies, and establish the official results.
