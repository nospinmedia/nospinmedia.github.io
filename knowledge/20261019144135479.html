Why Does It Take Days to Count Election Results?

October 20, 202611 min read

On election night, millions of Americans watch as vote totals appear on television and election websites. In some races, a winner becomes apparent within hours. In others, the outcome remains uncertain for days.

This can seem surprising in a country that uses computers and electronic ballot scanners to count votes. If machines can count thousands of ballots quickly, why can't every election be decided on the same night?

The answer is that counting votes is only one part of an election. Officials must also determine which ballots are eligible, process ballots received through different voting methods, check their records, resolve discrepancies, and complete procedures required by state law.

A delayed result does not automatically mean something has gone wrong. In many cases, it means election officials are still completing the normal legal process.

Election-Night Results Are Preliminary

The first thing to understand is that election-night results are not official results.

The numbers displayed on election websites and reported by news organizations are preliminary totals. They represent ballots that have been counted and reported at that point in time.

Some jurisdictions report most of their votes shortly after polls close. Others release results in stages as additional ballots are processed.

A news organization may project a winner before every ballot has been counted if its analysis indicates that the remaining votes cannot reasonably change the outcome. That projection is a journalistic determination, not an official government certification.

The official result is established later through procedures required by election law.

Why Aren't All Ballots Counted on Election Night?

Americans can vote in several ways, depending on their state's laws.

Some vote in person on Election Day. Others vote early, submit absentee or mail-in ballots, or cast provisional ballots when their eligibility requires additional verification.

Each type of ballot may follow a different processing schedule.

For example, ballots cast in person using optical scanners may be counted immediately. Mail-in ballots may require officials to examine return envelopes and verify eligibility before the ballots can be counted.

Some states permit election workers to begin processing mail ballots before Election Day. Others prohibit certain processing or counting activities until Election Day itself.

These legal differences can have a major effect on how quickly results become available.

Mail-In Ballots Require Additional Processing

Mail-in and absentee ballots often require more administrative work than ballots cast in person.

Before counting a returned ballot, election officials must determine whether it satisfies applicable legal requirements.

Depending on the state, that process may include checking the voter's registration, examining a signature, confirming required identifying information, or verifying that the person has not already voted in the election.

Officials may also need to remove ballots from envelopes, flatten folded ballot papers, and prepare them for scanning.

These tasks can involve large numbers of ballots and must follow established security and documentation procedures.

In states where early processing is restricted, much of this work cannot begin until Election Day or another legally specified time.

As a result, a jurisdiction with hundreds of thousands of mail ballots may need considerable time to complete the count.

Some Ballots Can Legally Arrive After Election Day

Not every state requires every ballot to physically arrive by the time polls close.

Some states accept qualifying mail ballots that arrive after Election Day if they were mailed by a specified deadline. Other states generally require ballots to be received by Election Day.

Military personnel and American citizens living overseas may also have special voting protections and deadlines under federal and state law.

Election officials must follow the rules that apply to each ballot category.

This means some legally eligible ballots may not be available to count on election night.

The fact that a ballot is counted after Election Day does not necessarily mean it was cast after the legal voting deadline.

What Are Provisional Ballots?

A provisional ballot allows a person to vote when election workers cannot immediately confirm that the person is eligible to cast a regular ballot.

For example, the voter's name may be missing from the registration list, or there may be a question about registration information or identification requirements.

Instead of immediately rejecting the voter, election officials can provide a provisional ballot under applicable law.

That ballot is generally kept separate while officials investigate the eligibility question.

After reviewing the relevant records, officials determine whether the ballot can be counted. In some circumstances, only votes for contests in which the voter was eligible to participate may be included.

Because these decisions require additional review, provisional ballots may not be counted until several days after Election Day.

Why Do Vote Totals Change After Election Night?

Election-night totals are updated as additional ballots are counted and reported.

Suppose a county reports 80,000 votes on election night. Over the following days, officials process another 12,000 eligible mail ballots and 1,000 provisional ballots.

The county's reported total would increase to 93,000 votes, assuming all those additional ballots are counted in the relevant contest.

Nothing about that increase is inherently suspicious. The original total simply did not include every eligible vote.

Reported totals can also change when officials correct administrative or reporting errors.

For example, a precinct's results might have been entered incorrectly into a reporting system, or a batch of votes might need to be reconciled against election records.

Corrections should follow documented procedures and applicable law.

The important distinction is between a preliminary number and a verified official total.

Why Can One Candidate Gain More Votes Than Another During Later Counting?

Different groups of voters do not necessarily vote in the same proportions.

For example, voters who cast ballots in person on Election Day may have different preferences from voters who use mail ballots.

If one category is counted and reported earlier than another, the apparent lead can change as more ballots are added.

Imagine an election in which the first 100,000 counted votes favor Candidate A by 55 percent to 45 percent.

Later, another 100,000 eligible votes are counted, but those votes favor Candidate B by 60 percent to 40 percent.

The combined result would be 95,000 votes for Candidate A and 105,000 for Candidate B.

Candidate B would move ahead even though Candidate A led in the earlier reports.

This is a mathematical consequence of counting different groups of ballots at different times.

Such changes do not, by themselves, establish that anything improper occurred. Determining whether a specific election had a problem requires examining evidence and the applicable procedures.

What Is the Difference Between Counting and Reporting Votes?

Counting votes and reporting results are separate activities.

A jurisdiction may have already counted a group of ballots but not yet included those votes in its publicly reported totals.

Election officials may need to complete internal checks, reconcile records, or prepare an authorized results report before releasing an update.

Different counties and states also use different reporting schedules.

Some release frequent updates throughout election night. Others publish results in larger batches at designated times.

As a result, a sudden increase in reported votes may represent ballots counted over several hours rather than votes that were all counted at the moment the update appeared.

Public reporting systems do not necessarily display every step of the underlying counting process in real time.

Why Do Some States Finish Faster Than Others?

Election administration is primarily the responsibility of state and local governments, and the rules differ substantially across the country.

Several factors influence how quickly results become available:

  • Whether mail ballots can be processed before Election Day.
  • How many voters use mail or absentee ballots.
  • Whether qualifying ballots can arrive after Election Day.
  • How provisional ballots are reviewed.
  • The number of contests and ballots requiring additional examination.
  • Staffing levels, equipment, and the size of the jurisdiction.
  • State requirements for reconciliation, canvassing, and certification.

A state that permits extensive early processing of mail ballots may report results more quickly than one that requires most processing to begin on Election Day.

A smaller election jurisdiction may also finish sooner simply because it has fewer ballots to handle.

Speed alone is not a reliable measure of election accuracy or security.

What Happens When a Ballot Has a Problem?

Some ballots cannot be counted automatically without additional examination.

A voter may mark more choices than allowed in a contest, make an unclear mark, or damage the ballot.

Election officials must follow legal standards for determining whether the voter's intent can be established.

This process is often called ballot adjudication.

In some circumstances, a damaged ballot may need to be duplicated onto a new ballot so that it can be scanned. The original ballot and replacement are documented according to the jurisdiction's procedures.

A ballot with an invalid selection in one contest may still contain valid votes in other contests.

These reviews help prevent eligible votes from being unnecessarily excluded, but they also take time.

What Is the Election Canvass?

After Election Day, officials conduct a formal process called the canvass.

The canvass involves reviewing election returns, accounting for ballots, reconciling records, and establishing the results that will be submitted for certification.

Election officials may compare the number of people recorded as voting with the number of ballots issued, cast, and counted.

They also review matters such as spoiled ballots, provisional ballots, and documented discrepancies.

If the records do not match as expected, officials investigate and attempt to explain the differences.

The canvass is an important part of verifying the election. It is not simply a repetition of the election-night announcement.

The time required for canvassing depends on state law, the number of ballots involved, and whether problems need to be resolved.

Can Audits and Recounts Delay Final Results?

Yes. Some elections require additional verification before or after certification.

An election audit checks whether the reported results accurately reflect the underlying ballots or election records. Some audits examine a sample of paper ballots and compare them with machine-counted totals.

A recount involves counting votes again in a particular contest.

State laws determine when recounts are automatic, when they may be requested, and how they must be conducted.

A particularly close election may remain unresolved while a recount is completed.

Legal challenges can also affect the timeline if a court must decide questions about ballot eligibility, counting procedures, or election administration.

These processes are governed by legal rules and do not automatically indicate fraud or misconduct.

What Does It Mean to Certify an Election?

Certification is the formal legal process through which designated election authorities confirm the official results.

Before certification, officials must complete the procedures required by their jurisdiction, which generally include canvassing and resolving matters that must be addressed under state law.

The responsible authority then formally certifies the results.

Depending on the election, certification may involve local election boards, state election officials, or other designated authorities.

Each state establishes its own certification deadlines and procedures within applicable federal requirements.

Certification is different from a television network projecting a winner. A projected winner may be widely recognized days before the results become legally official.

Certain recounts or legal proceedings may continue after initial certification, depending on the circumstances and applicable law.

Does a Slow Count Mean an Election Is Unsecure?

No. The amount of time required to count an election does not, by itself, establish whether the election was secure or insecure.

A slow count may result from large numbers of mail ballots, statutory deadlines, required eligibility checks, or careful reconciliation of election records.

Likewise, a fast count does not automatically prove that every procedure was followed correctly.

Election security and accuracy must be evaluated through evidence, including documented procedures, ballot accounting, audits, investigations, and other applicable safeguards.

Delays can sometimes result from genuine problems, including equipment failures, administrative errors, or disputes requiring investigation.

The existence of a delay alone does not identify its cause.

Why Can Election Results Be Known Before They Are Official?

A candidate can have a lead large enough that the remaining uncounted ballots are unlikely to change the outcome.

News organizations may use reported vote totals, information about outstanding ballots, and statistical analysis to project a winner.

However, those projections are not legally binding.

Election officials must continue processing eligible ballots and completing the required verification steps even when the outcome appears clear.

The official result is determined through the election process established by law, not by the timing of a media announcement.

The Bottom Line

Counting election results takes time because election officials are responsible for more than adding up numbers.

They must process ballots submitted through different voting methods, verify eligibility where required, examine ballots that need additional review, reconcile election records, and complete legally required checks before certification.

Vote totals often change after election night because additional eligible ballots are being counted or preliminary reporting errors are being corrected.

A delay does not automatically indicate fraud, misconduct, or a failure of election security. Understanding whether a particular delay is routine or problematic requires examining its actual cause.

The goal of the process is not simply to announce a winner as quickly as possible. It is to produce an accurate, verifiable, and legally valid election result.
