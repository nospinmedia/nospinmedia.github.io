What Is Regenerative Braking?

October 8, 202615 min read

Regenerative braking is a system used in electric and hybrid vehicles that recovers some of the energy normally lost when a vehicle slows down.

In a conventional vehicle, friction brakes slow the wheels by pressing brake pads against rotating discs or drums. The vehicle's kinetic energy is converted primarily into heat, which is released into the surrounding air.

An electric vehicle can do something different. Its electric motor can temporarily operate as a generator. The moving wheels drive the motor, electrical energy is produced, and some of that energy is sent back to the battery.

The process simultaneously slows the vehicle and recovers energy that would otherwise have been largely wasted as heat.

Where Does the Energy Come From?

A moving vehicle possesses kinetic energy, which is the energy associated with motion.

The faster and heavier the vehicle is, the more kinetic energy it has.

When any vehicle slows down, that energy must go somewhere. It cannot simply disappear.

With ordinary friction braking, most of the vehicle's kinetic energy becomes heat in the brakes.

With regenerative braking, some of the kinetic energy is instead converted into electrical energy and stored for later use.

This is why regenerative braking can improve the overall efficiency of an electric or hybrid vehicle.

How Can an Electric Motor Become a Generator?

Electric motors and electrical generators operate using closely related electromagnetic principles.

During normal acceleration, electrical energy is supplied to the motor. Magnetic fields inside the motor create torque, causing the motor's rotor to turn and drive the wheels.

During regenerative braking, the energy flow reverses.

The moving vehicle causes the wheels to continue rotating the motor. The motor's electromagnetic system then produces electrical energy instead of consuming it to produce propulsion.

The motor is effectively operating as a generator.

This conversion also creates torque opposing the rotation of the wheels. That opposing torque slows the vehicle.

What Happens When You Lift Off the Accelerator?

In many electric vehicles, releasing the accelerator immediately commands some amount of regenerative braking.

The vehicle's power electronics change the motor's operation so that it begins generating electricity.

The wheels drive the motor, the motor produces electrical power, and the vehicle slows.

How strongly this occurs depends on the vehicle and its settings.

Some EVs provide only mild deceleration when the accelerator is released. Others allow the driver to select stronger regenerative braking levels.

Some vehicles automatically vary regeneration according to traffic, navigation information, road conditions or other factors.

What Happens When You Press the Brake Pedal?

Pressing the brake pedal in many electrified vehicles does not necessarily mean the friction brakes immediately perform all of the braking.

The vehicle's control system can first request regenerative braking from the electric motor.

If additional braking is required, conventional friction brakes are added.

This combination is called blended braking.

The vehicle continuously determines how much braking can safely and effectively come from regeneration and how much must come from the friction brakes.

Ideally, the transition feels natural to the driver even though two different braking systems are working together.

Does Regenerative Braking Recharge the Battery?

Yes.

Electricity generated during regenerative braking can be returned to the high-voltage traction battery.

The inverter and other power electronics control the electrical energy flowing from the motor toward the battery. The battery-management system determines how much charging power the battery can safely accept.

The recovered energy can later be sent back through the drivetrain to help accelerate the vehicle again.

Regenerative braking therefore allows some energy to be reused instead of being lost entirely during every slowdown.

Can Regenerative Braking Recover All the Energy Used to Accelerate?

No.

No real energy-conversion system is perfectly efficient.

Energy is lost when electricity moves through the battery, inverter, motor, wiring and mechanical drivetrain. Additional energy is lost through tire deformation, aerodynamic drag, bearings, heating and other processes.

The battery also loses some energy while being charged and discharged.

As a result, regenerative braking can recover only part of the kinetic energy available during deceleration.

The vehicle cannot accelerate using energy from the battery and then recover all of that energy simply by braking.

Doing so would require a perfectly efficient system with no losses, which is not physically achievable.

How Much Energy Can Regenerative Braking Recover?

There is no single percentage that applies to every vehicle or driving situation.

The amount depends on vehicle speed, mass, motor capability, battery condition, temperature, state of charge and how strongly the vehicle must slow down.

Driving conditions also matter greatly.

Regenerative braking is particularly useful in city traffic, where vehicles repeatedly accelerate and decelerate.

On a highway where a vehicle maintains a nearly constant speed for long periods, there may be relatively few opportunities to recover braking energy.

The best efficiency does not come from maximizing regeneration. It comes from avoiding unnecessary acceleration and braking in the first place. Maintaining momentum generally uses less energy than accelerating and then recovering only part of that energy through regeneration.

Why Is Regenerative Braking Useful in City Driving?

Stop-and-go traffic requires repeated changes in speed.

A conventional vehicle repeatedly converts fuel energy into motion and then converts much of that motion into brake heat whenever it stops.

An electric or hybrid vehicle can recover part of the energy during each slowdown.

The recovered electricity can then help power the next acceleration.

This is one reason electric vehicles can be particularly efficient in urban driving and why hybrids can achieve substantial fuel savings in stop-and-go traffic.

What Is One-Pedal Driving?

One-pedal driving uses relatively strong regenerative braking when the driver releases the accelerator.

Pressing the accelerator makes the vehicle speed up. Gradually releasing it causes the vehicle to slow, often strongly enough to handle much of normal traffic without pressing the brake pedal.

Some systems can use regeneration and other braking controls to bring the vehicle all the way to a stop.

The driver still has a conventional brake pedal for stronger braking and emergencies.

One-pedal driving does not mean the vehicle has only one pedal. It means much of routine acceleration and deceleration can be controlled with the accelerator pedal.

Does One-Pedal Driving Recover More Energy?

Not automatically.

Strong regeneration can recover more energy than friction braking when the vehicle must slow down, but slowing unnecessarily still wastes energy compared with simply maintaining momentum.

For example, if a driver accelerates and then immediately uses strong regenerative braking, only part of the energy used for acceleration is recovered.

A driver who anticipates traffic and avoids the unnecessary acceleration may use less energy overall.

Regeneration is best thought of as energy recovery, not free energy.

Does Regenerative Braking Work at Every Speed?

Regenerative braking capability changes with motor speed and vehicle design.

At very low speeds, the motor may not be able to provide enough regenerative braking to bring the vehicle smoothly to a complete stop.

Some vehicles therefore blend in friction brakes as speed approaches zero. Others use motor-control techniques that can provide stronger low-speed control.

The exact behavior differs among vehicles.

This is one reason regenerative braking and conventional friction braking are normally used together rather than relying exclusively on the motor.

Why Is Regenerative Braking Sometimes Limited?

The vehicle cannot always accept the maximum possible regenerative power.

One major limitation is the battery.

Regenerative braking is effectively a form of rapid battery charging. If the battery is unable to accept the generated power safely, the vehicle must reduce regeneration.

The system can also limit regeneration because of motor temperature, inverter temperature, traction conditions or other component limits.

When regeneration is reduced, the friction brakes provide more of the required stopping force.

Why Is Regenerative Braking Reduced When the Battery Is Full?

A nearly full battery has limited room to accept additional energy.

The battery-management system must prevent individual cells from exceeding their maximum safe voltage.

If the battery is at or near its upper charge limit, the vehicle may substantially reduce regenerative braking because there is nowhere appropriate to store all of the generated electrical energy.

The vehicle then relies more heavily on friction brakes.

This is why an EV can sometimes feel different when driven immediately after being charged to a very high state of charge.

Some vehicles display a message or indicator showing that regenerative braking is temporarily limited.

Why Can Cold Weather Reduce Regenerative Braking?

Lithium-ion batteries cannot always accept high charging power when they are cold.

At low temperatures, ion movement and other electrochemical processes inside the battery slow down. Attempting to charge too aggressively under unfavorable conditions can contribute to battery damage.

The battery-management system may therefore restrict regenerative charging until the battery warms.

This means an EV driven on a very cold morning may initially provide less regenerative braking than usual.

As the battery reaches a more suitable temperature, normal regenerative capability can return.

Some vehicles can imitate the usual deceleration feel by automatically applying friction brakes when regeneration is unavailable, while others allow the driver to notice the difference more directly.

What Happens During Hard Emergency Braking?

Regenerative braking alone is generally not sufficient for the strongest possible stops.

During emergency braking, the vehicle uses its conventional friction brakes to provide whatever braking force is needed beyond the regenerative system's capability.

Modern braking systems can coordinate regenerative braking, hydraulic friction brakes, anti-lock braking and electronic stability control extremely quickly.

The priority during an emergency is stopping and maintaining vehicle control, not maximizing energy recovery.

If regeneration must be reduced to preserve traction or braking stability, the system sacrifices energy recovery in favor of safety.

How Does Traction Affect Regenerative Braking?

Regenerative braking applies torque to the driven wheels, just as acceleration does, although the torque acts in the opposite direction.

If the road is slippery, excessive regenerative braking could cause a tire to lose traction.

Vehicle stability and anti-lock braking systems therefore monitor wheel behavior and can reduce regenerative torque when necessary.

On snow, ice, loose gravel or other low-traction surfaces, the vehicle may rely more heavily on carefully controlled friction braking or reduce overall braking torque at a particular wheel.

Modern EV control systems coordinate these actions automatically.

Do Brake Lights Come On During Regenerative Braking?

They can.

When regenerative braking produces sufficient vehicle deceleration, electric vehicles are designed to illuminate their brake lights according to applicable vehicle regulations and control strategies, even if the driver has not pressed the brake pedal.

This is particularly important with strong one-pedal driving because releasing the accelerator can slow an EV much more rapidly than ordinary engine braking in many conventional vehicles.

Drivers behind the vehicle therefore receive a brake-light warning when the deceleration is significant enough to require it.

Exact brake-light behavior depends on vehicle design and regulatory requirements.

Does Regenerative Braking Save the Brake Pads?

Usually, yes.

Because the electric motor handles some of the vehicle's routine deceleration, the friction brakes can be used less frequently and less intensely.

Brake pads and discs can therefore last much longer than they might in a comparable conventional vehicle.

However, reduced brake use can create a different problem.

Brake discs are exposed to moisture, road salt and dirt. Regular friction braking helps clean their surfaces. If the mechanical brakes are rarely used, corrosion can sometimes develop more readily.

Manufacturers can address this through brake-system design and software that occasionally uses the friction brakes when appropriate.

Friction brakes still require inspection even on vehicles that rely heavily on regeneration.

Do Hybrid Vehicles Use Regenerative Braking?

Yes.

Regenerative braking became an important efficiency feature of hybrid vehicles before modern battery-electric vehicles became widespread.

A hybrid's electric motor can operate as a generator during deceleration and store recovered energy in the hybrid battery.

That energy can later assist the gasoline engine or propel the vehicle electrically, depending on the hybrid design.

Because hybrid batteries are generally much smaller than battery-electric vehicle packs, their ability to accept regenerative energy can sometimes become limited more quickly.

Regeneration is nevertheless a major reason hybrids can achieve particularly good efficiency in stop-and-go traffic.

Can Regenerative Braking Work Going Down a Mountain?

Yes, and long downhill grades can provide substantial opportunities for regeneration.

Gravity causes the vehicle to lose gravitational potential energy as it descends. Regenerative braking can convert part of that energy into electricity while controlling the vehicle's speed.

This can reduce reliance on friction brakes and return energy to the battery.

However, regeneration has limits.

If the battery becomes too full, becomes too hot or cannot accept the required charging power, regenerative braking may be reduced.

The vehicle must then rely more heavily on its friction brakes or other available braking strategies.

Drivers should therefore never assume that regenerative braking makes conventional brakes unnecessary on a long descent.

Can Regenerative Braking Add Driving Range?

Yes, in the sense that recovered energy can be reused later and therefore reduce the amount of net energy taken from the battery.

Suppose an EV descends a long hill. Some of the vehicle's gravitational potential energy can be converted into electricity and stored in the battery. The vehicle might reach the bottom displaying slightly more available battery energy than it had during part of the descent.

In normal driving, regeneration can similarly extend range compared with a vehicle that throws away all braking energy as heat.

But regenerative braking cannot create energy. Any energy recovered ultimately came from an earlier source, such as electricity used to accelerate the vehicle or gravitational potential energy gained by climbing to a higher elevation.

Why Can't a Car Recharge Itself Forever With Regenerative Braking?

A self-recharging EV that powered itself indefinitely through regenerative braking would violate conservation of energy.

The motor requires energy to accelerate the vehicle. When the vehicle later slows, regeneration can recover only a portion of that energy.

Some energy has already been lost through aerodynamic drag, tire resistance, electrical resistance, battery losses, motor losses and other processes.

Regeneration itself also has conversion losses.

Each accelerate-and-regenerate cycle therefore returns less energy than was originally used.

The vehicle must eventually receive new energy from an external source such as the electrical grid.

Does Regenerative Braking Make the Motor Wear Out Faster?

Regeneration uses the same basic electromagnetic hardware involved in propulsion, and electric motors are designed to operate under both driving and generating conditions.

Unlike friction brakes, regenerative braking does not depend on rubbing two surfaces together to create braking force.

There is therefore no equivalent regenerative brake pad that is consumed each time the motor slows the vehicle.

The motor, bearings, inverter, cooling system and other drivetrain components still experience normal operating stresses and can wear or fail over time, but regenerative braking itself is a normal designed operating mode rather than an abnormal use of the motor.

Is Regenerative Braking the Same as Engine Braking?

They can feel similar to the driver, but they work differently.

In a conventional vehicle, engine braking slows the vehicle because the drivetrain forces the internal-combustion engine to turn while the engine is not producing significant propulsion. Mechanical pumping, compression and friction create resistance.

Most of the vehicle's kinetic energy ultimately becomes heat.

Regenerative braking also creates resistance through the drivetrain, but the electric motor converts part of the kinetic energy into useful electrical energy.

The key difference is therefore energy recovery.

Is Regenerative Braking More Efficient Than Coasting?

If the vehicle does not need to slow down, coasting or maintaining momentum is generally more efficient than deliberately using regenerative braking.

Every conversion of energy involves losses.

Using electricity to accelerate, converting motion back into electricity through regeneration and then using that electricity to accelerate again loses more energy than avoiding the unnecessary speed change.

Regenerative braking becomes valuable when the vehicle needs to slow down anyway.

In that situation, recovering part of the kinetic energy is more efficient than converting all of it into heat with friction brakes.

What Determines How Strong Regenerative Braking Feels?

The vehicle manufacturer controls regenerative braking largely through software.

The system can command different amounts of generator torque when the driver releases the accelerator or presses the brake pedal.

Some vehicles provide selectable regeneration levels. Others automatically determine the amount of regeneration.

Driving modes may also change the response.

Two EVs using similar motors can therefore feel very different when their accelerators are released because their software has been designed with different braking behavior.

Battery and traction limitations can also change the available regenerative force from one moment to another.

The Bottom Line

Regenerative braking allows an electric or hybrid vehicle to recover some of the energy it would otherwise lose while slowing down.

During acceleration, electricity from the battery powers an electric motor, and the motor turns the wheels. During regenerative braking, that process partially reverses: the moving wheels drive the motor, the motor acts as a generator, and electrical energy flows back toward the battery.

Generating electricity creates opposing torque that slows the vehicle, allowing the motor to perform part of the job normally handled entirely by friction brakes.

Regeneration cannot recover all of the vehicle's kinetic energy, and it cannot replace conventional brakes. Battery limits, cold temperatures, a nearly full battery, low speeds, hard braking and poor traction can all reduce the amount of regenerative braking available.

When slowing is necessary, however, regenerative braking is much more useful than simply turning all of the vehicle's motion into brake heat. The recovered energy can be stored and used again during later acceleration.

That ability to repeatedly turn motion back into electricity is one of the major reasons electric and hybrid vehicles can use energy so efficiently, especially in stop-and-go driving.
