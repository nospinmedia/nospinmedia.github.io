How Does the U.S. Court System Work?

The United States has one of the world's largest and most complex court systems. Rather than operating as a single nationwide court, it consists of two separate systems: state courts and federal courts. Each has its own responsibilities, judges, and procedures.

Most legal disputes are handled in state courts, while federal courts hear cases involving federal laws, the U.S. Constitution, disputes between states, and certain other matters defined by federal law. Understanding which court hears a case helps explain why different lawsuits often follow different paths through the legal system.

Why Are There Two Court Systems?

The United States is a federal republic, meaning power is shared between the federal government and the states.

As a result, every state operates its own court system while the federal government maintains a separate national court system.

Although both systems interpret and apply the law, they generally handle different types of cases.

How Do State Courts Work?

State courts handle the vast majority of legal cases in the United States.

These include:

  • Criminal prosecutions under state law.
  • Family law matters such as divorce and child custody.
  • Probate and wills.
  • Traffic violations.
  • Most civil lawsuits.
  • Property disputes.
  • Contract disputes.

Each state organizes its courts somewhat differently, but most follow a similar structure.

Trial Courts

Most cases begin in a trial court. This is where evidence is presented, witnesses testify, and a judge—or sometimes a jury—determines the facts of the case.

Intermediate Appeals Courts

If one side believes legal errors affected the outcome, it may appeal to an intermediate appellate court. These courts generally do not hear new evidence. Instead, they review the trial court's proceedings to determine whether the law was applied correctly.

State Supreme Court

The highest court in most states is the state supreme court. It reviews selected appeals involving important legal questions or disputes about state law. Not every appeal is accepted for review.

How Do Federal Courts Work?

Federal courts hear cases involving federal law or issues specifically assigned to the federal government by the U.S. Constitution.

Examples include:

  • Federal criminal cases.
  • Constitutional disputes.
  • Civil rights claims.
  • Federal regulatory matters.
  • Bankruptcy cases.
  • Lawsuits involving the federal government.
  • Certain disputes between citizens of different states.

Federal courts are organized into three primary levels.

U.S. District Courts

District courts are the federal trial courts.

This is where lawsuits begin, evidence is presented, and juries hear criminal and many civil cases.

There are 94 federal judicial districts across the United States.

U.S. Courts of Appeals

If a party believes the district court made a legal error, the case may be appealed to one of the 13 U.S. Courts of Appeals, often called the circuit courts.

Appeals are usually decided by a panel of three judges who review the legal issues without conducting a new trial.

U.S. Supreme Court

The Supreme Court of the United States is the nation's highest court.

It has the final authority to interpret the U.S. Constitution and federal law.

Each year, thousands of parties ask the Supreme Court to hear their appeals, but the Court accepts only a small percentage of those requests. Most federal and state court decisions remain final without Supreme Court review.

How Does a Case Reach the Supreme Court?

Most cases never reach the Supreme Court.

A typical federal case follows this path:

  1. U.S. District Court
  2. U.S. Court of Appeals
  3. Petition to the U.S. Supreme Court

The Supreme Court generally chooses cases that involve:

  • Important constitutional questions.
  • Conflicting decisions among lower courts.
  • Significant questions of federal law.
  • Issues with broad national importance.

The Court usually decides whether to hear a case through a process known as granting or denying certiorari. If certiorari is denied, the lower court's decision remains in effect.

What Is the Difference Between Criminal and Civil Cases?

Courts hear two broad categories of cases.

Criminal Cases

Criminal cases involve allegations that someone violated a criminal law.

The government prosecutes the defendant, and penalties may include fines, probation, or imprisonment.

Examples include:

  • Murder.
  • Assault.
  • Robbery.
  • Fraud.
  • Drug offenses.

Civil Cases

Civil cases involve disputes between individuals, businesses, or government entities.

Rather than determining criminal guilt, the court decides issues such as liability, financial damages, or legal rights.

Examples include:

  • Personal injury lawsuits.
  • Contract disputes.
  • Employment discrimination claims.
  • Property disagreements.
  • Consumer protection lawsuits.

What Do Judges and Juries Do?

Judges oversee court proceedings, interpret the law, rule on legal questions, and ensure trials follow established procedures.

In many cases, a jury determines the facts by evaluating the evidence presented during trial.

Some cases, known as bench trials, are decided by a judge without a jury.

Why Are Appeals Important?

An appeal is not a second trial.

Instead, appellate courts review whether legal mistakes may have affected the outcome of the original case.

Appeals commonly examine issues such as:

  • Whether evidence was properly admitted.
  • Whether the judge correctly interpreted the law.
  • Whether constitutional rights were violated.
  • Whether proper legal procedures were followed.

If an appellate court finds significant legal errors, it may uphold the decision, reverse it, order a new trial, or send the case back to the lower court for additional proceedings.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that every case can be appealed indefinitely. In reality, appeals are limited by law, and higher courts often decline to hear additional appeals.

Another misunderstanding is that the Supreme Court must hear every important case. In fact, the Court has broad discretion over most of the appeals it accepts and hears only a small fraction of the petitions filed each year.

Finally, not every case involving the Constitution begins in federal court. Many constitutional issues first arise in state courts and may later be reviewed by the federal judiciary if federal constitutional questions remain.

Conclusion

The U.S. court system is built on two parallel judicial systems—state and federal—each with its own responsibilities and structure. Most cases begin in trial courts, while appeals focus on whether the law was correctly applied rather than retrying the facts. At the top of the federal system, the U.S. Supreme Court serves as the final interpreter of the Constitution and federal law, helping ensure that the nation's laws are applied consistently across the country.
