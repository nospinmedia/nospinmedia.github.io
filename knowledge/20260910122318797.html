How Does the Internet Travel Across Oceans?

September 30, 202613 min read

When you send a message, stream a video or open a website located on another continent, the information usually does not travel through a satellite. Most international internet traffic crosses oceans through fiber-optic cables installed on or beneath the seafloor.

Thousands of miles of submarine cable connect continents and islands into a worldwide communications network. Inside these cables, digital information travels primarily as pulses of light through extremely thin strands of glass fiber.

A request can cross an ocean in a fraction of a second, enter another country's terrestrial network and reach a data center or other destination. The response then travels back through interconnected networks, sometimes using a different route.

What Is a Submarine Communications Cable?

A submarine communications cable is a cable specifically designed to carry telecommunications signals across bodies of water.

Modern long-distance submarine systems use optical fiber as their primary communications medium. The glass fibers carrying the signals are surrounded by protective materials, electrical conductors and structural layers that allow the cable to survive deployment and decades of operation in the ocean environment.

These systems can stretch thousands of miles between landing stations on different continents.

The cables form some of the most important physical infrastructure underlying the global internet.

How Does Information Travel Through Fiber-Optic Cable?

Computers handle information digitally. Before information travels through an optical fiber, networking and optical communications equipment convert the data into signals carried by light.

Lasers transmit carefully controlled optical signals into extremely thin glass fibers.

The light remains guided along the fiber because of the optical properties of the fiber's core and surrounding cladding. A principle involving total internal reflection is often used to explain this behavior, although modern fiber propagation is more precisely described using electromagnetic waveguide principles.

At the receiving end, optical equipment detects the incoming light and converts the encoded signals back into electrical and digital information that networking equipment can process.

This conversion can happen repeatedly as information moves between optical networks, routers, data centers and other systems.

Is Internet Data Literally Traveling at the Speed of Light?

The optical signal does travel extremely fast, but not at the speed of light in a vacuum.

Light travels more slowly through glass than through empty space. In typical optical fiber, its propagation speed is roughly two-thirds of the speed of light in a vacuum.

The route also is rarely a perfectly straight line between two people or computers. Cables follow practical paths across the seafloor, and data may pass through routers and other network equipment along the way.

These factors contribute to latency, the delay between sending information and receiving a response.

Even so, fiber-optic communication is extraordinarily fast. An optical signal can cross the Atlantic Ocean in only a few tens of milliseconds, although the total round-trip delay experienced by an internet application will be higher because of the complete route and network processing involved.

Where Do Undersea Cables Reach Land?

Submarine cables connect to shore at facilities called cable landing stations.

Near the coastline, the cable is brought ashore through carefully selected routes. At the landing station, submarine cable equipment connects the undersea system to terrestrial fiber networks.

From there, internet traffic can travel through national and regional networks toward cities, data centers, internet exchanges, businesses and homes.

A single international internet connection therefore may use many different physical networks during one journey.

The submarine cable is the ocean-crossing portion of a much larger end-to-end path.

What Is Inside an Undersea Internet Cable?

The actual optical fibers are remarkably thin, but a submarine cable contains much more than glass fiber.

The fibers are protected by layers designed to provide strength, insulation and resistance to water and physical damage. The cable also contains a metallic conductor used to deliver electrical power to equipment installed along the submarine system.

Cable construction varies depending on where it will be installed.

In the deep ocean, where the risk of damage from ships and fishing activity is relatively low, cables can be comparatively thin.

Near shore, where cables face greater threats from anchors, fishing equipment and other activity, additional steel armor and protective layers can make them much thicker and heavier.

Why Does an Optical Cable Need Electricity?

Light traveling through optical fiber gradually becomes weaker over long distances.

A transoceanic cable can be thousands of miles long, far beyond the distance an optical signal could travel at useful strength without assistance.

Submarine cable systems therefore contain devices called repeaters or optical amplifiers at intervals along the route. Modern systems commonly use optical amplification to strengthen the optical signals without converting every signal all the way back into ordinary digital electrical data at each amplifier location.

These amplifiers require electrical power.

Landing stations supply high-voltage direct-current power through conductors in the submarine cable. The electrical circuit powers the repeaters positioned along the cable on the seafloor.

The fiber carries the communications signals while the electrical conductor supplies power to the undersea equipment.

How Can One Fiber Carry So Much Internet Traffic?

A fiber is not limited to transmitting one simple stream of light.

Modern optical networks use a technology called wavelength-division multiplexing, or WDM. Multiple optical signals using different wavelengths of light can travel through the same fiber simultaneously.

Each wavelength can carry a very high-speed stream of digital information.

Advanced modulation, signal processing and error-correction technologies allow enormous amounts of data to be transmitted through each optical fiber.

Submarine systems can also contain multiple fiber pairs, further increasing their total capacity.

As optical technology improves, operators can sometimes upgrade the equipment at the ends of an existing cable and increase the amount of information the installed fibers can carry without replacing the entire cable.

Who Owns the Cables?

There is no single organization that owns the worldwide submarine cable network.

Historically, many major cables were built by groups of telecommunications companies sharing the enormous cost of construction and operation.

Telecommunications carriers remain important participants, but large technology and cloud companies have increasingly invested directly in submarine cable systems because their services move enormous quantities of data between data centers around the world.

Some cables have many owners, while others are controlled by a smaller number of companies.

The global network is therefore a collection of independently built systems that interconnect with one another and with terrestrial networks.

How Are Undersea Cables Installed?

Before a cable is installed, engineers survey the proposed route.

They study seafloor geography, water depth, geological hazards, existing infrastructure, fishing activity, shipping routes and other factors that could affect installation or reliability.

A specialized cable-laying ship carries enormous lengths of cable and gradually pays it out while following the planned route.

Across much of the deep ocean, the cable can rest directly on the seafloor.

Closer to shore and in other areas where damage is more likely, equipment can bury the cable beneath the seabed. Specialized plows or remotely operated equipment create a trench or otherwise place the cable below the seafloor for additional protection.

Are the Cables Buried Across the Entire Ocean?

Usually not.

In very deep water, human activity at the seafloor is limited, so submarine cables can often be placed directly on the ocean bottom.

The greatest risk of accidental damage generally occurs in shallower coastal waters where ships anchor and fishing vessels operate equipment that can contact the seabed.

Cables are therefore more likely to receive heavy armor or be buried in these higher-risk areas.

Route selection also helps avoid known hazards whenever practical.

What Happens If an Undersea Cable Breaks?

Submarine cable failures occur regularly around the world, but a single break usually does not disconnect the global internet.

Internet networks are interconnected through many routes. When one cable becomes unavailable, network operators can redirect traffic through other submarine cables or terrestrial routes when sufficient alternative capacity exists.

Users may notice nothing at all, although major failures can cause slower connections, congestion or outages, particularly in places with only a small number of international cable connections.

Islands and countries with limited cable diversity can be especially vulnerable to a major cable failure.

Redundancy is therefore an important part of global network design.

What Causes Submarine Cable Damage?

Despite dramatic concerns about deep-ocean hazards, many cable faults occur relatively close to shore.

Fishing activity and ship anchors are major causes of accidental cable damage. Natural events such as underwater landslides and earthquakes can also damage cables, sometimes affecting multiple systems in the same region.

Equipment can fail as well.

Intentional damage is possible, but ordinary maritime activity and natural hazards account for many real-world cable faults.

The location of the fault strongly influences how difficult the repair will be.

How Do You Repair a Cable at the Bottom of the Ocean?

Specialized cable repair ships are used to locate and repair damaged submarine cables.

Engineers first determine the approximate position of the fault using electrical and optical measurements from the cable system.

A repair vessel travels to the area and retrieves the damaged cable. Depending on water depth and conditions, crews may use grappling equipment or remotely operated vehicles.

The damaged section is brought aboard the ship, removed and replaced with a new length of cable. The repaired cable is tested and then returned to the seafloor.

In deep water, retrieving a cable from several kilometers below the surface can make repairs complex and time-consuming.

Does the Internet Mostly Travel Through Satellites?

No.

Satellites are important for communications, especially in remote areas, aboard ships and aircraft, and in places without practical terrestrial infrastructure. Modern low-Earth-orbit satellite systems have greatly expanded satellite internet capabilities.

However, the enormous capacity and efficiency of fiber-optic cables make them the primary infrastructure for carrying high-volume communications between continents.

When two continents are connected by submarine fiber, most routine internet traffic between them is generally better suited to fiber than to satellite links.

Satellites complement the terrestrial and submarine internet rather than replacing the global fiber network.

Why Can Satellites Sometimes Have Lower Latency Than a Submarine Cable Route?

Fiber is usually extremely efficient, but an interesting exception can occur because light travels faster through space than through glass.

A low-Earth-orbit satellite system may sometimes create a route whose propagation characteristics compete with or potentially outperform a long or indirect terrestrial fiber path.

Whether this actually results in lower application latency depends on the satellite altitude, ground stations, routing, network congestion and the physical path of the competing fiber network.

This does not mean satellites are generally faster or have more capacity than submarine fiber. It illustrates that internet performance depends on the entire route rather than simply the technology used for one portion of it.

Does Your Data Take the Same Route Every Time?

Not necessarily.

The internet is composed of thousands of independently operated networks connected together.

Routers and network operators determine paths based on routing information, network policies, available connections and changing conditions.

Traffic between the same two locations can therefore take different routes at different times.

The outgoing request and returning response also do not have to follow identical physical paths.

If a cable fails or a network changes its routing policy, traffic may be redirected through another cable or even another country.

How Does the Internet Decide Which Ocean Cable to Use?

Ordinary computers generally do not select a particular submarine cable themselves.

Network routers make forwarding decisions based on routing information and policies established by internet service providers, backbone networks, cloud companies and other network operators.

On the global internet, a routing system centered around the Border Gateway Protocol, or BGP, allows independently operated networks to exchange information about which destinations they can reach.

Network operators can influence which connections traffic prefers based on business agreements, capacity, performance, redundancy and engineering policies.

The physical submarine cable carrying a packet is therefore the result of a much larger system of network routing decisions.

Does Every Visit to an Overseas Website Cross the Ocean?

No.

Large websites and online services frequently store copies of popular content in data centers located much closer to users.

Content delivery networks, or CDNs, distribute website files, videos, software and other content across servers in many geographic locations.

If someone in the United States visits a website operated by a company headquartered in Europe, the requested content might actually come from a server only a few hundred miles away.

Cloud providers and large online services also operate data centers around the world.

Submarine cables remain essential because those distributed systems must exchange and synchronize enormous quantities of information internationally, but not every individual webpage request needs to cross an ocean.

Can Someone See Internet Cables on the Ocean Floor?

In principle, yes, although most people will never encounter one.

A submarine cable resting on the deep seafloor is a physical object and can be observed by suitable underwater equipment.

Near shore, cables may be buried specifically to protect them from human activity.

Considering how much global communication they carry, deep-sea fiber cables are surprisingly small compared with pipelines and many other forms of marine infrastructure.

Their enormous capacity comes from the optical technology inside them rather than from having a huge physical diameter.

Are Undersea Cables Important to National Security?

Yes.

Submarine communications cables carry international business, government communications, financial transactions, cloud services and ordinary internet traffic. Disruption of important cable routes can therefore have economic and strategic consequences.

Governments and network operators pay attention to cable security, route diversity and the resilience of landing stations and other critical infrastructure.

The concentration of multiple cables in particular geographic corridors or landing areas can create vulnerabilities if a natural disaster or other event affects several systems simultaneously.

This is one reason new cable routes and geographically diverse landing locations can improve network resilience.

Why Not Build Everything Wirelessly?

Wireless communication is extremely useful for connecting moving devices and reaching places where cables are impractical.

But fiber-optic systems can carry extraordinary amounts of information efficiently and reliably over long distances. A physical fiber also provides a dedicated transmission medium rather than requiring many users to share open radio spectrum.

Wireless systems themselves usually depend on fiber somewhere behind the scenes. A cellphone may connect wirelessly to a cell site, for example, but that site often sends the resulting traffic into a fiber network.

Wi-Fi access points similarly provide wireless connections at the edge of a network while relying on wired infrastructure for much of the journey beyond the access point.

The modern internet is therefore a combination of wireless access and an enormous underlying network of physical cables.

The Bottom Line

Most internet traffic crossing oceans travels through fiber-optic submarine cables, not satellites.

Digital information is encoded onto optical signals that travel through glass fibers inside cables stretching thousands of miles across the seafloor. Optical amplifiers positioned along long submarine routes keep the signals strong, while electrical conductors in the cable deliver power to that undersea equipment.

At cable landing stations, the submarine system connects with terrestrial fiber networks that carry data onward to internet providers, data centers and other networks.

Many submarine cables connect the world's major regions, providing alternative paths when individual systems fail. Specialized ships install the cables and return to sea when damaged sections need to be repaired.

The result is a largely invisible physical network beneath the oceans that makes modern global communication possible. A message that appears to move instantly from one continent to another may actually have traveled thousands of miles as light through a strand of glass lying on the ocean floor.
