What Is a Government Shutdown?

August 26, 202615 min read

What Is a Government Shutdown?

A federal government shutdown occurs when funding expires for some or all federal agencies and Congress and the president have not enacted new appropriations or temporary funding to replace it.

The government does not literally close. Instead, agencies affected by the funding lapse generally must stop activities that federal law does not allow them to continue without appropriations.

Some government functions continue because they have funding from another source or fall within legal exceptions. Others stop or operate with reduced staffing until funding is restored.

A shutdown is therefore best understood as a lapse in appropriations, not as the federal government running out of money entirely.

Why Does a Government Shutdown Happen?

Congress controls federal spending through its constitutional power of the purse.

Federal agencies cannot simply spend money whenever they choose. Congress normally provides funding through appropriations laws that authorize agencies to spend specified amounts for particular purposes.

Most annual federal appropriations are tied to the federal fiscal year, which begins on October 1 and ends on September 30.

If funding expires and replacement appropriations have not been enacted, affected agencies experience a funding lapse.

A shutdown can also occur when temporary funding expires in the middle of a fiscal year without another funding measure taking its place.

What Is the Antideficiency Act?

The Antideficiency Act is one of the most important laws governing what happens during a shutdown.

Among other restrictions, it generally prohibits federal agencies from spending or creating financial obligations in advance of or beyond available appropriations. It also generally prevents agencies from accepting voluntary services from employees except where federal law permits an exception.

When appropriations lapse, affected agencies therefore cannot simply continue normal operations and promise to pay for them later.

They generally must begin shutting down activities that lack available funding and do not qualify for a legal exception.

This protects Congress's authority to decide how federal money is spent.

What Is a Continuing Resolution?

A continuing resolution, commonly called a CR, is temporary legislation that keeps some or all federal agencies funded when regular appropriations have not yet been enacted.

A CR usually extends funding for a limited period, often based on previous funding levels or other terms established by Congress.

Continuing resolutions can prevent an immediate shutdown while lawmakers continue negotiating longer-term appropriations.

If a CR expires before Congress enacts another funding measure, a shutdown can begin at that point.

Does the Entire Federal Government Shut Down?

No.

The phrase government shutdown can be misleading because large portions of the federal government may continue operating.

Whether an activity continues depends on factors such as:

  • Whether its current appropriation has actually expired.
  • Whether it has funding available from another source.
  • Whether the activity is financed through mandatory or permanent spending authority.
  • Whether continuing the activity is legally necessary to protect human life or government property.
  • Whether another law specifically authorizes the activity to continue.
  • Whether the activity is necessary to support another legally authorized function.

The result can be a mixture of closed offices, delayed services, furloughed workers, and agencies continuing important operations.

What Is a Partial Government Shutdown?

Congress does not necessarily fund every federal agency through a single law.

If lawmakers enact appropriations for some agencies but not others, only the agencies whose funding has lapsed are affected.

This is commonly called a partial government shutdown.

A broader shutdown occurs when funding lapses across a larger portion of the annually funded federal government.

What Does "Excepted" Mean During a Shutdown?

Federal employees who continue working during a shutdown are often described as essential employees, but the more precise legal term is usually excepted.

Excepted work is work that federal law allows to continue despite the funding lapse.

That can include activities necessary to protect human life or government property and functions that have another legal source of funding or authority.

An employee's job title alone does not determine whether the person works during a shutdown. Agencies analyze particular functions and funding sources to determine which employees are needed to perform legally permitted work.

Who Decides What Stays Open?

Federal agencies prepare shutdown contingency plans explaining how they expect to operate during a lapse in appropriations.

Those plans identify activities expected to continue, employees who may be furloughed, employees who may perform excepted work, and functions supported by funding that remains available.

The Office of Management and Budget provides government-wide guidance, while individual agencies apply appropriations law to their programs and funding.

Plans can differ from one shutdown to another because agency funding and legal circumstances can change.

What Happens to Federal Employees?

Federal employees affected by a shutdown generally fall into several categories.

Some employees are furloughed, meaning they are temporarily placed in a non-duty, non-pay status because their work cannot legally continue during the funding lapse.

Other employees perform excepted work and must continue reporting for duty even though the lapse may temporarily prevent normal salary payments.

Still other employees may continue working normally because their salaries are funded through appropriations or other budget authority that has not lapsed.

Do Furloughed Federal Employees Work During a Shutdown?

Generally, no.

Employees placed on shutdown furlough normally cannot continue performing their regular work, including working from home, simply because they want to keep up with their responsibilities.

The Antideficiency Act generally prohibits the government from accepting voluntary services that are not legally authorized.

Furloughed employees may perform limited activities necessary for an orderly shutdown and may be recalled if their work becomes legally permitted or necessary.

Do Federal Employees Get Back Pay?

Under current federal law, eligible federal employees affected by a lapse in appropriations generally receive retroactive pay after the lapse ends.

The Government Employee Fair Treatment Act of 2019 established statutory back-pay protection for federal employees who are furloughed because of a funding lapse and for employees required to perform excepted work during the lapse.

That means their pay may be delayed, but qualifying federal employees generally do not permanently lose their salary solely because of the shutdown.

What Happens to Federal Contractors?

Federal contractors are different from federal employees.

Employees of private companies performing federal contracts do not automatically receive the same statutory back-pay protection provided to federal employees.

Whether a contractor continues working or receives compensation depends on factors such as the contract, available funding, agency instructions, and the company's employment arrangements.

Contractor employees can therefore experience lost wages or work opportunities that are not automatically restored when the government reopens.

Does Social Security Stop During a Shutdown?

Generally, Social Security benefit payments do not simply stop because annual appropriations lapse.

Social Security benefits are primarily funded through mandatory spending rather than the annual discretionary appropriations that commonly cause shutdowns.

However, the Social Security Administration also performs administrative work that depends on annually appropriated funding. Some services, staffing levels, communications, or processing activities may therefore be affected during a shutdown.

The distinction is between the underlying benefit payment and the administrative operations supporting the program.

Does Medicare Stop During a Shutdown?

Medicare benefits also do not simply disappear during a shutdown.

Medicare is largely financed through mandatory federal spending and dedicated funding mechanisms rather than relying entirely on annual discretionary appropriations.

However, agencies administering Medicare may experience staffing or administrative disruptions depending on which appropriations have lapsed and which functions are legally permitted to continue.

Does the Postal Service Shut Down?

Generally, no.

The U.S. Postal Service is funded primarily through revenue from postage and postal services rather than ordinary annual appropriations used by many federal agencies.

Mail delivery therefore generally continues during a federal shutdown.

The Postal Service does have some connections to federal appropriations and federal law, but a lapse affecting ordinary annually appropriated agencies does not normally cause regular postal operations to shut down.

What Happens to the Military?

National defense activities can continue during a funding lapse when they fall within legal exceptions or have available funding.

Active-duty military personnel generally continue performing required duties. Civilian Defense Department employees may be treated differently depending on their duties and available funding.

A lapse in military appropriations can affect when compensation is paid unless Congress provides funding or another lawful source is available.

The precise effect depends on which defense appropriations have expired and what legislation Congress has enacted for the particular shutdown.

Do Federal Law Enforcement Agencies Keep Working?

Many federal law-enforcement and public-safety functions continue during a shutdown.

Employees whose work is legally necessary to protect human life or government property may continue performing excepted duties.

This can include many functions involving federal law enforcement, prisons, border operations, aviation safety, and national security.

However, supporting activities, administrative work, training, hiring, investigations, or other functions may be delayed or reduced depending on the agency's shutdown plan.

The fact that an agency remains operational does not mean every employee or service continues normally.

Do TSA Officers and Air Traffic Controllers Keep Working?

Many Transportation Security Administration officers and air traffic controllers perform functions that continue during a shutdown because of public-safety requirements and other legal authorities.

They may therefore be required to work even while normal appropriations have lapsed.

Long shutdowns can still affect staffing, administrative support, employee morale, training, hiring, and the timing of pay, potentially creating disruptions even when the core safety function continues.

What Happens to National Parks?

National parks can be significantly affected by shutdowns, but there is no universal rule that every park must operate in exactly the same way.

Depending on available funding and the National Park Service's contingency plan, facilities may close, visitor services may be suspended, or some areas may remain physically accessible with greatly reduced staffing.

Services such as visitor centers, restrooms, trash collection, maintenance, permitting, and ranger programs can be reduced or unavailable.

The government cannot simply use money intended for another purpose to keep normal park operations running unless federal law permits it.

What Happens to Federal Courts?

The federal judiciary may sometimes continue operating for a period during an appropriations lapse by using available fee balances or other funding that has not yet been exhausted.

If a shutdown continues long enough, court operations can eventually be affected.

Constitutionally required judicial functions and matters involving public safety or liberty can also receive different treatment from ordinary administrative activities.

The precise effect depends on available judiciary funding and the length of the shutdown.

What Other Government Services Can Be Delayed?

The effects vary considerably among agencies, but shutdowns can delay or reduce activities involving:

  • Permits and licenses.
  • Federal grants and loans.
  • Regulatory reviews.
  • Inspections.
  • Scientific research.
  • Government statistics and reports.
  • Hiring and employee training.
  • Processing of applications.
  • Public information services.
  • Administrative hearings.
  • Maintenance of federal facilities.

Some services continue because they have separate funding or qualify for an exception, while others may stop almost immediately.

Do Government Websites Shut Down?

Some continue operating while others may not be updated regularly.

Automated websites and computer systems may remain online even when employees responsible for updating them are furloughed.

Other online services may be reduced or unavailable if operating them requires work that cannot legally continue during the funding lapse.

A functioning government website therefore does not necessarily mean the agency behind it is operating normally.

What Are the Economic Effects of a Shutdown?

A shutdown can affect the economy in several ways.

Possible effects include:

  • Delayed federal salaries.
  • Lost income for some contractor employees.
  • Reduced spending by affected workers.
  • Delayed government purchases and payments.
  • Disruptions to tourism and businesses near closed federal facilities.
  • Delays in permits, loans, grants, and regulatory decisions.
  • Administrative backlogs that remain after agencies reopen.

Some economic activity is merely postponed. Federal employees receiving statutory back pay, for example, eventually receive wages that were delayed.

Other losses may never be recovered. A contractor employee who loses work, a business that loses customers because a park is closed, or an applicant harmed by a delayed government decision may not automatically be compensated later.

The economic impact generally increases as a shutdown lasts longer and affects more agencies.

Is a Government Shutdown the Same as the Debt Ceiling?

No.

They involve two different federal financial issues.

A government shutdown occurs when agencies lack appropriations or other legal authority to continue particular spending and operations.

The debt limit, commonly called the debt ceiling, restricts the amount of debt the Treasury is authorized to issue to finance obligations the federal government has already incurred under law.

A shutdown therefore concerns authority to obligate and spend money for government operations, while a debt-limit crisis concerns the government's borrowing authority to meet existing financial obligations.

A government shutdown does not mean the United States has defaulted on its debt.

Does a Shutdown Mean the Government Ran Out of Money?

Not exactly.

The problem is primarily legal authority to spend rather than the federal government's bank account reaching zero.

Congress determines through appropriations and other laws when agencies may incur obligations and spend federal funds.

Even if Treasury has cash available, an affected agency generally cannot spend money for an activity when its appropriation has expired and no other legal authority permits the spending.

That is why a shutdown is more accurately described as a funding lapse or lapse in appropriations.

Can the President Keep the Government Open Without Congress?

The president cannot simply order federal agencies to ignore appropriations law and continue normal spending after funding has expired.

The Constitution gives Congress substantial authority over federal spending, and the Antideficiency Act restricts executive agencies from obligating money without lawful budget authority.

The executive branch determines how existing shutdown laws and exceptions apply to agency operations, but it cannot create an appropriation that Congress has not enacted.

Can Congress Force the Government to Reopen?

Congress can pass appropriations legislation or a continuing resolution restoring funding.

Under the ordinary legislative process, funding legislation must pass both the House and Senate and be presented to the president.

If the president signs the legislation, funding is restored.

If the president vetoes it, Congress can still enact the measure by overriding the veto with the constitutionally required two-thirds vote in each chamber.

How Does a Government Shutdown End?

A shutdown ends for an affected agency when sufficient funding or other legal budget authority becomes available.

Most commonly, Congress passes and the president signs:

  • Regular appropriations legislation.
  • A continuing resolution providing temporary funding.
  • Another funding law covering the affected operations.

Agencies then recall furloughed employees, resume suspended activities, process delayed work, and begin addressing backlogs created during the shutdown.

Reopening does not mean every government service immediately returns to normal. Work that accumulated during the lapse can take days, weeks, or longer to clear.

Common Misunderstandings

The Entire Federal Government Closes

No.

Only activities affected by the funding lapse and unable to continue under another legal authority must stop. Many government functions continue.

A Shutdown Means the Government Ran Out of Money

No.

A shutdown is primarily a legal appropriations problem. Agencies generally cannot spend without congressional authority even if federal funds exist elsewhere.

Federal Employees Who Keep Working Are Volunteers

No.

Excepted employees may be legally required to continue performing their duties during the funding lapse.

Furloughed Employees Can Just Work From Home

Generally, no.

Federal law normally prevents agencies from accepting unauthorized voluntary work during a shutdown.

Federal Employees Permanently Lose Their Pay

Under current law, eligible federal employees affected by a shutdown generally receive retroactive pay after funding is restored.

Federal Contractors Automatically Receive Back Pay Too

No.

Contractor employees do not receive the same automatic statutory back-pay protection that qualifying federal employees receive.

Social Security Checks Automatically Stop

No.

Social Security benefits are primarily mandatory spending and generally continue, although some administrative services can be affected.

The Postal Service Closes

Generally, no.

The Postal Service relies primarily on postal revenue rather than the annual appropriations that commonly lapse during a shutdown.

A Shutdown Is the Same as a Debt Default

No.

A shutdown concerns appropriations for government operations. A debt-limit crisis concerns Treasury's authority to borrow to meet existing federal obligations.

The President Can Simply Order Every Agency to Stay Open

No.

The executive branch remains bound by federal appropriations law and generally cannot spend money Congress has not authorized.

Why Government Shutdowns Matter

Shutdowns demonstrate how closely federal operations are tied to Congress's constitutional authority over spending.

They can interrupt public services, delay government decisions, disrupt federal workers and contractors, create administrative backlogs, and impose economic costs even though many critical government functions continue.

The phrase government shutdown can therefore obscure what is actually happening. The important questions are which appropriations have expired, which agencies are affected, which activities have another source of funding, and which functions federal law permits to continue.

Understanding those distinctions makes it easier to interpret reports claiming that a particular agency, benefit, employee, or government service will be affected by a funding lapse.

Conclusion

A federal government shutdown is a lapse in appropriations that requires affected agencies to stop activities they are not legally authorized to continue without funding.

The Antideficiency Act generally prevents agencies from spending or creating financial obligations without available appropriations, while specific exceptions and other funding sources allow many important government functions to continue.

Some federal employees are furloughed while others must continue performing excepted work. Under current law, qualifying federal employees generally receive retroactive pay after the shutdown ends, while federal contractors do not automatically receive the same protection.

Programs such as Social Security and Medicare do not simply disappear, the Postal Service generally continues operating, and critical public-safety and national-security functions may continue under applicable law.

Most importantly, a government shutdown is not the same as the government running out of money or defaulting on its debt. It is fundamentally a dispute or delay over the legal authority to fund federal operations.
