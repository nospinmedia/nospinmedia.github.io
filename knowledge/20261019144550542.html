What Is Election Certification and Can It Be Refused?

October 23, 202612 min read

When an election ends, the vote totals reported on television and election websites are not yet official. Before the results become legally recognized, election officials must complete a process called election certification.

Certification is the formal act of confirming election results after the required counting, verification, and review procedures have been completed.

The process is governed primarily by state law, although federal requirements also apply to certain elections, particularly presidential elections.

An important question is whether election officials can refuse to certify results if they disagree with the outcome or believe something went wrong.

The answer depends on the official's legal responsibilities and the circumstances. In many jurisdictions, certification is a mandatory duty once the required conditions have been satisfied. Officials generally cannot refuse simply because they dislike the outcome or have unsupported suspicions about the election.

However, election laws also provide procedures for investigating discrepancies, correcting errors, conducting recounts, and resolving legal disputes.

What Does Election Certification Mean?

Election certification is the formal legal confirmation of election results by the government authority responsible for that task.

It establishes the official vote totals and identifies the candidates or ballot measures that prevailed under the applicable election rules.

Certification is different from counting ballots. Ballots must first be processed and counted, and election records must be reviewed according to the law.

Certification is also different from a news organization announcing a winner. Television networks and other media outlets may project election outcomes before every ballot has been counted, but those projections have no independent legal authority.

The official result comes from the election process established by law.

What Happens Before an Election Is Certified?

After polls close, election officials begin completing several administrative and verification tasks.

The exact procedures vary by state, but they commonly include reviewing reported vote totals, accounting for ballots, resolving discrepancies, and completing the election canvass.

The canvass is a formal review of election returns and related records. It helps establish that the reported totals accurately reflect the ballots legally counted.

Election officials may compare the number of voters recorded as participating with the number of ballots issued, cast, and counted. They also review matters such as provisional ballots, eligible absentee ballots, spoiled ballots, and documented counting errors.

Some jurisdictions require audits before certification. Others conduct certain audits afterward.

If officials identify a discrepancy, they must follow the procedures established by law to determine whether a correction or additional review is necessary.

Once the legally required steps are completed, the responsible authority certifies the results.

Who Is Responsible for Certifying Elections?

There is no single national official who certifies every election in the United States.

Certification responsibilities are divided among state and local authorities.

Depending on the state and election, certification may involve county election boards, municipal officials, canvassing boards, secretaries of state, or state election commissions.

Local officials often certify or formally approve results within their jurisdictions before transmitting them to state authorities.

State officials may then canvass or certify statewide results and results for offices covering multiple local jurisdictions.

The specific responsibilities depend on state law and the office being elected.

Federal elections are generally administered through these state and local systems, even when voters are selecting members of Congress or presidential electors.

Is Certification Just a Formality?

Certification is a legal responsibility, not merely a ceremonial announcement.

It establishes the official election record and allows the next steps required by law to proceed.

However, certification is generally not an unrestricted opportunity for election officials to reconsider whether they approve of an election outcome.

In many jurisdictions, the officials responsible for certification perform a duty described in law as ministerial.

A ministerial duty is one that an official must carry out when specified legal conditions have been met, rather than deciding whether to act based on personal preference.

For example, if the law requires an election board to certify properly canvassed results by a particular deadline, board members ordinarily cannot refuse merely because their preferred candidate lost.

The precise scope of a certifying official's authority varies by jurisdiction. Some laws require officials to examine particular records or resolve specified issues before certification.

Those responsibilities must be distinguished from a general power to reject an election outcome.

Can Election Officials Refuse to Certify Results?

An election official may raise concerns about discrepancies or possible violations of election law. Whether certification can legally be delayed or withheld depends on the applicable statute, the official's duties, and the facts involved.

A legally authorized review or court order may affect when certification occurs.

But a refusal based only on disagreement with the outcome, political objections, or unsupported allegations generally does not provide a lawful basis for ignoring a mandatory certification duty.

Election officials are required to follow the law governing their responsibilities.

They cannot create new certification requirements simply because they distrust the reported results.

When a dispute arises, the question is not whether an official personally believes the election was satisfactory. It is whether the law permits or requires the action being taken.

What Happens if an Election Board Refuses to Certify?

A refusal can trigger administrative or judicial proceedings.

Depending on state law, a state election authority, candidate, voter, or another party with legal standing may seek a court order requiring officials to perform their duties.

One legal remedy is called a writ of mandamus. It is an order directing a government official or public body to perform a duty that the law requires.

Mandamus is not available automatically in every dispute. Courts consider the applicable legal standards, including whether the duty is sufficiently clear and whether the requested remedy is appropriate.

A court may order officials to complete certification if it determines that they have unlawfully failed to perform a mandatory duty.

Some states also provide administrative procedures for addressing failures by local election boards.

The available remedies, responsible authorities, and consequences depend on state law.

Can an Official Face Consequences for Refusing Certification?

Potentially, yes.

An official who unlawfully refuses to carry out a required duty may face a court order compelling compliance.

Depending on the circumstances and applicable law, additional consequences could include contempt proceedings for disobeying a court order, removal from office through an authorized process, or other legal remedies.

Criminal penalties are not automatic. They would require conduct covered by an applicable criminal statute and proof of the necessary legal elements.

A disagreement over election procedures does not, by itself, establish criminal wrongdoing.

The consequences depend on what the official was legally required to do, what actions were taken, and how the relevant law applies.

What if Officials Discover Evidence of an Actual Problem?

Election officials have responsibilities to identify and address discrepancies according to law.

Problems can include counting errors, missing documentation, equipment malfunctions, or questions about whether certain ballots qualify for counting.

When an issue is discovered, officials may be required to examine records, correct tabulation errors, review ballots, or refer the matter to the appropriate authority.

A recount, audit, investigation, or court proceeding may be available depending on the circumstances.

The important distinction is between using an authorized process to address a documented problem and refusing to perform a mandatory duty without a lawful basis.

Certification rules do not require officials to ignore genuine errors. They establish how results must be verified and when the formal legal process must be completed.

Can Certification Be Delayed Because of Fraud Allegations?

An allegation of election fraud is not the same as proof that fraud occurred.

Election officials and courts must evaluate claims using evidence and applicable legal standards.

A specific allegation supported by relevant evidence may lead to an investigation, legal challenge, or other authorized proceeding.

However, unsupported accusations do not automatically suspend certification deadlines or give officials authority to reject election results.

Election laws often establish separate procedures for investigating alleged misconduct and challenging results.

Some disputes can continue after certification.

Whether a particular allegation legally affects certification depends on the jurisdiction, the evidence, the relief requested, and any applicable court orders.

What Is the Difference Between Certification and a Recount?

Certification formally establishes official results. A recount examines the votes again to verify or correct the totals in a particular contest.

Recounts may be required automatically when a race falls within a specified margin, requested by an eligible party, or ordered through a legal proceeding.

Some states conduct certain recounts before certification. Others permit recounts after an initial certification and provide procedures for amending the official results when necessary.

A recount does not automatically mean the original count was inaccurate. It is a method of checking the results under conditions established by law.

Certification and recount procedures must operate according to the applicable deadlines and legal requirements.

Can a Certified Election Result Be Changed?

Yes, in circumstances authorized by law.

Certification does not necessarily eliminate every opportunity for correction or legal challenge.

A recount may identify an error requiring the official totals to be amended. A court may also order appropriate relief if a legal challenge establishes grounds for changing a result.

The procedures and deadlines for contesting certified results vary by jurisdiction and type of election.

A certified result cannot ordinarily be changed merely because someone disagrees with it.

There must be a legal basis and an authorized procedure for making the correction.

How Does Certification Work in Presidential Elections?

Presidential elections involve additional constitutional and federal statutory requirements because voters are participating in the process of selecting presidential electors.

After the statewide popular vote is counted and the required state procedures are completed, the state determines which presidential electors have been appointed under its laws.

Federal law establishes procedures and deadlines for issuing the certificate of ascertainment identifying the appointed electors.

The Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 clarified this process and established rules governing the recognition of state certifications and the resolution of certain disputes.

Under the federal framework, the state's executive, generally the governor unless state law enacted before Election Day designates another official, is responsible for issuing the certificate of ascertainment.

Federal law also provides a process for expedited judicial review of certain disputes involving these certificates.

The appointed electors meet and cast their electoral votes. Congress later counts those electoral votes in a joint session.

The vice president, acting as president of the Senate, presides over that joint session but does not have unilateral authority to accept, reject, or resolve disputes over electoral votes.

Presidential certification therefore involves both state election procedures and a separate federal constitutional process.

Can Congress Refuse to Accept a State's Presidential Election Results?

Congress has a defined role in counting electoral votes, but that role is governed by federal law.

The Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 revised the procedures for congressional objections to electoral votes.

An objection must meet statutory requirements, including support from at least one-fifth of the members of each chamber.

The law also limits the grounds on which objections may be made.

An objection must fit within the legally specified grounds, rather than simply expressing dissatisfaction with a state's election outcome.

The House and Senate consider qualifying objections under the procedures established by law.

Congress does not possess an unrestricted power to substitute its preferred presidential candidate for the candidate selected through the lawful electoral process.

What Happens if Certification Deadlines Are Missed?

Election laws establish deadlines to ensure that results become official in time for the next steps of government.

If officials miss a deadline, the consequences depend on the jurisdiction and the type of election.

Courts may be asked to order officials to complete required duties. State authorities may also have specific statutory powers to address delays.

A missed deadline does not automatically erase valid ballots or transfer victory to another candidate.

In presidential elections, federal law establishes additional deadlines connected to the appointment of electors and the congressional electoral vote count.

Disputes involving those deadlines may require expedited legal proceedings.

The legal consequences must be determined under the applicable constitutional and statutory framework.

Does Certification Mean Every Ballot Was Counted?

Certification means the election results have been established through the legally required procedures. It does not necessarily mean that every physical ballot submitted was accepted as a valid vote.

Some ballots may be rejected because they fail applicable eligibility or procedural requirements.

A ballot may also contain a valid vote in one contest but no countable vote in another because the voter left it blank or made an invalid selection.

Election officials must account for ballots and apply the law governing which votes qualify for counting.

Certification confirms the official totals produced through that process.

It is not a guarantee that no administrative mistake was ever made, which is one reason election systems include audits, recounts, and legal review procedures.

Why Is Certification Important?

Certification creates an official legal record of the election outcome.

That record is needed to determine who has been elected, issue certificates of election where required, and allow the lawful transfer or continuation of governmental authority.

It also establishes a documented conclusion to the ordinary counting and verification process, subject to any further proceedings authorized by law.

Without certification, election results could remain administratively unresolved even after the votes had been counted.

The process helps separate the factual determination of election results from political preferences about who should win.

The Bottom Line

Election certification is the formal legal process that makes election results official.

State and local officials count ballots, review election records, resolve discrepancies, and complete the procedures required before certification.

In many jurisdictions, certification is a mandatory legal duty once those requirements have been satisfied. Election officials generally cannot lawfully refuse to certify results merely because they disagree with the outcome or have unsupported suspicions.

Genuine problems must be addressed through the procedures established by law, including audits, recounts, investigations, and court proceedings when appropriate.

If an official unlawfully refuses to certify an election, courts or other authorized authorities may intervene.

The purpose of certification is to establish a legally recognized result based on the votes that qualify under election law, not on the personal preferences of the officials responsible for confirming it.
