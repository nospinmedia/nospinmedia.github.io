What Is a Presidential Pardon?

August 14, 20268 min read

What Is a Presidential Pardon?

A presidential pardon is an exercise of executive clemency that forgives a federal offense and can relieve a person from some of the legal consequences associated with that offense.

The power comes directly from the U.S. Constitution. Article II gives the president authority to grant reprieves and pardons for offenses against the United States, except in cases of impeachment.

The pardon power is broad, but it is not unlimited. It applies to federal offenses, does not erase an impeachment, and cannot be used to forgive crimes that have not yet been committed.

Where Does the President Get the Pardon Power?

The presidential pardon power comes from Article II, Section 2 of the Constitution.

It gives the president authority to grant clemency for "Offences against the United States," meaning federal offenses.

The power is exercised by the president rather than Congress or the federal courts. The Supreme Court has historically treated it as a broad constitutional power that Congress generally cannot substantially restrict.

What Does a Presidential Pardon Do?

A pardon can relieve a person from punishment and certain legal consequences associated with a federal offense.

Depending on the circumstances, a pardon may restore certain civil rights or remove legal disabilities that resulted from the offense.

However, a pardon does not automatically erase the historical fact that a conviction occurred.

The Department of Justice specifically distinguishes a pardon from expungement. If someone receives a presidential pardon after a federal conviction, the conviction generally remains on the person's criminal record along with information showing that a pardon was granted.

Does a Pardon Mean Someone Is Innocent?

No.

A presidential pardon is an act of clemency, not a court ruling that the person was factually innocent or that the conviction was legally invalid.

A president may grant a pardon for many reasons, including mercy, rehabilitation, fairness, public policy, or concerns about the severity or consequences of a conviction.

In rare circumstances, a pardon may reflect doubts about a conviction, but the pardon itself is not the same as a judicial declaration of innocence.

Does Accepting a Pardon Mean Admitting Guilt?

This question is more complicated than it is sometimes presented.

In the 1915 Supreme Court case Burdick v. United States, the Court stated that a pardon carries an implication of guilt and that accepting one carries an acknowledgment of that implication.

However, that language arose in a specific legal dispute involving whether a person could reject a pardon and continue asserting the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. It should not be simplified into a rule that every person who accepts a pardon has formally confessed guilt in every legal sense.

A pardon still does not function as a criminal conviction or a judicial finding of guilt.

Can Someone Be Pardoned Before Being Convicted?

Yes.

A person does not have to be convicted, sentenced, or even formally charged before receiving a presidential pardon, as long as the federal offense has already occurred.

The Supreme Court has recognized that the pardon power may be exercised after an offense is committed but before criminal proceedings begin, while proceedings are underway, or after conviction.

This means a president may issue what is sometimes called a preemptive pardon covering federal offenses that have already occurred even if prosecutors have not brought charges.

The president cannot pardon someone in advance for crimes they may commit in the future.

Can a President Pardon State Crimes?

No.

The president's pardon authority applies only to offenses against the United States, meaning federal offenses.

A president cannot pardon someone for a conviction under state law.

State constitutions and laws establish their own clemency systems, which may give pardon authority to a governor, a pardon board, or another state institution.

The same conduct can sometimes violate both federal and state law. A presidential pardon covering the federal offense does not automatically eliminate any separate state prosecution or conviction arising from state law.

What Is the Difference Between a Pardon and a Commutation?

Both are forms of executive clemency, but they do different things.

A pardon forgives the federal offense and may remove some of its legal consequences.

A commutation reduces or ends a person's punishment without pardoning the underlying offense.

For example, a president could commute a 20-year federal prison sentence to 10 years or to time already served. The conviction itself would remain.

The Department of Justice recognizes several forms of executive clemency, including:

  • Pardons.
  • Commutations of sentences.
  • Reprieves, which temporarily delay punishment.
  • Remission of fines or restitution in appropriate cases.

Does a Pardon Erase a Criminal Record?

No, not automatically.

A presidential pardon and expungement are different legal actions.

A pardon generally does not remove the conviction or underlying offense from judicial or executive branch records. Instead, the record may show both the conviction and the later pardon.

This is an important distinction because people sometimes describe a pardon as though the conviction disappears entirely. In most cases, it does not.

Can a President Pardon a Group of People?

Yes.

Presidential clemency can be granted to individual people or, in some circumstances, to categories or groups of people.

Presidents have historically issued broad grants of clemency covering classes of individuals who committed particular types of federal offenses.

The constitutional power is not limited to one named individual at a time.

Can a President Pardon Someone for Impeachment?

No.

The Constitution specifically excludes cases of impeachment from the presidential pardon power.

A president therefore cannot use a pardon to stop or reverse impeachment by the House of Representatives or conviction and removal by the Senate.

However, impeachment and criminal prosecution are separate processes. If conduct involved in an impeachment also constitutes a federal crime, the pardon power may still raise separate questions concerning that criminal offense.

Can a President Pardon Themselves?

The answer is legally unresolved.

The Constitution does not explicitly state whether a president may pardon themselves, and no president has attempted a self-pardon that produced a definitive court ruling.

The Supreme Court has never decided whether a presidential self-pardon would be constitutional.

A 1974 Justice Department Office of Legal Counsel opinion concluded that a president cannot pardon themselves, reasoning that no person should act as judge in their own case. That opinion is an executive branch legal interpretation, not a Supreme Court ruling.

Because there is no controlling judicial precedent, the constitutionality of a presidential self-pardon remains an open legal question.

Does Congress Have to Approve a Pardon?

No.

Congress does not vote on individual presidential pardons.

The president exercises the constitutional pardon power directly. The Department of Justice's Office of the Pardon Attorney reviews many clemency applications and makes recommendations, but the final decision belongs to the president.

A president is not constitutionally required to follow the recommendation of the Pardon Attorney.

Can Courts Overturn a Presidential Pardon?

Courts generally cannot simply cancel a valid presidential pardon because they disagree with the president's decision.

The pardon power comes directly from the Constitution, and the Supreme Court has historically recognized broad presidential discretion in exercising it.

Courts can still resolve legal disputes involving the scope, interpretation, validity, or consequences of a pardon when those questions arise in an appropriate case.

Common Misunderstandings

A Pardon Means the Person Was Innocent

No.

A pardon is an exercise of executive clemency. It is not the same as a court overturning a conviction or declaring someone innocent.

A Pardon Erases the Conviction

No.

A presidential pardon generally does not expunge the person's criminal record. The conviction and the pardon may both remain part of the record.

The President Can Pardon State Crimes

No.

The presidential pardon power applies to federal offenses. State offenses are handled through state clemency systems.

Someone Must Be Convicted Before Receiving a Pardon

No.

A president may pardon a federal offense after it has been committed even if the person has not yet been charged or convicted.

A President Can Pardon Future Crimes

No.

The pardon power can reach offenses that have already occurred. It cannot give someone advance permission to commit crimes in the future.

A Pardon and a Commutation Are the Same Thing

No.

A pardon forgives an offense, while a commutation reduces or ends a sentence without pardoning the conviction itself.

Why Presidential Pardons Matter

The pardon power gives the president an unusual ability to intervene directly in the federal criminal justice system.

It can be used to show mercy, correct perceived injustices, reduce the continuing consequences of old convictions, or address broader public policy concerns.

Because the power is broad and generally does not require approval from Congress or the courts, presidential pardons can also generate political and legal controversy.

Understanding what a pardon actually does—and what it does not do—helps distinguish the legal effects of clemency from political arguments surrounding individual pardon decisions.

Conclusion

A presidential pardon is a constitutional form of executive clemency that applies to federal offenses. It can forgive an offense and relieve some of its legal consequences, but it does not automatically erase a conviction, establish innocence, or affect state crimes.

Presidents may issue pardons before or after conviction and may pardon individuals or groups for offenses that have already occurred. The Constitution excludes impeachment from the pardon power, and whether a president can pardon themselves remains an unresolved constitutional question.
