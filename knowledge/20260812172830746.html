What Is a Grand Jury?

August 20, 202611 min read

What Is a Grand Jury?

A grand jury is a group of citizens that investigates possible crimes and determines whether there is sufficient legal basis to bring certain criminal charges.

A grand jury does not decide whether someone is guilty. Its role comes earlier in the criminal process. In the federal system, prosecutors present evidence to the grand jury, which then decides whether there is enough basis to issue an indictment formally accusing someone of a crime.

This distinction is important because news reports often announce that someone has been indicted by a grand jury. An indictment is a criminal charge, not a conviction.

What Does a Grand Jury Do?

A grand jury has both investigative and charging functions.

It can hear testimony, examine documents and other evidence, and issue subpoenas as part of an investigation. Federal prosecutors commonly work with grand juries to investigate possible violations of federal criminal law.

After considering the evidence, the grand jury may be asked to determine whether an indictment should be returned.

The grand jury is not supposed to determine guilt or innocence. That question is resolved later through the criminal justice process if charges are filed and the case proceeds.

What Is an Indictment?

An indictment is a formal criminal accusation approved by a grand jury.

It identifies the criminal charges the government intends to prosecute. In the federal system, at least 12 grand jurors must agree before an indictment can be returned.

An indictment means the grand jury found sufficient basis for the charges to proceed. It does not mean prosecutors have proved the charges beyond a reasonable doubt.

A defendant remains presumed innocent unless and until guilt is established through the criminal justice process.

What Is a True Bill or No Bill?

The phrase true bill traditionally refers to a grand jury's decision to approve an indictment.

A no bill generally means the grand jury declined to indict on the proposed charge.

Terminology and procedures vary among jurisdictions, particularly at the state level.

A grand jury's refusal to indict is not the same as an acquittal after a criminal trial.

How Is a Grand Jury Different From a Trial Jury?

A grand jury and a trial jury perform very different jobs.

A grand jury considers whether criminal charges should proceed. A trial jury, sometimes called a petit jury, determines whether prosecutors have proved a defendant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt at trial.

Grand-jury proceedings generally occur without the public courtroom procedures associated with a trial. There is normally no judge presiding inside the federal grand-jury room, and the defense does not present its case in the same adversarial manner used at trial.

The burden required to obtain an indictment is also substantially different from the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard required for a criminal conviction.

Does the Constitution Require Grand Juries?

The Fifth Amendment requires grand-jury involvement for certain serious federal criminal prosecutions.

The amendment says that a person generally cannot be required to answer for a capital or otherwise infamous federal crime without a grand-jury presentment or indictment, subject to the Constitution's military exception.

The federal grand-jury requirement does not apply to the states in the same way.

The Supreme Court has not incorporated the Fifth Amendment's Grand Jury Clause against state governments through the Fourteenth Amendment. States therefore may use grand juries, preliminary hearings, prosecutors' informations, or other charging procedures permitted by their laws.

This is why grand-jury procedures can differ substantially between federal and state criminal cases.

How Many People Serve on a Federal Grand Jury?

Under Rule 6 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, a federal grand jury has between 16 and 23 members.

At least 12 grand jurors must agree before the grand jury can return an indictment.

State grand juries may have different sizes and voting requirements because state procedures are governed by state law.

Who Presents the Evidence?

Federal prosecutors normally present evidence to the grand jury.

They may call witnesses, introduce documents, explain relevant law, and ask the grand jury to approve particular criminal charges.

Grand jurors themselves also have investigative authority and may ask questions of witnesses.

Unlike a criminal trial, however, the grand-jury process is not normally an adversarial proceeding in which prosecutors and defense attorneys present competing cases before the jury.

Is the Defense Attorney in the Grand-Jury Room?

Generally, no.

A witness appearing before a federal grand jury does not ordinarily have their attorney sitting beside them inside the grand-jury room during testimony.

A witness who has an attorney may generally leave the room for a reasonable opportunity to consult with counsel before answering questions.

The defendant's attorney also does not cross-examine government witnesses or present a defense case before the grand jury in the way an attorney would during a criminal trial.

State procedures can differ.

Can the Person Being Investigated Testify?

Sometimes.

A person under investigation may testify before a federal grand jury, but there is generally no federal legal requirement that prosecutors give every target an opportunity to present their side of the case before an indictment is sought.

Department of Justice policy provides circumstances in which reasonable requests by subjects or targets to testify should receive favorable consideration, but that policy is not the same as a constitutional right to testify before the grand jury.

Someone considering grand-jury testimony can face significant legal consequences because statements made under oath may later be used in criminal proceedings.

What Is a Grand-Jury Target?

Federal prosecutors commonly distinguish among targets, subjects, and witnesses during grand-jury investigations.

A target is a person whom prosecutors or the grand jury have substantial evidence linking to a crime and whom prosecutors consider a potential defendant.

A subject is someone whose conduct falls within the scope of the investigation but who has not necessarily been identified as a likely defendant.

A witness may have information relevant to the investigation without being suspected of committing the crime being investigated.

These terms describe a person's position within an investigation and can change as investigators obtain additional evidence.

Can a Grand Jury Issue Subpoenas?

Yes.

Grand juries have broad investigative powers and may use subpoenas to obtain testimony and evidence.

A grand-jury subpoena may require someone to appear and testify or require the production of documents, records, or other evidence.

Ignoring a valid grand-jury subpoena can result in court enforcement proceedings and potentially contempt sanctions.

A recipient may also challenge a subpoena or assert an applicable constitutional or legal privilege.

Can a Witness Plead the Fifth Before a Grand Jury?

Yes, when the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination properly applies.

A grand-jury witness may refuse to answer a question when a truthful answer could reasonably tend to incriminate that person.

The privilege does not automatically allow a witness to refuse every question. It applies to testimony that creates a genuine risk of self-incrimination.

Federal prosecutors have Department of Justice procedures for advising known targets and subjects about certain rights before grand-jury testimony.

In some circumstances, prosecutors may also seek immunity that protects qualifying compelled testimony from being used against the witness in specified ways. Immunity can affect whether the witness may continue refusing to testify on Fifth Amendment grounds.

Why Are Grand-Jury Proceedings Secret?

Federal grand-jury proceedings are generally conducted in secret.

Rule 6 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure imposes secrecy obligations on grand jurors, prosecutors, court reporters, interpreters, and certain other people involved in the proceedings, subject to specified exceptions.

Grand-jury secrecy serves several purposes. Among other things, it can protect ongoing investigations, reduce the risk that potential defendants flee or interfere with witnesses, and protect people who are investigated but never charged.

The secrecy rules do not operate exactly the same way for everyone. For example, federal Rule 6 does not itself impose the same secrecy obligation on an ordinary witness concerning that witness's own testimony.

Who Is Allowed Inside a Federal Grand-Jury Room?

Federal rules strictly limit who may be present while a grand jury is in session.

During testimony, permitted people generally include:

  • Grand jurors.
  • Federal government attorneys.
  • The witness being questioned.
  • An interpreter when necessary.
  • A court reporter or operator of a recording device.

Only grand jurors may be present while the grand jury deliberates or votes.

Neither the judge nor the suspect's defense attorney normally participates inside the room during those proceedings.

What Happens After an Indictment?

Once a federal grand jury returns an indictment, the criminal case proceeds through the federal court system.

Depending on the circumstances, the defendant may be arrested or summoned to court, formally appear before a judge, enter a plea, challenge aspects of the prosecution, and prepare for trial or another resolution of the case.

The government still carries the burden of proving the criminal charges if the case goes to trial.

The grand jury's decision to indict does not reduce the prosecution's trial burden or eliminate the defendant's presumption of innocence.

What Happens If the Grand Jury Does Not Indict?

If the required number of grand jurors does not approve an indictment, the proposed indictment is not returned.

That does not amount to an acquittal.

Because a grand-jury proceeding is not a criminal trial, double jeopardy generally does not attach merely because a grand jury considered charges and declined to indict.

Depending on the circumstances and applicable law, prosecutors may sometimes continue investigating, present additional evidence, or later seek charges again.

Other restrictions, including statutes of limitations and prosecutorial rules, may affect what prosecutors can do.

Does an Indictment Mean the Evidence Is Strong Enough for a Conviction?

Not necessarily.

The grand jury's decision concerns whether charges should proceed, not whether prosecutors can ultimately prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

A criminal trial provides protections and procedures that are not part of the grand-jury process, including an adversarial defense, cross-examination of prosecution witnesses, presentation of defense evidence, judicial rulings during trial, and the requirement that guilt be proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

A defendant can therefore be indicted and later acquitted, have charges dismissed, or obtain another outcome that does not result in conviction.

Common Misunderstandings

An Indictment Means Someone Is Guilty

No.

An indictment is a formal accusation. It allows criminal charges to proceed but does not establish guilt.

A Grand Jury Is the Jury at a Criminal Trial

No.

A grand jury considers whether charges should proceed. A trial jury determines guilt or innocence after hearing evidence in an adversarial criminal trial.

Prosecutors Must Prove Guilt Beyond a Reasonable Doubt to a Grand Jury

No.

Beyond a reasonable doubt is the standard required for a criminal conviction at trial. The grand jury performs a preliminary charging function and does not determine guilt.

The Defense Presents Its Case to the Grand Jury Just Like at Trial

No.

Federal grand-jury proceedings are not adversarial trials. Defense attorneys do not normally present and cross-examine witnesses inside the grand-jury room.

Refusing to Indict Means the Person Was Acquitted

No.

A grand jury's decision not to indict is not an acquittal, and double jeopardy generally has not attached simply because a grand jury considered the matter.

Every Felony Case Requires a Grand Jury

No.

The Fifth Amendment's grand-jury requirement applies to certain serious federal prosecutions. States are not constitutionally required to use the same grand-jury process and may use other charging procedures.

Grand-Jury Witnesses Have No Fifth Amendment Rights

No.

A witness may invoke the privilege against compelled self-incrimination when the legal requirements for that privilege are satisfied.

Why Grand Juries Matter

Grand juries serve as both an investigative tool and a screening mechanism in the criminal justice system.

In federal cases covered by the Fifth Amendment's Grand Jury Clause, prosecutors cannot simply bring certain serious criminal charges on their own. A grand jury must approve the indictment.

At the same time, the grand-jury process is very different from a trial. Proceedings are generally secret, prosecutors play a central role in presenting evidence, and the accused does not receive the full adversarial procedures that apply once a criminal case reaches court.

Understanding that distinction makes news about grand-jury investigations and indictments much easier to interpret. When a headline says someone was indicted, it means criminal charges have been formally approved. It does not mean the person has been found guilty.

Conclusion

A grand jury is a group of citizens that investigates possible crimes and determines whether certain criminal charges should proceed. In the federal system, grand juries contain between 16 and 23 members, and at least 12 must agree to return an indictment.

An indictment is an accusation, not a conviction. Grand-jury proceedings occur before trial, are generally secret, and operate under different rules from a trial jury. The Fifth Amendment requires grand-jury indictments for certain serious federal crimes, but states may use different procedures because that constitutional requirement has not been applied to the states.
