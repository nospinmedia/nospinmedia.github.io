What Is Due Process?

August 22, 202612 min read

What Is Due Process?

Due process is a constitutional protection that limits how government may deprive a person of life, liberty, or property.

At its core, due process requires government to act according to law and, when protected interests are at stake, provide whatever fair procedures the Constitution requires. Depending on the situation, those protections may include notice of the government's proposed action, an opportunity to challenge it, and a fair decision-making process.

Due process does not guarantee that a person will win a dispute with the government. It requires that government respect constitutional limits when making and carrying out decisions that affect protected interests.

Where Does Due Process Come From?

The Constitution contains two Due Process Clauses.

The Fifth Amendment says the federal government may not deprive a person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.

The Fourteenth Amendment places a similar restriction on state and local governments.

This means due-process protections can apply whether the government action comes from a federal agency, a state, a county, a city, or another state or local governmental body.

Why Does the Constitution Say "Person" Instead of "Citizen"?

Both Due Process Clauses protect persons, not only U.S. citizens.

That distinction matters. Noncitizens within the United States can have constitutional due-process protections, including in immigration proceedings and other government actions affecting protected interests.

That does not mean every person has identical rights in every situation. Immigration status, location, the type of government action, and other circumstances can affect which constitutional protections apply and what procedures are required.

But due process is not a protection reserved exclusively for U.S. citizens.

What Does Life, Liberty, or Property Mean?

Due process becomes relevant when government seeks to deprive someone of a constitutionally protected life, liberty, or property interest.

Life includes the most serious government actions affecting a person's continued existence, including criminal punishment involving the death penalty.

Liberty extends beyond freedom from imprisonment. Depending on the circumstances, constitutionally protected liberty interests can involve physical freedom and other interests recognized by constitutional law.

Property can include traditional property such as money or real estate, but constitutional property interests can also arise from legal entitlements created by law. Certain government benefits, licenses, or public employment rights can qualify when a person has a legitimate legal entitlement rather than merely hoping or expecting to receive them.

Not every government decision affects a protected interest. Determining whether life, liberty, or property is actually at stake is therefore an important first step in a due-process analysis.

What Is Procedural Due Process?

Procedural due process concerns the procedures government must follow before or in connection with depriving someone of a protected interest.

The basic question is: Was the person given the process constitutionally required under the circumstances?

Common procedural protections can include:

  • Adequate notice of the proposed government action.
  • An opportunity to respond or be heard.
  • A meaningful hearing when one is required.
  • An impartial decision-maker.
  • An opportunity to present evidence or arguments in appropriate cases.
  • Other safeguards when necessary to reduce the risk of an erroneous decision.

Not every case requires all of these protections.

Due process is flexible. The procedures required depend on the interests involved and the circumstances of the government action.

Does Due Process Always Require a Court Hearing?

No.

Due process does not mean every government decision requires a full courtroom trial.

Some situations require extensive judicial procedures. Others may require an administrative hearing, written notice and an opportunity to respond, or another type of review.

In some circumstances, particularly when immediate government action is necessary, a hearing may occur after the initial action rather than before it.

The constitutional question is whether the procedures provided are adequate for the particular deprivation at issue.

What Does Notice and an Opportunity to Be Heard Mean?

Notice and an opportunity to be heard are central concepts in procedural due process.

Notice generally means the government must provide adequate information about the proposed action so the affected person has a meaningful opportunity to understand and respond to it.

An opportunity to be heard means the person must have an appropriate chance to challenge the government's action or the factual and legal basis for it.

The required hearing does not always resemble a criminal trial. Its form depends on the circumstances.

A hearing must also be meaningful. Simply providing a procedure on paper does not necessarily satisfy due process if the procedure does not provide an adequate opportunity to protect the interest at stake.

How Much Process Is Due?

There is no single procedure that applies to every government decision.

For many civil and administrative cases, the Supreme Court uses a framework associated with Mathews v. Eldridge, a 1976 case involving the termination of Social Security disability benefits.

The Mathews framework considers three general factors:

  1. The private interest affected by the government action. The more significant the potential loss, the more important procedural protections may become.
  2. The risk of an incorrect decision under the existing procedures and the value of additional safeguards. Courts consider whether additional procedures would meaningfully reduce the chance of error.
  3. The government's interest. This includes the purpose of the government action and the financial and administrative burdens that additional procedures would impose.

The result is a balancing process rather than a rule requiring the same hearing in every case.

What Is Substantive Due Process?

Substantive due process is different from procedural due process.

Procedural due process asks whether government used constitutionally adequate procedures. Substantive due process concerns constitutional limits on certain government actions regardless of how many procedures the government provides.

The Supreme Court has interpreted the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clauses as protecting certain fundamental rights from government interference even when those rights are not expressly listed in the Due Process Clauses themselves.

Substantive due process has played a significant role in constitutional law and has also generated substantial legal and historical debate over which rights qualify for constitutional protection and how courts should identify them.

The scope of substantive due process has changed through Supreme Court decisions over time. For that reason, claims involving particular unenumerated rights must be evaluated under current constitutional precedent rather than assuming every asserted liberty interest receives heightened constitutional protection.

How Does Due Process Apply in Criminal Cases?

Due process is fundamental to the criminal justice system.

A criminal prosecution can threaten a person's liberty and, in some cases, life or property. Constitutional protections therefore regulate many parts of the process.

Depending on the circumstances, protections in criminal proceedings can involve fair notice of criminal accusations, an impartial tribunal, appropriate evidentiary procedures, and the requirement that guilt be proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

Many specific criminal-procedure rights also come from other constitutional provisions, including the Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, and Eighth Amendments.

Due process works alongside those more specific protections rather than replacing them.

Does Due Process Apply to Government Benefits?

It can.

Government benefits can sometimes constitute protected property interests when the law gives an individual a legitimate entitlement to receive them after specified conditions are met.

If a protected property interest exists, government may have to provide appropriate procedures before terminating or denying the benefit.

The required procedure depends on the benefit and circumstances.

For example, Supreme Court cases involving welfare and Social Security disability benefits have reached different conclusions about exactly when a hearing must occur because the interests, risks, and government programs involved were different.

Does Due Process Apply to Government Jobs?

Sometimes.

A government employee may have a protected property interest in continued employment when a statute, contract, employment rule, or other source of law creates a legitimate entitlement to the job.

An employee who can be dismissed only for specified reasons may therefore have due-process protections that an at-will employee does not have.

When a protected interest exists, government may have to provide notice and an appropriate opportunity to respond before or after termination, depending on the circumstances.

Does Due Process Apply to Professional Licenses?

It can.

Government-issued licenses can create protected interests under certain circumstances. A government agency seeking to revoke or suspend an existing professional license may therefore have to provide constitutionally adequate procedures.

The precise requirements depend on the type of license, applicable law, and nature of the government action.

Do Noncitizens Have Due-Process Rights in Immigration Proceedings?

Yes, due-process protections are not limited to U.S. citizens.

Immigration proceedings are civil rather than criminal proceedings, but government actions involving detention or removal can affect significant liberty interests.

Noncitizens in the United States can therefore have due-process protections in immigration proceedings.

The exact procedures and constitutional protections available depend on the person's circumstances and the type of proceeding. Due process in immigration law does not necessarily provide every protection that applies to a defendant in a criminal prosecution.

This distinction is important: saying that someone has due-process rights does not mean that every type of legal proceeding must use the same procedures.

What Happens During an Emergency?

An emergency does not automatically suspend due process.

Emergency circumstances can, however, affect what process is constitutionally required and when it must occur.

Government sometimes must act immediately to protect public safety, property, or other important interests. When waiting for a full hearing would defeat the purpose of necessary emergency action, the Constitution may permit action before a hearing under appropriate circumstances, provided adequate procedures are available afterward when required.

The existence of an emergency therefore can affect the due-process analysis, but declaring an emergency does not simply erase constitutional protections.

What Is Incorporation?

The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause played a major role in applying many protections in the Bill of Rights to state and local governments.

Originally, the Bill of Rights primarily restricted the federal government. Through a constitutional doctrine known as selective incorporation, the Supreme Court has held that many of those protections also restrict state governments through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Examples include major protections involving freedom of speech, criminal procedure, searches and seizures, and the right to counsel.

Not every provision of the Bill of Rights has been incorporated in exactly the same way. For example, the Fifth Amendment's requirement for grand-jury indictment in certain serious federal criminal cases has not been incorporated against the states.

Is Due Process the Same as Equal Protection?

No.

Due process and equal protection are related constitutional concepts, but they address different questions.

Due process generally concerns whether government has improperly deprived someone of life, liberty, or property and whether constitutionally adequate procedures or substantive protections apply.

Equal protection concerns government treatment of people or groups and whether distinctions drawn by government comply with constitutional requirements.

The Fourteenth Amendment expressly contains both a Due Process Clause and an Equal Protection Clause.

What Happens If the Government Violates Due Process?

The remedy depends on the type of violation and the legal proceeding involved.

A court may prevent an unlawful government action, require additional procedures, overturn or set aside a government decision, order a new hearing, exclude or affect the use of certain evidence in appropriate contexts, or provide another remedy authorized by law.

Some constitutional violations can also support claims for damages under particular statutes or legal doctrines, although significant rules and limitations govern when government officials or entities may be sued.

A due-process violation therefore does not produce one automatic remedy in every case.

Common Misunderstandings

Due Process Means Everyone Gets a Jury Trial

No.

A jury trial is required in certain legal proceedings under specific constitutional provisions, but procedural due process does not require a jury for every government decision.

Due Process Only Protects Criminal Defendants

No.

Due process also applies in many civil and administrative settings involving protected liberty or property interests.

Only U.S. Citizens Have Due-Process Rights

No.

The Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments protect persons, not only citizens. The particular protections available can still depend on the circumstances.

Providing Any Hearing Automatically Satisfies Due Process

No.

The procedure must be constitutionally adequate. A meaningless opportunity to object does not necessarily satisfy the requirement simply because the government labels it a hearing.

Due Process Means the Government Can Never Take Property

No.

Government can sometimes lawfully deprive a person of property after providing constitutionally adequate process. Other constitutional protections, including the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause, may impose additional requirements in particular circumstances.

An Emergency Suspends Due Process

No.

Emergency conditions can affect which procedures are practical and constitutionally required, including whether some procedures may occur after immediate government action. They do not automatically eliminate the Due Process Clauses.

Due Process Guarantees the Correct Outcome

No.

Due process is designed in part to reduce the risk of mistaken or unjustified government decisions, but no procedure can guarantee that every decision will be correct. The Constitution requires adequate protections, not a guarantee that a particular person will prevail.

Why Due Process Matters

Due process places constitutional limits between individuals and government power.

Those limits matter whenever government seeks to imprison someone, terminate certain protected benefits, revoke a protected license, take certain actions affecting property, remove a noncitizen through immigration proceedings, or otherwise interfere with a protected life, liberty, or property interest.

But due process is not a single checklist that applies identically everywhere. The Constitution may require very different protections before imprisoning a criminal defendant than before terminating an administrative benefit.

That leads to the central question courts often must answer: What process is due under these particular circumstances?

Conclusion

Due process means government cannot deprive a person of life, liberty, or property without satisfying constitutional requirements. The Fifth Amendment applies this protection to the federal government, while the Fourteenth Amendment applies it to state and local governments.

Procedural due process focuses on the fairness and adequacy of government procedures, often including notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard. Substantive due process addresses constitutional limits on certain government actions regardless of the procedures used.

The exact protections depend on the circumstances. Due process does not guarantee a particular outcome or require a full trial every time government acts. Its fundamental purpose is to ensure that government power over people's protected interests remains subject to constitutional limits.
