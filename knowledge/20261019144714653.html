What Is a Midterm Election and Why Does It Matter?

October 24, 202610 min read

Every four years, Americans vote in a presidential election. But halfway through each president's four-year term, another major national election takes place.

This is called a midterm election.

Midterm elections determine who will serve in the U.S. House of Representatives, which Senate seats will change hands, and who will hold many state and local offices. Although the presidency is not on the ballot, the results can significantly change how the federal government operates.

A midterm election can determine which political party controls Congress, which legislation advances, how federal spending is negotiated, and how effectively a president can pursue a legislative agenda.

Why Is It Called a Midterm Election?

The name comes from its timing.

A president serves a four-year term. Congressional elections occur every two years, including halfway through that presidential term.

For example, if a presidential election takes place in 2024, the next midterm election occurs in 2026. The following presidential election takes place in 2028.

Midterm elections are held on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November of even-numbered years that fall between presidential elections.

The term midterm is commonly used to describe these elections collectively, even though many of the offices on the ballot have terms that do not correspond to the president's term.

Which Federal Offices Are Elected During the Midterms?

The two main federal offices elected during midterms are members of the U.S. House of Representatives and the U.S. Senate.

Every seat in the House is contested, while approximately one-third of Senate seats are normally up for election.

The presidency and vice presidency are not elected during midterms.

The House of Representatives

The House has 435 voting members, each representing a congressional district.

Representatives serve two-year terms, so all 435 voting seats are regularly contested every two years.

This means every midterm election gives voters the opportunity to choose the entire voting membership of the House.

The number of representatives assigned to each state is based on population, with every state guaranteed at least one representative.

Residents of the District of Columbia and certain U.S. territories also elect delegates or a resident commissioner under separate arrangements. These officials do not have the same full voting powers on the House floor as voting representatives.

The U.S. Senate

The Senate has 100 members, with two senators representing each state.

Senators serve six-year terms, but their elections are staggered so that approximately one-third of Senate seats are contested every two years.

The Senate is divided into three classes for this purpose.

In a typical midterm election, 33 or 34 regularly scheduled Senate seats are on the ballot. Additional seats may be contested through special elections when vacancies occur.

Because only part of the Senate is elected at one time, its membership changes more gradually than the House.

Are Governors Elected During Midterms?

Yes. Many states hold gubernatorial elections during midterm years.

Governors lead their states' executive branches and have responsibilities that can include implementing state laws, proposing budgets, appointing officials, and responding to emergencies.

Most governors serve four-year terms, although New Hampshire and Vermont elect governors to two-year terms.

Not every state elects its governor during the same election cycle. Some hold gubernatorial elections during presidential years, while others use midterm years or odd-numbered years.

The number of governorships contested in a particular midterm depends on these state schedules and any special elections.

What Other Offices Appear on Midterm Ballots?

Midterm elections can involve much more than Congress and governors.

Depending on the state and community, voters may also elect:

  • State senators and representatives.
  • Attorneys general, secretaries of state, and other statewide officials.
  • County commissioners and other county officials.
  • Mayors and city or town council members.
  • School board members.
  • Judges, where judicial elections are used.

Some states and local governments also place constitutional amendments, referendums, bond questions, or other ballot measures before voters.

These measures may address subjects such as taxes, public spending, education, infrastructure, or changes to state law.

The offices and questions on a ballot depend on where the voter lives. Not every state or municipality schedules its local elections during federal midterms.

Why Does Control of Congress Matter?

Congress is the legislative branch of the federal government. It makes federal laws, approves government spending, and exercises oversight of the executive branch.

Congress has two chambers: the House of Representatives and the Senate.

A political party generally controls a chamber when it has enough members, including any supporting independents, to organize the chamber and elect its leadership.

Control matters because congressional leaders have significant influence over committee assignments, legislative schedules, and which proposals receive consideration.

The House elects a Speaker, while the Senate's majority and minority leadership help organize legislative business.

A change in control of either chamber can therefore change congressional priorities and the direction of federal policymaking.

What Happens if the President's Party Controls Congress?

When the president's political party also controls both chambers of Congress, it may be easier for that administration to advance legislation supported by its members.

For example, the president and congressional leaders may share priorities involving taxes, federal spending, healthcare, or other policies.

However, control of Congress does not guarantee that legislation will pass.

Members of the same party can disagree, particularly when legislation affects their states or districts differently.

The Senate also has procedural rules that can require more than a simple majority to advance certain legislation. Some bills may be subject to a filibuster, which generally requires 60 votes to end debate under current Senate rules.

Other types of legislation follow different procedures.

Even when one party controls the presidency and both chambers, legislative negotiations and constitutional requirements still apply.

What Is Divided Government?

Divided government occurs when the presidency and at least one chamber of Congress are controlled by different political parties.

For example, the president's party might control the Senate while the opposing party controls the House.

Alternatively, the opposing party might control both chambers.

Divided government can make it more difficult to pass legislation when the parties disagree on major policies.

Bills generally must pass both the House and Senate in the same form before being presented to the president.

If the chambers cannot agree, legislation may stall or require negotiation.

Divided government does not prevent Congress from passing laws. Bipartisan agreements can still occur, particularly on matters where members find common ground.

The practical effects depend on the composition of Congress, the issues involved, and the willingness of elected officials to negotiate.

Can Midterm Elections Change Federal Spending?

Yes. Congress has constitutional authority over federal appropriations, which provide funding for government programs and operations.

The House and Senate must approve appropriations legislation, and the president generally must sign it for it to become law unless Congress overrides a veto.

A change in congressional control can alter spending priorities, negotiations over federal programs, and the conditions attached to certain funding decisions.

Disagreements over appropriations can also contribute to funding lapses and government shutdowns if required legislation is not enacted in time.

Midterm results do not automatically change the federal budget. Changes require action through the applicable legislative process.

How Do Midterms Affect Presidential Appointments?

The Senate has an important role in confirming many presidential appointments.

These include federal judges, Supreme Court justices, Cabinet secretaries, ambassadors, and other officials whose positions require Senate confirmation.

The president nominates individuals for these positions, but the Senate decides whether to provide its advice and consent.

If control of the Senate changes after a midterm election, the confirmation process may change as well.

Senate leaders and committees influence when nominations receive hearings and votes.

The Senate's constitutional role remains the same regardless of which political party controls it.

The House does not vote to confirm presidential nominees.

Can Midterms Affect Congressional Investigations?

Yes. Congress has oversight responsibilities that allow it to examine federal agencies, government spending, and executive branch activities.

Congressional committees may conduct hearings, request records, issue subpoenas under applicable authority, and investigate matters related to their legislative and oversight responsibilities.

The party controlling a chamber generally has substantial influence over committee leadership and investigative priorities.

A change in congressional control can therefore affect which subjects receive attention and how oversight activities are conducted.

Congressional investigative powers are not unlimited. They are subject to constitutional restrictions, applicable laws, chamber rules, and judicial review.

Can a Midterm Election Remove the President?

No. A midterm election does not directly determine who serves as president.

The president continues serving the elected four-year term unless the office becomes vacant through a constitutionally recognized process.

However, midterm results can affect the president's ability to obtain congressional support for legislation, spending proposals, and appointments.

Congress also has constitutional impeachment powers.

The House has the sole power of impeachment, while the Senate has the sole power to try impeachments. Conviction and removal of a president require a two-thirds vote of senators present.

Winning control of Congress does not automatically remove a president or initiate impeachment proceedings.

Why Is Voter Turnout Often Different in Midterm Elections?

Voter turnout in midterm elections has historically tended to be lower than in presidential elections.

One reason is that presidential elections receive extensive national attention and involve a contest for the country's highest executive office.

Midterms may receive less attention from voters who are primarily interested in presidential politics.

Turnout also varies with the competitiveness of races, public interest in political issues, voter mobilization, and election conditions.

Lower turnout does not make a midterm election less legally important.

The elected officials receive the same constitutional powers and responsibilities regardless of how many eligible voters participate.

Does the President's Party Usually Lose Seats in the Midterms?

Historically, the political party holding the presidency has often lost seats in the House during midterm elections.

Political scientists study several possible explanations, including differences in voter participation, public assessments of the president's performance, and the tendency of some voters to favor divided control of government.

However, this historical pattern is not a rule.

There have been exceptions, and results vary depending on the election, political environment, candidates, and other circumstances.

Past patterns do not determine the outcome of any particular future election.

What Happens After a Midterm Election?

After voting ends, state and local election officials count ballots, complete required verification procedures, and certify results under applicable law.

The newly elected Congress begins its term at noon on January 3 following the election, unless Congress has established a different day by law.

The House elects its Speaker and organizes its committees and other leadership positions.

The Senate continues as an institution, with newly elected and reelected senators joining members whose terms have not expired.

Newly elected governors and other state officials take office according to their states' laws, which may establish different inauguration dates.

The results determine the composition of government for the next legislative period, but policy changes still require the appropriate governmental actions.

Why Do Midterm Elections Matter to Everyday Life?

Federal and state governments make decisions that affect many aspects of daily life.

Congress considers laws and funding involving healthcare, taxes, transportation, national defense, education programs, environmental regulation, and other federal responsibilities.

State legislatures and governors make decisions about state budgets, schools, roads, public safety, and many other matters within state authority.

Local governments may make decisions involving property taxes, land use, public services, and community infrastructure.

Midterm elections determine who will exercise these responsibilities, even though the presidency is not being contested.

The effects of an election depend on the policies that elected officials subsequently propose, negotiate, enact, and implement.

The Bottom Line

A midterm election takes place halfway through a U.S. president's four-year term.

All 435 voting seats in the House of Representatives and approximately one-third of Senate seats are regularly contested. Many states and communities also elect governors, legislators, and other officials or decide ballot measures.

Midterm results can change which party controls the House, the Senate, or both. Those changes can affect legislation, federal spending, oversight, and the confirmation of presidential appointments.

Midterms do not directly elect or remove the president, but they help determine the political and legislative environment in which the president governs.

Although they occur between presidential elections, midterms are a central part of how Americans choose the officials who make and oversee government decisions.
