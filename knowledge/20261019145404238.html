Why Does a War in the Middle East Affect Gas Prices in America?

October 27, 202614 min read

A war thousands of miles from the United States can cause gasoline prices at American gas stations to rise within days, even if the United States is not directly involved in the fighting.

That happens because crude oil is traded in an international market. Oil produced in one country can affect prices paid by buyers in many others, including countries that produce substantial amounts of their own petroleum.

The Middle East is especially important because several countries in the region are major oil producers and exporters. The region also contains shipping routes used to transport petroleum to international markets.

A conflict that threatens production facilities, pipelines, ports, or shipping lanes can raise oil prices by reducing supplies or increasing the risk of future disruptions.

Those higher costs can eventually reach American drivers through refineries, fuel distributors, and gas stations.

Why Is the Middle East Important to Global Oil Supplies?

The Middle East contains some of the world's largest petroleum reserves and several major oil-producing countries.

Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Iran, Kuwait, and the United Arab Emirates are among the region's important producers.

Oil from these countries supplies refineries and consumers in many parts of the world, particularly Asia and Europe, as well as other international markets.

The region's importance is not limited to the amount of oil it produces. It also includes export terminals, pipelines, storage facilities, and shipping routes that connect producers with customers.

When a conflict threatens these systems, buyers around the world may become concerned about whether enough oil will remain available.

Even countries that import relatively little oil directly from the affected region can experience higher prices because they participate in the same interconnected market.

Why Does a Global Oil Market Affect American Prices?

Crude oil is an internationally traded commodity.

Although different types of crude oil have different prices, major oil markets are connected through international trade.

A refinery in Asia, Europe, or North America may purchase crude oil from several possible suppliers, depending on price, quality, transportation costs, and refinery requirements.

When oil becomes scarce in one region, buyers may seek replacement supplies from other producers.

That additional demand can increase prices elsewhere.

For example, suppose a conflict interrupts oil exports from a Middle Eastern country.

Refineries that normally buy that oil may turn to suppliers in the United States, West Africa, South America, or other producing regions.

Those alternative suppliers may then receive more purchase requests, creating upward pressure on their prices.

American refiners must compete in this broader market, even if their crude oil comes from domestic producers.

The result is that a supply problem in one part of the world can influence petroleum prices across international borders.

But Doesn't the United States Produce Its Own Oil?

Yes. The United States is one of the world's largest crude oil producers and has substantial domestic production.

However, producing large amounts of oil does not isolate a country from global oil prices.

American oil producers generally operate in a market where prices are influenced by international supply and demand.

If crude oil prices rise worldwide, domestic producers can often sell their oil at higher prices too, subject to differences in crude quality, transportation costs, and regional market conditions.

American refineries purchasing that crude may therefore face higher costs.

The United States also both imports and exports petroleum because different crude oils and refined products serve different refinery and market needs.

Some American refineries are designed to process particular grades of crude oil, including heavier varieties that may be imported.

Meanwhile, certain types of domestically produced oil may be exported when international buyers offer competitive prices.

Domestic production improves the country's supply position, but it does not create a separate American oil market disconnected from world prices.

What Happens When a War Threatens Oil Production?

A conflict can affect petroleum supplies in several ways.

Oil wells, processing facilities, pipelines, storage terminals, and export ports may be damaged or forced to suspend operations.

Workers may be unable to reach facilities safely, while transportation companies may suspend deliveries because of security concerns.

Electricity, communications, and other infrastructure necessary for production can also be disrupted.

If oil production declines, the amount available for sale may fall.

When demand remains relatively stable but available supply decreases, buyers may offer higher prices to secure the oil they need.

The size of the price increase depends on how much production is affected, how long the disruption lasts, and whether other producers can increase their output.

Not every conflict causes a major supply interruption. The market response depends on the actual and anticipated effects on petroleum production and transportation.

What Is the Strait of Hormuz and Why Does It Matter?

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea.

It is one of the world's most important petroleum shipping routes.

Large quantities of crude oil and petroleum products produced in Persian Gulf countries travel through the strait aboard commercial tankers.

Liquefied natural gas shipments also pass through the waterway.

Because the strait is geographically narrow and strategically important, threats to shipping can create concern about the movement of energy supplies.

A military confrontation, attack on a commercial vessel, naval restriction, or other security incident could interrupt traffic or make shipping more expensive.

Some oil can be transported through pipelines that bypass the strait, but alternative routes do not necessarily have enough capacity to replace all shipments that normally pass through it.

A major disruption could therefore affect buyers well beyond the Middle East.

The Strait of Hormuz matters not because every gallon of American gasoline originates there, but because a significant portion of internationally traded petroleum depends on the route.

Can Gas Prices Rise Even if No Oil Supply Has Been Interrupted?

Yes.

Oil prices respond not only to current supply and demand but also to expectations about future conditions.

Suppose fighting begins near major petroleum facilities, but production continues normally.

Traders, refiners, and other buyers may still become concerned that the conflict could expand or disrupt exports.

Some buyers may seek additional supplies or adjust their purchasing decisions to reduce the risk of shortages.

The market price can rise because participants assign greater importance to the possibility of future supply problems.

This is sometimes described as a geopolitical risk premium.

A risk premium is not a fixed charge added by a government or a single company. It is a way of describing how uncertainty and potential disruptions can influence market prices.

If the perceived risk later decreases, some of that upward pressure may disappear.

However, the price response is not automatic or predictable. Other changes in supply, demand, and inventories may occur at the same time.

How Do Shipping Costs Affect Oil Prices During a War?

Moving oil from producers to refineries requires tankers, pipelines, terminals, and other infrastructure.

During a conflict, commercial shipping companies may face additional risks.

Insurance costs can increase, particularly for vessels traveling through areas where attacks or military activity are possible.

Shipowners may demand higher freight rates, avoid certain routes, or delay voyages.

If ships must travel farther to reach their destinations, transportation becomes more expensive and deliveries take longer.

These additional costs can affect the delivered price of crude oil and refined petroleum products.

A conflict can therefore influence energy prices even when oil production itself has not been significantly reduced.

Shipping disruptions may also create temporary shortages in particular regions if replacement supplies cannot arrive quickly enough.

How Does Higher Crude Oil Turn Into Higher Gasoline Prices?

Crude oil is the primary raw material used to manufacture gasoline.

Refineries purchase crude oil and process it into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and other petroleum products.

When crude oil becomes more expensive, refiners generally face higher raw-material costs.

Those costs influence wholesale gasoline prices, which affect the prices paid by distributors and retailers.

Gas stations then establish retail prices based on their fuel acquisition costs, expected replacement costs, operating expenses, taxes, and competition.

The process can be summarized as follows:

  1. A conflict threatens or reduces oil supplies.
  2. Crude oil prices rise in international markets.
  3. Refineries face higher crude oil costs.
  4. Wholesale gasoline prices may increase.
  5. Distributors and gas stations face higher fuel costs.
  6. Retail gasoline prices rise as those costs move through the supply chain.

The relationship is not perfectly proportional. Refinery conditions, gasoline demand, regional inventories, and other factors can strengthen or weaken the effect.

Why Can Gasoline Prices Rise Before the More Expensive Oil Arrives?

Retail gasoline prices do not depend only on what a station paid for the fuel currently in its tanks.

Businesses also consider what replacement supplies are expected to cost.

If wholesale gasoline prices rise sharply, a station may increase its retail price before receiving a new delivery.

That can happen because the station expects to pay more to replenish its inventory.

Refiners and distributors also respond to current and anticipated market conditions rather than waiting until every barrel purchased at an earlier price has been processed or sold.

This helps explain why drivers may see prices increase soon after international oil markets react to a conflict.

The physical movement of petroleum takes time, but price information can travel almost immediately.

Does the United States Have to Import Oil From the Middle East for Prices to Rise?

No.

The United States can experience higher gasoline prices even if the particular oil used by an American refinery was produced entirely within the country.

Consider a simplified example.

A major overseas supply disruption causes international buyers to seek additional oil from American producers.

Those producers now have more potential customers competing for their output.

The price of domestic crude oil may rise as a result.

An American refinery purchasing domestic crude may therefore pay more, even though its physical supply route has not changed.

This is a central feature of internationally connected commodity markets: a disruption can affect prices through competition among buyers, not just through direct dependence on a particular supplier.

Can Other Countries Replace the Missing Oil?

Sometimes, but the ability to replace disrupted supplies depends on several factors.

Other producers may have spare production capacity, meaning they can increase output beyond their current levels.

However, not every producer can quickly expand production.

Oil fields, wells, processing equipment, pipelines, and export facilities have physical operating limits.

Some producers may already be operating near their available capacity.

Even when additional crude oil is available, it may differ in quality from the oil that refiners normally use.

Transportation capacity and delivery times also matter.

A relatively small disruption may be absorbed through additional production, existing inventories, or changes in trade flows.

A larger or prolonged disruption can be harder to replace, increasing pressure on prices.

What Role Does OPEC Play?

OPEC, the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, is a group of oil-producing nations that coordinates petroleum policies among its members.

Several important Middle Eastern oil producers belong to OPEC.

OPEC members, along with certain cooperating nonmember producers in the broader OPEC+ arrangement, may agree on production targets or adjustments.

These decisions can influence the amount of oil supplied to international markets.

During a disruption, additional production from countries with available capacity may help offset lost supplies.

However, production decisions depend on each country's circumstances, agreements, infrastructure, and other considerations.

OPEC does not directly set the retail gasoline price at American stations.

Its influence operates primarily through petroleum supply and the international market for crude oil.

Can the Strategic Petroleum Reserve Help?

The United States maintains the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, commonly called the SPR, as an emergency stockpile of crude oil.

The reserve is intended to help address significant petroleum supply disruptions and other circumstances authorized by federal law.

When legally authorized, crude oil can be released from the reserve and made available to the market.

Additional supply may help reduce shortages or ease upward pressure on crude oil prices.

However, the reserve has limits.

Oil can be withdrawn and delivered only within the capabilities of its facilities and transportation systems. The reserve also contains crude oil rather than finished gasoline ready for immediate sale at retail stations.

A release does not guarantee that gasoline prices will fall by a particular amount.

Its effect depends on the size and duration of the disruption, the amount released, and broader market conditions.

Why Do Some Wars Have a Bigger Effect on Oil Prices Than Others?

Not every war in the Middle East produces the same reaction in energy markets.

The effect depends on where the fighting occurs, which infrastructure is threatened, and how the conflict changes expectations about supply.

A conflict far from major production facilities and shipping routes may have a limited direct impact on petroleum markets.

A conflict that interrupts a large export terminal, damages a major refinery, or threatens a heavily used shipping route may have a much larger effect.

Markets also consider whether the disruption is likely to last days, weeks, or months.

Available inventories, spare production capacity, and worldwide demand influence how easily lost supplies can be replaced.

Oil prices may even decline during a conflict if other market forces, such as weakening global demand or increased production elsewhere, outweigh the supply risks.

War does not automatically cause oil prices to rise. The economic effect depends on how the conflict changes the expected balance between supply and demand.

Why Can Gas Prices Stay High After Fighting Stops?

A ceasefire or reduction in fighting does not necessarily restore oil supplies immediately.

Damaged facilities may require repairs. Ports and pipelines may need inspections, and shipping companies may wait for security conditions to stabilize.

Insurance costs and transportation arrangements may take time to return to earlier levels.

Even if crude oil prices fall quickly, wholesale gasoline and retail pump prices may adjust at different speeds.

Economists have documented situations in which gasoline prices rise faster in response to increasing costs than they fall when costs decline. This is sometimes called the rockets-and-feathers effect.

Retail inventories, replacement costs, refining margins, and local competition can contribute to these differences.

As a result, the end of a conflict does not guarantee an immediate return to previous gasoline prices.

Does Producing More Oil in America Prevent These Price Increases?

Additional domestic production can strengthen supply and may help moderate prices, depending on market conditions.

But increasing oil production is not always immediate.

New wells, pipelines, processing facilities, and other infrastructure may require substantial investment and time.

Domestic production also remains connected to international pricing because oil is traded across borders.

Even if the United States produced enough crude oil to meet its own total refinery needs, American prices could still respond to global supply disruptions through trade and competition.

The degree of exposure would depend on the structure of domestic production, refining, transportation, and international trade.

Producing more oil can influence supply conditions, but it does not automatically eliminate the connection between American gasoline prices and world oil markets.

How Can Drivers Understand Whether a Conflict Is Affecting Gas Prices?

Several indicators help explain the connection.

International crude oil benchmarks, including Brent, provide information about global petroleum market conditions.

West Texas Intermediate, commonly called WTI, is another important crude oil benchmark used in the United States.

Wholesale gasoline prices show how changes in crude oil and gasoline supply conditions are affecting the market for finished fuel.

Reports on refinery operations, petroleum inventories, shipping disruptions, and oil production can provide additional context.

Comparing these indicators is more useful than assuming every retail price change has a single cause.

A gasoline price increase during a conflict may reflect international oil costs, but seasonal demand, refinery problems, taxes, and local supply conditions can also contribute.

The Bottom Line

A war in the Middle East can raise gasoline prices in the United States because oil is traded in an interconnected international market.

The region contains major petroleum producers and critical export routes. Threats to production, transportation, or future supplies can increase crude oil prices even before a physical shortage occurs.

Those higher prices can affect American refiners, wholesalers, distributors, and gas stations, regardless of whether the oil they handle comes directly from the Middle East.

American oil production helps supply the domestic market, but it does not completely separate the country from international price movements.

The size and duration of any gasoline price increase depend on the actual disruption, available alternative supplies, refining and transportation conditions, and worldwide demand.

The essential connection is economic rather than geographic: when a disruption changes the price of oil in the global market, consumers thousands of miles away can feel the effect at the pump.
