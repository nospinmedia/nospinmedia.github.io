How Do Voting Machines Work?

July 23, 20265 min read

How Do Voting Machines Work?

Voting machines are devices used to record and, in many cases, count votes during an election. In the United States, there is no single nationwide voting machine. Instead, states and local governments choose from certified voting systems that meet federal and state requirements.

Most modern voting systems use either paper ballots that are scanned electronically or touchscreen devices that print a voter-verifiable paper record.

Although the technology varies, every voting system is designed to accurately record each voter's selections while protecting ballot secrecy.

What Types of Voting Machines Are Used?

Most U.S. elections use one of two primary voting systems.

Optical Scan Systems

Optical scan systems use paper ballots.

Voters fill in bubbles or mark their choices on a paper ballot, which is then fed into a scanner. The scanner reads the marks electronically while preserving the original paper ballot for recounts or audits.

Today, optical scan systems are the most widely used voting technology in the United States.

Direct Recording Electronic (DRE) Systems

Some jurisdictions use touchscreen voting machines known as Direct Recording Electronic (DRE) systems.

Many modern DRE systems include a Voter-Verified Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT) that prints a paper record the voter can review before casting the ballot. This paper record can be used during audits or recounts.

Some older DRE systems did not produce a paper record, but many states have moved away from those systems.

What Happens After You Cast Your Ballot?

After a ballot is cast, the voting system securely stores the vote.

For paper-ballot systems, both the electronic tally and the original paper ballot are retained.

At the close of voting, election officials collect vote totals from each polling place. These unofficial results are combined with absentee, mail, military, overseas, and provisional ballots according to state election laws.

Official results are certified only after required verification procedures have been completed.

Are Voting Machines Connected to the Internet?

Most voting machines used to cast and count ballots are not connected to the internet while voting is taking place.

Many voting systems are intentionally designed to operate as standalone devices to reduce cybersecurity risks.

Election jurisdictions may use separate computer systems to report unofficial election-night results, but those systems are generally distinct from the voting machines used to record ballots.

How Are Voting Machines Tested?

Voting equipment undergoes multiple layers of testing before an election.

These may include:

  • Testing by federally accredited laboratories.
  • Certification under federal guidelines.
  • State certification and approval.
  • Public logic and accuracy testing before Election Day.
  • Testing after software updates or equipment changes.

Many states also conduct additional testing before and after elections to verify that equipment is functioning correctly.

How Are Elections Audited?

Many states perform post-election audits to compare paper ballots with the electronic vote totals.

Depending on state law, these audits may include:

  • Random sample audits.
  • Risk-limiting audits.
  • Full recounts when required.
  • Manual reviews of paper ballots.

These procedures help confirm that voting equipment accurately recorded and counted votes.

Who Certifies Voting Machines?

Voting systems may be tested and certified through the U.S. Election Assistance Commission (EAC) using the current Voluntary Voting System Guidelines (VVSG 2.0).

Independent laboratories evaluate voting equipment against federal standards for security, accessibility, reliability, and functionality before a system may receive federal certification.

VVSG 2.0 is the current federal standard for new EAC certification applications, replacing the earlier VVSG 1.0 and 1.1 testing framework for newly submitted systems.

However, each state decides which certified voting systems it will approve for use under its own election laws.

Current Certification Status

As of 2026, the EAC continues administering its existing testing and certification program through its professional staff and accredited laboratories. However, because the commission currently lacks a quorum of commissioners, it cannot formally adopt new voting system guidelines or approve certain commission-level policy changes until new commissioners are nominated and confirmed.

Can Voting Machines Be Hacked?

Like most electronic systems, voting equipment can face cybersecurity risks, which is why election officials use multiple layers of security.

Common safeguards include:

  • Paper ballots or paper audit trails.
  • Physical security controls.
  • Secure storage and chain-of-custody procedures.
  • Pre-election testing.
  • Post-election audits.
  • Limited network connectivity.

No voting system is considered completely immune from potential threats. Instead, election security relies on multiple independent safeguards designed to detect problems, verify results, and provide evidence for audits or recounts when necessary.

Why Are Voting Machines Frequently in the News?

Voting machines are often mentioned during discussions about:

  • Election security.
  • Recounts.
  • Election audits.
  • Voting system certification.
  • Cybersecurity.
  • Election law.
  • Federal and state election guidance.

Questions about voting technology frequently arise during close elections or when jurisdictions replace or upgrade their voting equipment.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that every voting machine in the United States is the same. In reality, states and local jurisdictions use a variety of certified voting systems.

Another misunderstanding is that voting machines send votes directly over the internet while people are voting. Most voting systems used to cast ballots operate without an internet connection during voting.

Finally, voting machines do not independently certify election results. Election officials verify vote totals through canvassing, audits, and certification procedures established under state law.

Conclusion

Voting machines are designed to help record and count votes accurately while protecting ballot secrecy and supporting election integrity. Most modern U.S. voting systems rely on paper ballots or voter-verifiable paper records, combined with testing, certification, physical security, and post-election audits. Although the technology differs from state to state, election officials use multiple safeguards to verify that votes are accurately recorded and counted.
