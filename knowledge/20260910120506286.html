What Causes Thunder and Lightning?

September 18, 20268 min read

Lightning is a powerful electrical discharge produced when electrical charges build up and separate inside a thunderstorm. Thunder is the sound created when lightning rapidly heats the surrounding air, causing it to expand explosively and produce a shock wave.

The two are parts of the same event. Lightning produces thunder, which means there is no such thing as lightning from a thunderstorm that does not create thunder. Sometimes the thunder simply occurs too far away to be heard.

How Does a Thunderstorm Become Electrically Charged?

Thunderstorms contain powerful updrafts and downdrafts that move water droplets, ice crystals, slush-like particles called graupel and sometimes hail through different parts of the cloud.

As these particles collide, electrical charge is transferred between them. The exact details of thunderstorm electrification are complex, but scientists understand that collisions among different types of ice particles, combined with strong vertical air currents, play a major role in separating electrical charge within the cloud.

Typically, much of the upper portion of a thunderstorm becomes positively charged while a large region lower in the cloud becomes negatively charged. Smaller regions with different charges can also develop.

This separation creates an enormous electrical potential difference — somewhat like the voltage between the terminals of a battery, but on a vastly larger scale.

How Does Lightning Begin?

Air normally acts as an electrical insulator, meaning electrical current does not easily pass through it.

As opposite charges become increasingly separated inside a storm, however, the electric field can become strong enough to begin breaking down the insulating properties of the air.

A channel of electrically conductive, ionized air starts developing through the atmosphere. Once a connection is established between regions with sufficiently different electrical charges, a powerful electrical current can flow through the channel.

That discharge is lightning.

The entire process happens extremely quickly, and what appears to the eye as a single flash can actually consist of several rapid electrical pulses traveling along the same general channel.

Does Lightning Always Strike the Ground?

No. In fact, lightning can occur in several different ways.

A large amount of lightning occurs within a single cloud, where electrical discharges travel between differently charged regions. Lightning can also travel from one cloud to another or between a cloud and the surrounding air.

Cloud-to-ground lightning occurs when an electrical connection develops between a storm cloud and Earth's surface.

The negative charge commonly found near the lower part of a thunderstorm can influence electrical charges on the ground below. Electrons in the ground are repelled, leaving the surface beneath the storm relatively positively charged.

As a negatively charged channel called a stepped leader moves downward from the cloud, upward-moving electrical channels called streamers can develop from trees, buildings, towers and even the ground itself.

When a connection is made, a powerful electrical current called a return stroke travels through the completed channel. This produces the brilliant flash commonly recognized as a lightning strike.

Not all cloud-to-ground lightning follows this exact charge arrangement. Positive lightning can also occur when positive charge from a storm connects with the ground.

How Hot Is Lightning?

The electrical current in a lightning channel heats the surrounding air extremely rapidly.

The temperature of the channel can reach roughly 30,000 kelvins, or around 30,000 degrees Celsius — several times hotter than the visible surface of the Sun.

This extreme temperature lasts only briefly, but it heats the surrounding air almost instantaneously.

That sudden heating is what produces thunder.

What Causes Thunder?

When lightning heats the air around its channel, the air expands extremely rapidly.

The expansion is so sudden that it produces a pressure wave, initially behaving like a shock wave. As that wave travels away from the lightning channel, it becomes the sound wave we hear as thunder.

Thunder is therefore not caused by clouds crashing together, nor is it a separate event that happens after lightning.

It is the sound produced by the lightning discharge itself interacting with the atmosphere.

Why Do We See Lightning Before We Hear Thunder?

Light travels enormously faster than sound.

The light from a nearby lightning strike reaches your eyes almost instantaneously for practical purposes. Sound travels through air much more slowly, at roughly 343 meters per second under typical conditions near sea level, although its exact speed changes with temperature and other atmospheric conditions.

That is why you see the flash before hearing the thunder.

The farther away the lightning is, the longer the delay becomes.

A useful approximation is that every five seconds between the flash and thunder represents about one mile of distance. In metric units, about three seconds corresponds to roughly one kilometer.

This estimate tells you approximately how far away the lightning was, not necessarily how far away the entire thunderstorm is.

Why Does Thunder Sometimes Crack and Sometimes Rumble?

Nearby lightning can produce an extremely sharp crack or explosive boom. More distant lightning often produces a long, rolling rumble.

There are several reasons for the difference.

A lightning channel can extend for several kilometers and contain many branches. Sound from different portions of that channel travels different distances before reaching you. The sound therefore arrives over a period of time rather than all at once.

Sound can also be reflected and refracted by terrain, buildings and different layers of air in the atmosphere.

Higher-frequency sounds tend to weaken more rapidly with distance than lower-frequency sounds. As a result, distant thunder often loses its sharp cracking sound and becomes a deeper rumble.

Can There Be Lightning Without Thunder?

Every ordinary lightning discharge produces thunder, but you may not always hear it.

Thunder becomes difficult to hear when lightning is sufficiently far away. Atmospheric conditions and surrounding noise also affect how far the sound travels.

People sometimes call distant flashes heat lightning, particularly when they see lightning on a warm summer night but hear no thunder.

Heat lightning is not a special type of lightning caused by heat. It is usually ordinary lightning from a thunderstorm too far away for its thunder to be heard.

Can There Be Thunder Without Lightning?

Thunder from a thunderstorm requires lightning. The electrical discharge is what creates the rapid heating and expansion that produces the sound.

Someone may hear thunder without seeing the corresponding flash because the lightning is hidden inside clouds, behind terrain or outside their field of view.

So the absence of a visible flash does not mean lightning did not occur.

Why Does Lightning Strike Tall Objects?

Lightning does not simply choose the tallest object in an area, but height can influence where a connection develops between a storm and the ground.

Tall objects reduce the distance between the ground and the charged region above and can help upward electrical streamers develop. Trees, towers, antennas and tall buildings are therefore frequent strike points.

But lightning can and does strike open ground and shorter objects. Being near something taller does not guarantee protection.

Lightning can also travel through the ground after a strike or jump between nearby objects, which is why standing under an isolated tree during a thunderstorm is dangerous.

Can Lightning Strike the Same Place Twice?

Yes.

The idea that lightning never strikes the same place twice is a myth. Tall buildings, communication towers and other prominent structures can be struck repeatedly.

Some structures are struck many times in a single year because their height and location make them favorable points for electrical discharges.

A previous lightning strike does nothing to prevent another one from occurring at the same location.

What Is Positive Lightning?

Most cloud-to-ground lightning transfers negative charge toward the ground, but some strikes carry positive charge from positively charged regions of a thunderstorm.

Positive lightning is less common but can be particularly powerful. It can originate from portions of a storm far from the heaviest rain and may strike outside the area people normally think of as being underneath the thunderstorm.

This helps explain why lightning can sometimes strike when the storm itself appears to be some distance away.

How Far Away Can Lightning Be Dangerous?

Lightning can strike outside the area of heavy rain and away from the darkest part of a thunderstorm.

If thunder can be heard, lightning is close enough to present a potential danger. A storm does not need to be directly overhead for a strike to occur nearby.

A substantial building or a hard-topped enclosed vehicle provides much better protection than remaining outdoors. Open shelters, isolated trees and partially enclosed structures do not provide reliable lightning protection.

The Bottom Line

Lightning and thunder begin with electrical charges separating inside a thunderstorm. Collisions among water droplets and ice particles, combined with powerful air currents, help create regions of different electrical charge.

When the electrical difference becomes strong enough, the insulating air breaks down and a large electrical discharge occurs. That discharge is lightning.

Lightning heats the surrounding air to tens of thousands of degrees in a fraction of a second. The air expands violently, producing the pressure wave we hear as thunder.

We see the lightning first simply because light travels vastly faster than sound. The brilliant flash and the rolling thunder are therefore not two separate weather events — they are different effects of the same enormous electrical discharge in the atmosphere.
