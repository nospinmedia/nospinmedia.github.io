How Does a Tsunami Form?

September 17, 20268 min read

A tsunami forms when a large amount of water is suddenly displaced, sending a series of powerful waves outward across the ocean. Most tsunamis are caused by large earthquakes beneath or near the ocean, but volcanic eruptions, landslides and other events can also produce them.

Unlike ordinary waves created mainly by wind, a tsunami involves movement through a large portion of the water column, from the ocean surface toward the seafloor. This allows tsunami waves to carry enormous amounts of energy across entire ocean basins.

In deep water, a tsunami may be difficult to notice. The danger becomes much greater as the waves reach shallow coastal waters, where they slow down, grow in height and can send water far inland.

How Does an Earthquake Create a Tsunami?

Most large tsunamis begin with an undersea earthquake at a tectonic plate boundary.

The most dangerous situations often occur at subduction zones, where one tectonic plate is being forced beneath another. The plates may become locked together while geological forces continue pushing them.

Stress can build for decades or centuries. When the fault finally slips during a large earthquake, part of the seafloor can suddenly rise or fall.

The ocean above the moving seafloor is displaced with it.

Gravity then tries to restore the water to its normal level. The displaced water begins moving outward, creating a series of waves that can travel enormous distances from the earthquake.

Does Every Undersea Earthquake Cause a Tsunami?

No.

An earthquake must move enough water to generate a significant tsunami. Several factors determine whether that happens.

The earthquake generally needs to be sufficiently large and relatively shallow beneath the seafloor. The type of fault movement also matters greatly.

Earthquakes that cause substantial vertical movement of the seafloor are particularly capable of generating tsunamis because they push large volumes of water upward or allow them to drop downward.

A large earthquake involving mainly horizontal movement may produce little or no tsunami because it does not significantly displace the water above it.

The size of the rupture, water depth and shape of the seafloor also influence the resulting waves.

A Tsunami Is Usually More Than One Wave

A tsunami is not normally a single giant wave.

It is a series of waves produced by the original displacement of water. These waves may arrive minutes to more than an hour apart, depending on the event and local geography.

The first wave is not necessarily the largest.

Later waves can be higher and more destructive, which is why returning to the shoreline immediately after the first wave passes can be extremely dangerous.

Tsunami activity can continue for hours as waves reflect from coastlines and interact with underwater terrain.

How Fast Can a Tsunami Travel?

In the deep ocean, tsunami waves can travel extraordinarily fast — several hundred kilometers per hour. In very deep water, their speed can approach that of a commercial jet aircraft.

Their speed depends primarily on the depth of the water. The deeper the ocean, the faster the wave can travel.

Despite this speed, a tsunami in the open ocean may have a relatively small wave height and an extremely long wavelength — the distance between successive wave crests can extend for many kilometers.

A ship far offshore might pass over a tsunami without its crew noticing anything unusual.

That changes dramatically as the tsunami approaches land.

Why Does a Tsunami Get Bigger Near Shore?

As a tsunami enters shallower water, interaction with the seafloor causes the wave to slow down.

The energy carried by the wave does not simply disappear. As the wave slows and its wavelength becomes shorter, the water can build upward and the wave height can increase.

This process is known as shoaling.

The exact behavior depends strongly on the shape of the coastline and seafloor. Bays, harbors and underwater features can concentrate tsunami energy in certain locations, making water levels much higher in one area than another nearby.

This is why the same tsunami can produce dramatically different effects along different sections of coastline.

Does a Tsunami Always Look Like a Giant Breaking Wave?

No.

Movies often portray tsunamis as enormous curling waves towering over a city. A tsunami can sometimes form a steep wall of water, but many real tsunamis look very different.

The water may instead rise rapidly like an extremely fast and powerful flood. It can surge inland around buildings, vehicles and trees while carrying large amounts of debris.

Because a tsunami can involve an enormous volume of moving water, even a wave that does not resemble a traditional breaking surf wave can be devastating.

The water flowing back toward the ocean can also be extremely dangerous, carrying people, debris and damaged structures with it.

Why Does the Ocean Sometimes Suddenly Pull Back?

One of the most recognizable natural tsunami warning signs is an unusual and rapid retreat of the ocean from the shoreline.

This can happen when the trough, or low portion, of a tsunami reaches the coast before the crest. Water is drawn away from shore, sometimes exposing areas of the seafloor that are normally underwater.

This does not happen before every tsunami, so its absence does not mean the coast is safe.

But if the ocean suddenly recedes an unusual distance, it can be a warning that a powerful surge of water may follow. People should move to higher ground or farther inland immediately rather than approaching the exposed seafloor.

Can Landslides Cause Tsunamis?

Yes.

A large landslide entering the ocean, or an underwater landslide moving along the seafloor, can suddenly displace enough water to generate a tsunami.

Landslide-generated tsunamis can be particularly dangerous close to their source. In confined bays or narrow bodies of water, a large mass of rock entering the water can produce extremely high local waves.

Earthquakes can also trigger underwater landslides, meaning an earthquake may generate a tsunami both through movement of the seafloor and indirectly by causing sediment to collapse.

Can Volcanoes Cause Tsunamis?

Yes.

Volcanic eruptions can generate tsunamis in several ways. Part of a volcanic island can collapse into the ocean, an underwater eruption can displace water, or a large explosion can rapidly disturb the sea.

The collapse of a volcanic structure can move enormous quantities of rock and water in a short period, potentially producing destructive waves.

Large volcanic events can therefore generate tsunamis even when no major tectonic earthquake occurs.

Can a Meteorite or Asteroid Cause a Tsunami?

A sufficiently large asteroid or other space object striking an ocean could displace enormous amounts of water and generate powerful waves.

Such events are possible but extremely rare compared with earthquake-generated tsunamis.

For modern tsunami hazards, earthquakes, landslides and volcanic activity are far more important sources.

How Are Tsunamis Detected?

Tsunami warning systems use several types of instruments.

Seismometers can quickly detect large earthquakes and determine their approximate location, depth and magnitude. This allows scientists to estimate whether an earthquake has the potential to generate a tsunami.

Ocean instruments can then help determine whether a tsunami was actually produced. Deep-ocean systems can detect changes in water pressure as a tsunami passes overhead and transmit measurements for analysis. Coastal tide gauges measure changes in sea level as waves reach shorelines.

Scientists combine these observations with computer models to estimate when waves may arrive and which coastlines could be threatened.

How Much Warning Is Possible?

The amount of warning depends largely on distance from the tsunami's source.

If a tsunami is generated on the other side of an ocean, authorities may have hours to issue warnings and evacuate threatened areas.

A local tsunami can arrive much faster — sometimes within minutes of the earthquake that caused it. In those situations, there may not be enough time to wait for an official alert.

Strong or prolonged earthquake shaking near a coastline is therefore itself an important natural warning sign. People in a tsunami hazard area may need to move immediately to higher ground or farther inland.

What Is the Difference Between a Tsunami and a Tidal Wave?

Tsunamis are sometimes called tidal waves, but that term is misleading.

Normal ocean tides are caused primarily by the gravitational effects of the Moon and Sun. Tsunamis are caused by sudden displacement of water, usually from earthquakes and sometimes from landslides or volcanic activity.

Tides can affect the final water level reached when a tsunami arrives, but they do not cause the tsunami itself.

The Bottom Line

A tsunami forms when a large volume of ocean water is suddenly displaced. Most major tsunamis are caused by undersea earthquakes that rapidly raise or lower part of the seafloor, although landslides and volcanic eruptions can also generate them.

The resulting waves can travel across deep oceans at hundreds of kilometers per hour while remaining relatively difficult to notice. As they reach shallow coastal water, they slow down, become compressed and can grow dramatically in height.

A tsunami is usually a series of waves rather than one giant wave, and the first is not necessarily the largest. What begins as a sudden movement of the seafloor can therefore send destructive surges of water across an ocean and onto coastlines thousands of kilometers away.
