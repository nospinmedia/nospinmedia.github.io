What Is Budget Reconciliation?

August 29, 202614 min read

What Is Budget Reconciliation?

Budget reconciliation is a special congressional process used to consider certain legislation affecting federal spending, revenue, or the debt limit under expedited procedures.

Its most important effect in the Senate is that debate on a reconciliation bill is limited. Because debate cannot continue indefinitely, the ordinary legislative filibuster cannot be used to block the bill in the usual way.

That means qualifying reconciliation legislation can pass the Senate with a simple majority rather than needing the 60 votes normally required to invoke cloture on most filibustered legislation.

But reconciliation is not a general shortcut for passing anything with 51 votes. The process begins with the congressional budget and is restricted by detailed rules governing what provisions may be included.

Why Does Budget Reconciliation Exist?

Congress created the modern reconciliation process in the Congressional Budget Act of 1974.

The purpose is to help Congress bring existing spending and revenue laws into line with the fiscal goals established in a congressional budget resolution.

For example, Congress may establish a budget plan calling for specified reductions in spending, increases or decreases in revenue, or other changes affecting the federal budget. Reconciliation instructions can then direct congressional committees to develop legislation producing those budgetary results.

Over time, reconciliation has become an important tool for enacting major tax and spending legislation when the majority party does not have enough Senate votes to overcome an ordinary filibuster.

What Is a Budget Resolution?

A budget resolution is Congress's framework for federal spending, revenues, deficits, debt, and related fiscal policy.

The House and Senate agree to the same budget resolution, but it is a concurrent resolution, not an ordinary law. It does not go to the president for signature and does not itself appropriate money or change tax law.

Instead, it establishes congressional budget targets and can contain instructions that begin the reconciliation process.

What Are Reconciliation Instructions?

A budget resolution may contain reconciliation instructions directing one or more House or Senate committees to recommend legislation that changes spending, revenues, or the debt limit by specified amounts.

The instructions generally tell a committee the budgetary result it must achieve and a deadline for submitting its recommendations. They do not necessarily dictate every policy choice the committee must make.

For example, a committee could be instructed to develop legislation reducing the federal deficit by a specified amount over a particular period. The committee would then determine what qualifying policy changes within its jurisdiction could satisfy that instruction.

How Does the Reconciliation Process Work?

Although the details can vary, the process generally works like this:

  1. Congress adopts a budget resolution.
  2. The budget resolution includes reconciliation instructions directing specified committees to produce legislation meeting particular budgetary targets.
  3. The instructed committees develop legislative recommendations within their jurisdictions.
  4. The recommendations are combined into reconciliation legislation when multiple committees are involved.
  5. The House and Senate consider the legislation under their respective procedures.
  6. The Senate uses special expedited procedures, including limited debate.
  7. The House and Senate must ultimately approve identical legislation.
  8. The bill goes to the president, who may sign or veto it like other legislation.

Reconciliation changes the congressional procedure used to consider the bill. It does not eliminate the normal constitutional requirement that both chambers pass the same legislation and present it to the president.

Why Can Reconciliation Avoid the Ordinary Filibuster?

The modern Senate filibuster works largely because debate on ordinary legislation can continue unless the Senate votes to invoke cloture.

For most legislation, cloture normally requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn—usually 60 votes in a 100-member Senate.

Reconciliation legislation is different because the Congressional Budget Act limits Senate debate. Once the allotted debate time has expired, senators cannot simply keep debate going indefinitely.

That removes the procedural basis for an ordinary legislative filibuster and allows the Senate to reach a final vote without first obtaining the usual 60-vote cloture majority.

A reconciliation bill can therefore pass with a simple majority. In a 50-50 Senate, the vice president can cast a tie-breaking vote on final passage.

How Long Can the Senate Debate a Reconciliation Bill?

The Congressional Budget Act generally limits Senate debate on a reconciliation measure to 20 hours.

Limiting debate is central to reconciliation's expedited status. Senators cannot keep the bill from reaching a vote simply by extending debate indefinitely.

However, the end of debate does not necessarily mean the Senate immediately finishes considering amendments. That leads to the procedure commonly known as a vote-a-rama.

What Is a Vote-a-Rama?

A vote-a-rama is an extended series of amendment votes that can occur near the end of Senate consideration of a budget or reconciliation measure.

Once the statutory debate time has expired, senators may continue offering qualifying amendments, but debate on them is greatly restricted or unavailable. The Senate can therefore move through amendment votes one after another, sometimes for many hours.

Some amendments are intended to change the legislation directly. Others may force senators to take public positions on politically significant issues.

The term vote-a-rama is informal, but it has become a familiar part of modern Senate budget proceedings.

What Is the Byrd Rule?

The Byrd Rule is the principal Senate restriction preventing reconciliation bills from becoming vehicles for policy provisions that are considered extraneous to the budget process.

Named for Senator Robert C. Byrd, the rule is contained in Section 313 of the Congressional Budget Act.

It allows senators to raise points of order against provisions that fail the rule's requirements.

A provision can be considered extraneous under the Byrd Rule for several reasons, including if it:

  • Does not produce a change in federal spending or revenue.
  • Produces a budgetary change that is merely incidental to the provision's broader non-budgetary policy effects.
  • Falls outside the jurisdiction of the committee that submitted it.
  • Produces budgetary effects that do not comply with the committee's reconciliation instructions.
  • Increases the deficit outside the period covered by the reconciliation measure under the applicable rule.
  • Makes changes to Social Security that are prohibited from reconciliation consideration.

These restrictions are why simply having some connection to money or the federal budget does not automatically make a provision eligible for reconciliation.

What Does "Merely Incidental" Mean?

One of the most important and debated parts of the Byrd Rule concerns provisions whose budgetary effects are merely incidental to their broader policy effects.

A proposal may technically change federal spending or revenue but still be vulnerable if its primary effect is a major policy change and the budgetary impact is secondary to that policy.

This prevents Congress from automatically putting any regulatory or social policy into reconciliation merely by attaching a small budgetary consequence to it.

Determining whether a provision satisfies this test can involve detailed procedural and budget analysis.

What Is a Byrd Bath?

A Byrd bath is the informal name for the review process in which senators and committee staff present arguments about whether provisions in a reconciliation bill comply with the Byrd Rule.

The Senate parliamentarian evaluates the procedural arguments and advises the Senate's presiding officer about how the Byrd Rule applies.

During this process, provisions considered vulnerable may be rewritten, narrowed, or removed before floor consideration.

A Byrd bath is not a vote of the Senate and does not determine whether the underlying policy is good or bad. It concerns whether the provision qualifies for the special reconciliation process.

Does the Senate Parliamentarian Decide What Can Become Law?

No.

The Senate parliamentarian is a nonpartisan official who advises the presiding officer and senators on Senate rules, precedents, and procedure.

During reconciliation, the parliamentarian provides important advice about whether provisions comply with the Byrd Rule.

But the parliamentarian does not personally enact legislation, veto provisions, or possess an independent constitutional vote over Congress.

The Senate itself retains ultimate authority over its proceedings, while the presiding officer makes procedural rulings that can be challenged under Senate procedures.

Can the Senate Waive the Byrd Rule?

Yes, but doing so generally requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn, normally 60 votes in a full Senate.

If a senator raises a valid Byrd Rule point of order against a provision, the provision can be removed unless the Senate successfully waives the rule or otherwise resolves the procedural issue.

This creates an important limitation on reconciliation: the process avoids the ordinary 60-vote filibuster, but a provision that violates the Byrd Rule may itself need 60 votes to survive a procedural challenge.

Can Reconciliation Be Used to Change Social Security?

The Byrd Rule specifically protects Social Security from changes through reconciliation.

Provisions making changes to the Social Security program covered by the rule are considered extraneous and cannot simply be included in reconciliation legislation under the expedited process.

This restriction is separate from the broader question of whether other federal benefit programs may be affected through reconciliation. Programs such as Medicare or Medicaid can be affected when proposed provisions otherwise satisfy reconciliation requirements.

What Can Budget Reconciliation Be Used For?

Reconciliation has commonly been used for legislation involving:

  • Federal taxes.
  • Mandatory spending programs.
  • Health-care spending.
  • Deficit reduction or increases.
  • Certain federal benefit provisions.
  • The statutory debt limit.
  • Other policies with qualifying federal budget effects.

The crucial issue is not the political subject of the proposal but whether the provision satisfies the budget resolution's instructions and Senate reconciliation rules.

Can Reconciliation Be Used for Any Policy That Costs Money?

No.

Nearly every major federal policy has some financial effect, but that alone does not make it eligible for reconciliation.

The Byrd Rule looks at the nature of the budgetary effect, committee jurisdiction, compliance with reconciliation instructions, long-term deficit effects, and whether the financial consequences are merely incidental to broader policy changes.

A proposal can therefore have a measurable cost or savings and still be excluded from reconciliation.

Can Reconciliation Increase the Federal Deficit?

It can, depending on the budget resolution and the time period involved.

Reconciliation has been used both to reduce deficits and to enact policies that increase projected deficits within the budget window.

However, the Byrd Rule restricts provisions that increase the deficit beyond the period covered by the reconciliation measure under its applicable test.

This rule has influenced the design of major tax legislation. Congress has sometimes made provisions temporary so their budgetary effects do not violate reconciliation restrictions outside the budget window.

Can Reconciliation Be Used Only Once a Year?

The common statement that Congress can use reconciliation only once per year is an oversimplification.

The Congressional Budget Act allows a budget resolution to contain reconciliation instructions dealing with spending, revenues, and the debt limit.

Senate precedent generally prevents Congress from using more than one reconciliation measure from the same budget resolution for each of those major categories. If instructions affecting multiple categories are combined into one reconciliation bill, using that bill can consume the available reconciliation opportunity for each category it contains.

How many reconciliation measures are practically available therefore depends on the budget resolution, the instructions it contains, which budgetary categories are involved, and Senate precedent.

Congress also does not necessarily adopt a new budget resolution every calendar year, and budget resolutions are tied to fiscal years rather than a simple once-per-calendar-year rule.

Does the House Have a Filibuster Problem With Reconciliation?

No.

The House does not operate under the Senate's modern filibuster system. House rules generally allow the majority to limit debate and bring legislation to a vote.

Reconciliation's special importance is therefore most visible in the Senate, where its statutory debate limits prevent the ordinary legislative filibuster from operating.

The House still must pass the reconciliation bill, and the House may amend or reject Senate provisions just as it can with other legislation.

Does Reconciliation Bypass the President?

No.

A reconciliation bill is still legislation.

After the House and Senate pass identical versions, the bill is presented to the president under the normal constitutional process.

The president may sign the bill into law or veto it. If the president vetoes the measure, Congress would need the constitutionally required two-thirds vote in each chamber to override the veto.

Reconciliation changes Senate consideration of legislation. It does not eliminate presidential participation in lawmaking.

Does Reconciliation Bypass the House?

No.

Both chambers of Congress must pass identical legislation before it can be sent to the president.

The Senate's ability to consider reconciliation under expedited procedures does not allow senators to enact a law without approval from the House.

Differences between House and Senate versions must therefore be resolved before final enactment.

Has Budget Reconciliation Been Used by Both Political Parties?

Yes.

Congressional majorities from both major political parties have used reconciliation to enact significant legislation.

Examples over the decades have included:

  • Spending and tax changes during the Reagan administration.
  • Deficit-reduction legislation in the 1990s.
  • Tax cuts enacted during the George W. Bush administration.
  • The Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010, which made changes associated with the Affordable Care Act and other programs.
  • The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017.
  • The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021.
  • The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022.

The process is therefore not inherently associated with one political party or one type of fiscal policy.

Is Reconciliation the Same as the Federal Budget?

No.

The congressional budget resolution establishes a fiscal framework for Congress. Reconciliation is a legislative process that may be triggered by instructions contained in that budget resolution.

The budget resolution itself generally does not directly change taxes or spending laws.

Reconciliation legislation can make those statutory changes because, unlike the budget resolution, it is a bill that passes both chambers and is presented to the president.

Is Reconciliation the Same as Appropriations?

No.

Appropriations legislation generally provides budget authority for discretionary federal agencies and programs.

Reconciliation is generally used to change laws governing revenues, mandatory spending, or the debt limit in accordance with budget instructions.

The processes serve different purposes and operate under different congressional rules.

This distinction helps explain why reconciliation cannot simply replace the regular appropriations process used to fund much of the federal government each year.

Common Misunderstandings

Reconciliation Lets the Senate Pass Anything With 51 Votes

No.

Only legislation that satisfies reconciliation rules receives the expedited treatment, and the Byrd Rule can remove provisions considered extraneous to the budget process.

Reconciliation Eliminates the Filibuster

No.

The ordinary legislative filibuster continues to exist. Reconciliation is a separate procedure with statutory debate limits that prevent the ordinary filibuster from blocking qualifying reconciliation legislation.

Anything That Affects the Federal Budget Can Be Included

No.

The Byrd Rule imposes additional requirements, including the rule against provisions whose budgetary effects are merely incidental to broader policy changes.

The Senate Parliamentarian Personally Decides What Becomes Law

No.

The parliamentarian advises the Senate on rules and precedents. The official does not independently enact or veto legislation.

Reconciliation Can Be Used Only Once Every Calendar Year

Not exactly.

The number of reconciliation measures available depends on the budget resolution, its instructions, the spending, revenue, and debt-limit categories involved, and Senate precedent.

Reconciliation Bills Do Not Need the House

False.

The House and Senate must pass identical legislation.

Reconciliation Bills Do Not Need the President's Signature

False.

Like other bills, reconciliation legislation must be presented to the president and can be vetoed.

The Vice President Cannot Affect Reconciliation

False.

Because final passage can occur by simple majority, the vice president may break a 50-50 Senate tie.

Reconciliation Can Freely Change Social Security

No.

The Byrd Rule specifically treats qualifying changes to Social Security as extraneous to reconciliation.

Why Budget Reconciliation Matters

Budget reconciliation can dramatically affect what legislation is politically possible in the Senate.

A majority party with 51 Senate votes—or 50 senators plus the vice president—may be unable to overcome a filibuster on ordinary legislation but still be able to enact qualifying tax or spending legislation through reconciliation.

At the same time, the Byrd Rule, reconciliation instructions, committee jurisdiction, budgetary scoring, and other procedural restrictions limit what that majority can include.

That means the most useful question when politicians say they plan to pass something through reconciliation is not simply whether the policy affects money. The better question is: Does each major provision satisfy the rules that qualify legislation for reconciliation's expedited Senate treatment?

Conclusion

Budget reconciliation is a special congressional process created by the Congressional Budget Act of 1974 to help Congress align spending, revenues, and debt policy with its budget goals.

The process begins with reconciliation instructions in a congressional budget resolution. Committees then develop qualifying legislation, which must ultimately pass both the House and Senate and be presented to the president.

Reconciliation is especially powerful in the Senate because debate is limited, preventing the ordinary legislative filibuster from blocking the measure. That allows qualifying legislation to pass with a simple majority.

But reconciliation is not an unrestricted 51-vote shortcut. The Byrd Rule excludes provisions considered extraneous to the budget process, and waiving a Byrd Rule objection generally requires 60 votes. Understanding those restrictions explains both why reconciliation is such a powerful legislative tool and why congressional majorities cannot use it to enact every policy they support.
