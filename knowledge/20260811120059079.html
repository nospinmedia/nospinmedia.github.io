What Is the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact?

August 11, 20265 min read

What Is the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact?

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact (NPVIC) is an agreement among participating U.S. states and the District of Columbia to award their electoral votes to the winner of the nationwide popular vote, regardless of which candidate wins within their own state.

The compact was created to ensure that the candidate receiving the most votes across the entire country becomes President of the United States while keeping the Electoral College in place. It is one of the most significant proposed changes to presidential elections in modern American history.

Why Was the Compact Created?

Supporters of the compact argue that every vote should carry equal weight, no matter where it is cast.

Under the current Electoral College system, presidential campaigns often focus heavily on a relatively small number of competitive swing states. States that consistently vote for one political party may receive less campaign attention because their outcomes are considered more predictable.

The compact was developed as an alternative to a constitutional amendment, which is generally much more difficult to adopt.

How Does the Compact Work?

The U.S. Constitution gives each state the authority to decide how its presidential electors are appointed.

Most states currently award all of their electoral votes to the candidate who wins that state's popular vote.

Under the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, participating states agree that once the compact becomes active, they will instead award all of their electoral votes to the candidate who wins the nationwide popular vote.

For example, if Candidate A wins the national popular vote but loses a participating state, that state would still award its electoral votes to Candidate A because the compact requires participating states to follow the national result rather than their own statewide outcome.

When Would the Compact Take Effect?

One of the most important features of the compact is that it does not take effect immediately.

It becomes active only after states representing at least 270 electoral votes—the number required to elect a president—have joined the agreement.

As of July 2026, the compact has been enacted by 18 states and the District of Columbia, representing a total of 222 electoral votes. That leaves it 48 electoral votes short of the 270 required for implementation.

Until that threshold is reached, participating states continue awarding their electoral votes under their existing state laws.

This provision is intended to prevent the compact from changing election outcomes until it has enough participating states to determine the presidency.

Does the Compact Eliminate the Electoral College?

No.

The Electoral College would still exist.

Electors would continue to be appointed, electoral votes would still be cast, and the constitutional process for electing the president would remain in place.

The proposed change affects how participating states choose to award their electoral votes, not whether the Electoral College itself exists.

Why Do Supporters Favor the Compact?

Supporters argue that the compact would:

  • Ensure the nationwide popular vote winner becomes president.
  • Encourage presidential candidates to campaign throughout the country rather than concentrating on swing states.
  • Give voters in every state a more equal role in determining the election outcome.
  • Avoid the need for a constitutional amendment.

Supporters also point out that the Constitution gives states broad authority to determine how their presidential electors are selected.

Why Do Critics Oppose the Compact?

Critics raise several concerns.

Some argue that the compact could reduce the influence of smaller or less populous states by encouraging campaigns to focus primarily on areas with large populations.

Others question whether the compact would withstand constitutional challenges, particularly because it involves an agreement among multiple states affecting presidential elections.

Some critics also suggest that a very close nationwide popular vote could increase the likelihood of nationwide recounts or election disputes.

These legal and policy questions continue to be debated by scholars, lawmakers, and courts.

Has the Compact Taken Effect?

No.

As of July 2026, 18 states and the District of Columbia have enacted legislation joining the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, representing 222 electoral votes.

Virginia became the newest member on April 13, 2026, when Governor Abigail Spanberger signed House Bill 965 and Senate Bill 322 into law, adding the Commonwealth's 13 electoral votes to the compact.

Because the compact requires participation from states representing at least 270 electoral votes before it can be implemented, it remains 48 electoral votes short of taking effect. Until that threshold is reached, participating states continue allocating their electoral votes according to their existing election laws.

Common Misunderstandings

Several misconceptions frequently arise when the compact is discussed.

One is that it abolishes the Electoral College. It does not. The Electoral College would continue to operate under the Constitution.

Another misconception is that states joining the compact immediately begin awarding their electoral votes based on the national popular vote. In reality, the compact remains inactive until participating states collectively represent at least 270 electoral votes.

It is also sometimes assumed that the compact changes the Constitution. Instead, it relies on each state's existing constitutional authority to determine how its electors are appointed.

Conclusion

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact is a proposed agreement among states to award their electoral votes to the winner of the nationwide popular vote rather than the winner of each participating state. It is designed to work within the existing Electoral College system and would only take effect if states representing at least 270 electoral votes join the agreement.

As of July 2026, the compact has secured 222 electoral votes, leaving it 48 votes short of becoming active. Although it has not yet taken effect, it remains one of the most significant proposals for changing how presidential elections are decided in the United States and continues to be the subject of legal, political, and constitutional debate.
