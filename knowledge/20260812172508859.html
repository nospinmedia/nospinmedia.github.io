What Is the National Guard and Who Controls It?

The National Guard is a military force with both state and federal roles. Its members can serve under the command of a governor during state emergencies or become part of the federal military under the president.

That dual role is what makes the National Guard different from the active-duty military and other reserve forces—and it is also the source of frequent confusion when Guard deployments appear in the news.

The simplest answer to "Who controls the National Guard?" is: it depends on the Guard members' legal duty status.

Guard members generally serve in one of three statuses: State Active Duty, Title 32, or Title 10. Those statuses determine who commands them, who pays for the mission, and which laws govern what they may do.

What Is the National Guard?

The National Guard consists primarily of the Army National Guard and Air National Guard.

Guard members have a dual state-federal status. They serve in their state's National Guard while also belonging to the Army National Guard of the United States or Air National Guard of the United States, which are reserve components of the U.S. Armed Forces.

This allows the same Guard units and personnel to perform missions for their states and, when placed into federal service, missions for the United States.

What Does the National Guard Do?

National Guard missions can range from responding to emergencies at home to participating in military operations overseas.

State missions may include responding to:

  • Hurricanes and floods.
  • Wildfires.
  • Severe winter storms.
  • Public health emergencies.
  • Civil disturbances.
  • Other emergencies in which a governor needs additional personnel or equipment.

Federal missions may include:

  • Overseas military deployments.
  • Combat operations.
  • Homeland defense.
  • Federal emergency missions.
  • Training and support operations.
  • Other missions authorized under federal law.

Whether a particular mission is considered state or federal depends on the legal authority under which Guard members are serving.

What Are the Three National Guard Duty Statuses?

The three basic statuses are State Active Duty, Title 32, and Title 10.

Understanding these statuses is the easiest way to understand who controls the National Guard.

What Is State Active Duty?

Under State Active Duty, Guard members are activated by their governor under state law.

They remain under state command and are considered state employees for the mission. Their pay and benefits are generally determined by state law rather than the federal military pay system, although federal disaster funding may sometimes reimburse states for certain costs.

Governors commonly use State Active Duty for emergencies such as natural disasters or other situations requiring state military assistance.

In this status:

  • Command: State governor.
  • Legal authority: State law.
  • Funding: Primarily state funding, although federal reimbursement may sometimes be available.
  • Military status: State rather than federal service.

What Is Title 32 Status?

Title 32 refers to Title 32 of the United States Code, which governs important aspects of the National Guard.

Under Title 32 status, Guard members remain under the command and control of their state or territorial governor while performing federally authorized or federally funded duties.

This creates a state-federal combination: the federal government may fund and regulate the mission, but the governor retains command.

Traditional Guard training and many full-time Guard positions operate under Title 32 authority. Title 32 can also be used for certain federally supported domestic missions.

In this status:

  • Command: State or territorial governor.
  • Funding: Federal government.
  • Legal framework: Federal Title 32 authority while remaining under state command.

This distinction can be extremely important during domestic deployments because Guard members under Title 32 remain under state control rather than becoming federal active-duty forces.

What Is Title 10 Status?

Title 10 is federal military status.

When National Guard members are ordered into federal service under Title 10 authority, they become federally controlled and federally funded. For the duration of that federal service, they operate within the federal military chain of command rather than under their governor's command.

Title 10 status is commonly used for overseas deployments, combat missions, federal military operations, and other missions requiring Guard members to serve as federal forces.

In this status:

  • Command: Federal chain of command under the president.
  • Funding: Federal government.
  • Legal authority: Federal military law.
  • Military status: Federal active-duty service.

When Guard members enter federal active duty, federal law generally relieves them from their state National Guard duty for the duration of that federal service.

Can the President Federalize the National Guard?

Yes, when federal law provides authority to do so.

Congress has enacted laws allowing the president to call National Guard members and units into federal service under specified circumstances.

For example, 10 U.S.C. § 12406 authorizes the president to call National Guard forces into federal service when the United States or certain territories or possessions are invaded or in danger of invasion, when there is a rebellion or danger of rebellion against federal authority, or when the president is unable to execute federal law using the regular forces.

Other federal statutes provide additional authorities for mobilizing reserve forces under particular circumstances.

Once Guard members are properly placed into federal Title 10 service, they operate under federal command.

Does the President Need a Governor's Permission to Federalize the Guard?

Not necessarily.

Governors normally control their National Guard forces when those forces are operating in state-controlled status. But Congress has given the president statutory authority to call Guard forces into federal service under specified conditions.

When the legal requirements for a federal call-up are satisfied, a governor does not necessarily have the power to prevent the federalization simply by refusing consent.

The exact authority matters, however. A president cannot federalize the Guard merely by declaring that a state mission is now federal; the action must rest on constitutional or statutory authority.

What Is the Insurrection Act?

The Insurrection Act is a group of federal statutes that allows the president, under specified circumstances, to use federal armed forces and federalized National Guard forces domestically to enforce federal law or respond to insurrection, rebellion, or certain forms of domestic violence.

The law provides exceptions to the usual restrictions on using federal military forces for civilian law enforcement.

The circumstances in which the Insurrection Act can be invoked involve significant constitutional and legal questions, so the law is best understood separately from the National Guard's ordinary state and federal roles.

What Is the Posse Comitatus Act?

The Posse Comitatus Act generally restricts the use of federal military forces to execute civilian laws unless the Constitution or Congress provides an exception.

National Guard status matters greatly when applying this rule.

Guard members serving under State Active Duty or Title 32 generally are not subject to the Posse Comitatus Act in the same way as federal Title 10 forces because they remain under state command.

When National Guard members are federalized under Title 10, they generally become subject to the restrictions that apply to federal military forces.

There are statutory exceptions, including authorities such as the Insurrection Act, so the statement that "the military can never perform domestic law enforcement" is too broad.

Can National Guard Members Act as Police?

Sometimes, depending on their duty status, mission, and applicable law.

National Guard forces operating under state authority may perform certain law-enforcement-related functions when state law and the governor's orders permit them to do so.

Title 32 Guard forces also remain under state command, which can make their legal position different from federal active-duty troops.

Federalized Title 10 forces generally face the restrictions imposed on federal military participation in civilian law enforcement, unless a constitutional or statutory exception applies.

This is why knowing whether Guard personnel are serving under State Active Duty, Title 32, or Title 10 can be crucial when evaluating a domestic deployment.

What Is Different About the D.C. National Guard?

The District of Columbia is a major exception to the normal state National Guard command structure.

D.C. does not have a governor. The president is commander in chief of the District of Columbia National Guard, with command authority exercised through the federal chain of command and delegated officials.

As a result, the mayor of Washington, D.C. does not command the D.C. National Guard in the way a state governor commands a state's Guard.

This difference frequently becomes important during National Guard deployments in the nation's capital.

Can National Guard Units Serve in Other States?

Yes.

States regularly assist one another during disasters and other emergencies. Governors can provide National Guard forces to another state through interstate arrangements, including mechanisms such as the Emergency Management Assistance Compact (EMAC).

Guard members sent to another state through these arrangements generally remain under the authority established for that state-controlled mission rather than automatically becoming federal troops simply because they cross a state line.

The federal government can also deploy or mobilize Guard forces under separate federal authority.

Can the National Guard Be Sent Overseas?

Yes.

National Guard units and individual Guard members routinely serve on overseas federal missions.

When ordered to federal active duty, Guard personnel can deploy for combat operations, military exercises, security missions, humanitarian operations, and other assignments just like other components of the U.S. Armed Forces.

Overseas deployments are commonly performed under Title 10 federal authority.

Is the National Guard Part of the U.S. Military?

Yes.

The Army National Guard of the United States and Air National Guard of the United States are reserve components of the U.S. Armed Forces.

The Guard's state role does not make it separate from the national military system. Its dual status allows Guard members to serve both their states and the federal government.

How Is the National Guard Different From the Army Reserve and Air Force Reserve?

The major difference is the National Guard's state role.

Army Reserve and Air Force Reserve forces are federal reserve components. They do not normally operate under the command of state governors.

The National Guard can operate under state command or federal command depending on its duty status.

That means a governor can activate National Guard forces for a state emergency but cannot similarly activate Army Reserve or Air Force Reserve units as state forces.

Who Pays the National Guard?

It depends on duty status.

Under State Active Duty, the state generally pays Guard members according to state law, although federal disaster or emergency funding may sometimes reimburse eligible costs.

Under Title 32, the federal government funds the Guard members while the governor retains command.

Under Title 10, the federal government both funds and controls the forces.

This is another reason reports about a National Guard deployment should identify the legal status under which the troops are serving.

Common Misunderstandings

The Governor Always Controls the National Guard

No.

Governors normally command Guard forces operating in state-controlled status, but Guard members placed into federal Title 10 service operate under the federal military chain of command.

The President Always Controls the National Guard

No.

Most National Guard forces ordinarily remain under state command unless they are placed into federal service. The D.C. National Guard is an important exception because the District does not have a governor.

The National Guard Is Just a State Militia

Not in the ordinary sense of that phrase.

Today's National Guard has a dual state-federal role. Its Army and Air components are integrated into the U.S. military's reserve structure while also serving as organized state military forces when not federalized.

National Guard Members Cannot Deploy Overseas

False.

National Guard units and personnel regularly deploy overseas as federal military forces.

A Governor Can Always Refuse a Presidential Federalization Order

No.

Federal law gives the president authority to call National Guard forces into federal service under specified circumstances. Whether a particular federalization is lawful depends on the legal authority being used and the circumstances.

National Guard Troops Can Always Perform Civilian Law Enforcement

No.

Their authority depends heavily on whether they are serving under State Active Duty, Title 32, or Title 10 and on the federal and state laws governing the particular mission.

Crossing a State Line Automatically Makes the Guard Federal

No.

States can send Guard forces to assist other states while those forces remain in a state-controlled status.

Why National Guard Status Matters

News reports often say that "the National Guard has been deployed" without immediately explaining who ordered the deployment or what legal authority is being used.

Those details can fundamentally change what the deployment means.

A governor activating Guard members under state authority is legally different from a federally funded Title 32 mission, and both are different from the president placing Guard members into federal Title 10 service.

Duty status affects the chain of command, funding, benefits, applicable laws, and potentially what Guard members are permitted to do during a domestic mission.

As a result, one of the most useful questions to ask when reading about any National Guard deployment is: Under what authority and duty status are the Guard members serving?

Conclusion

The National Guard is unique because it serves both state and federal governments. Governors normally command their Guard forces during state-controlled missions, while the president commands Guard members who have been placed into federal Title 10 service.

State Active Duty places Guard members under state control and generally state funding. Title 32 keeps them under a governor's command while providing federal funding. Title 10 places them under federal command and funding as federal military forces.

Understanding those three statuses explains why both a governor and a president can truthfully be described as controlling the National Guard under different circumstances—and why the legal status of a Guard deployment matters.
