How Does Refrigeration Keep Food Cold?

September 22, 20269 min read

A refrigerator keeps food cold by moving heat from inside the refrigerator to the surrounding room. It does not create cold in the same way a furnace creates heat. Instead, it uses electrical energy and a circulating refrigerant to collect heat from the refrigerated space and release that heat outside the appliance.

This continuous movement of heat is called the refrigeration cycle. The same basic principle is used in freezers, air conditioners and many heat pumps.

Why Does Heat Need to Be Removed?

Heat naturally flows from warmer objects toward colder ones. If a refrigerator were simply an insulated box, its interior would gradually warm as heat entered from the surrounding room, especially whenever the door was opened.

A refrigerator must therefore continually remove the heat that enters through its walls, door seals, food and incoming air.

The insulation around the compartment slows heat transfer, while the refrigeration system removes the heat that still gets inside.

What Is Refrigerant?

The refrigeration system contains a fluid called a refrigerant.

A refrigerant is chosen because it can efficiently absorb and release heat while changing pressure and, during parts of the cycle, changing between liquid and vapor.

The refrigerant circulates through a sealed system of tubing and components. It is reused continuously rather than being consumed during normal operation.

By controlling the refrigerant's pressure, the refrigerator can make it absorb heat at a low temperature inside the appliance and release heat at a higher temperature outside it.

What Are the Main Parts of a Refrigerator?

A typical vapor-compression refrigerator relies on four major components: the compressor, condenser, expansion device and evaporator.

Together, these components repeatedly change the pressure, temperature and physical state of the refrigerant.

The cycle can be thought of as four basic steps: compress the refrigerant, release heat, reduce its pressure and absorb heat.

Step 1: The Compressor Raises the Pressure

The cycle begins at the compressor, which is usually located near the bottom rear of a household refrigerator.

The compressor draws in low-pressure refrigerant vapor and compresses it.

Compression raises the refrigerant's pressure and temperature. The refrigerant leaving the compressor is therefore a hot, high-pressure vapor.

The compressor requires electricity to do this work. It is one of the main reasons a refrigerator consumes electrical energy.

Step 2: The Refrigerant Releases Heat

The hot, high-pressure refrigerant then flows through the condenser.

In many refrigerators, the condenser is located on the back or underneath the appliance. Its job is to transfer heat from the refrigerant into the surrounding room air.

Because the refrigerant is hotter than the room, heat flows from the refrigerant to the surrounding environment.

As it loses heat, the refrigerant condenses from vapor into a high-pressure liquid.

This is why parts of a refrigerator can feel warm. The appliance is releasing heat that was removed from its interior, along with additional heat resulting from the electrical work done by the compressor.

Step 3: The Refrigerant's Pressure Drops

The high-pressure liquid refrigerant next passes through an expansion device, often a narrow capillary tube in household refrigerators.

The expansion device restricts the refrigerant's flow and produces a large pressure drop between the high-pressure and low-pressure sides of the system.

As the refrigerant enters the lower-pressure region, some of it rapidly evaporates and the mixture becomes much colder.

The refrigerant is now at a sufficiently low temperature to absorb heat from inside the refrigerator.

Step 4: The Refrigerant Absorbs Heat

The cold, low-pressure refrigerant enters the evaporator.

The evaporator is located inside or adjacent to the refrigerated compartment. Air from the refrigerator or freezer passes across the cold evaporator surface.

Heat flows from the warmer interior air and food toward the colder refrigerant.

As the refrigerant absorbs this heat, it evaporates into a low-pressure vapor.

The vapor then returns to the compressor, and the entire cycle begins again.

The refrigerator therefore continually transports heat from inside the appliance to the room outside it.

Why Does Evaporation Cool Things?

Changing a liquid into a vapor requires energy.

When refrigerant evaporates inside the evaporator, it absorbs energy from its surroundings. That energy comes largely from heat inside the refrigerator.

This principle is not unique to refrigerators. Evaporation also helps cool your body when sweat evaporates from your skin.

In a refrigeration system, however, pressure is carefully controlled so the refrigerant can evaporate at a temperature low enough to cool the refrigerator compartment.

Why Does Changing Pressure Matter?

The boiling temperature of a substance depends on pressure.

Reducing pressure lowers the temperature at which a refrigerant can boil. Increasing pressure allows it to condense at a higher temperature.

The refrigeration cycle takes advantage of this relationship.

On the low-pressure side of the system, refrigerant can evaporate at a temperature cold enough to absorb heat from inside the refrigerator. On the high-pressure side, it can release that heat and condense at a temperature above that of the surrounding room.

The compressor and expansion device maintain this pressure difference.

Does a Refrigerator Actually Produce Cold?

In everyday language, it is perfectly reasonable to say that a refrigerator makes things cold. Physically, however, the process is better understood as removing heat.

Cold is not a substance that the refrigerator pumps into the food.

Thermal energy is transferred from the food and air inside the refrigerator into the refrigerant. The refrigeration system then carries that energy outside and releases it into the room.

The contents become colder because they have lost thermal energy.

Why Is the Back or Bottom of a Refrigerator Warm?

The outside of a refrigerator can feel warm because that is where the heat removed from the interior is released.

The condenser transfers this heat into the room.

The refrigerator also releases energy associated with the work performed by the compressor and other electrical components. As a result, the total heat released into the room is greater than the heat removed from the refrigerated compartment alone.

This leads to an interesting consequence: leaving a refrigerator door open cannot cool a closed room. Over time, an operating refrigerator with its door open actually adds net heat to the room because of the electrical energy it consumes.

How Does Cold Air Reach the Food?

Refrigerator designs vary.

In many modern refrigerator-freezer combinations, much of the cooling occurs at an evaporator near the freezer compartment. A fan circulates cold air through ducts into the refrigerator section.

Other designs position cooling components differently.

Air circulation is important because it distributes heat from the food and interior air toward the evaporator, where that heat can be transferred to the refrigerant.

Overpacking a refrigerator can sometimes block airflow and create areas that are warmer than others.

Why Does Frost Form Inside a Freezer?

Air contains water vapor. When humid air enters a freezer and encounters surfaces below freezing, moisture can condense and freeze.

Older freezers often required periodic manual defrosting because ice accumulated on the evaporator and other cold surfaces.

Most modern frost-free refrigerators periodically warm the evaporator enough to melt accumulated frost. The resulting water drains away and eventually evaporates outside the refrigerated compartment.

This automatic defrost process helps maintain airflow and efficient heat transfer.

Why Does Opening the Door Make a Refrigerator Work Harder?

When the refrigerator door opens, some cold interior air mixes with warmer room air. The incoming air also often contains additional moisture.

After the door closes, the refrigerator must remove the added heat and, in many cases, deal with moisture that entered with the room air.

The longer and more frequently the door remains open, the more heat enters the appliance and the more work the refrigeration system must perform to restore the desired temperature.

Warm food placed inside creates a similar effect because its thermal energy must also be removed.

Why Does a Refrigerator Turn On and Off?

A refrigerator does not normally need to run its compressor continuously.

Temperature sensors or thermostatic controls monitor conditions inside the appliance. When the temperature rises above the desired range, the cooling system operates. Once enough heat has been removed, the compressor can shut off.

The insulation then slows the rate at which heat reenters the compartment.

Modern refrigerators may use variable-speed compressors and more sophisticated electronic controls rather than simply switching between fully on and fully off, but the goal remains the same: maintain the desired temperature while minimizing unnecessary energy use.

How Does Refrigeration Help Preserve Food?

Refrigeration does more than make food more pleasant to eat. Lower temperatures slow many of the biological and chemical processes that cause food to spoil.

Bacteria and other microorganisms generally reproduce more slowly at refrigerator temperatures than they do at warmer room temperatures. Cooling also slows many chemical and enzymatic reactions that affect flavor, texture, color and freshness.

Refrigeration does not sterilize food. Some microorganisms can survive and even grow slowly at refrigerator temperatures.

That is why refrigerated foods still have storage limits and why safe food-handling practices remain important.

Why Is a Freezer Colder Than a Refrigerator?

A freezer uses the same general refrigeration principles but maintains a much lower interior temperature.

The system removes enough heat to keep food below the freezing point of water. In many refrigerator-freezer designs, the two compartments share parts of the same refrigeration system, with airflow and electronic controls regulating their different temperatures.

Freezing slows microbial growth and many chemical reactions even more dramatically than ordinary refrigeration, although it does not necessarily kill all microorganisms.

Is Refrigeration the Same as Air Conditioning?

The underlying technology is very similar.

Most household air conditioners also use a compressor, condenser, expansion device, evaporator and refrigerant to move heat.

The major difference is where the heat is being removed from and where it is released.

A refrigerator removes heat from a small insulated compartment and releases it into the room. An air conditioner removes heat from a building's interior and releases it outdoors.

A heat pump uses the same basic refrigeration cycle and can be designed to move heat in either direction, allowing it to heat as well as cool a building.

The Bottom Line

A refrigerator keeps food cold by moving heat from inside the appliance into the surrounding room.

A refrigerant continuously circulates through a sealed system. The compressor raises its pressure and temperature, the condenser allows it to release heat into the room, the expansion device lowers its pressure, and the evaporator allows the cold refrigerant to absorb heat from inside the refrigerator.

The refrigerant then returns to the compressor and repeats the cycle.

Insulation slows heat from getting back inside, while thermostats, sensors, fans and electronic controls help maintain a stable temperature.

By continuously removing heat, refrigeration keeps food at temperatures that slow microbial growth and many of the chemical reactions responsible for spoilage. What feels like the refrigerator creating cold is, physically, a carefully controlled process of moving heat somewhere else.
