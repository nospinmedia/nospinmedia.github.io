Why Is the Ocean Salty?

July 9, 20264 min read

Why Is the Ocean Salty?

The world's oceans contain vast amounts of dissolved salt, giving seawater its distinctive taste. Although it may seem like the salt has always been there, it has actually accumulated over hundreds of millions of years through natural geological processes.

The simple explanation is that rain slowly dissolves tiny amounts of minerals from rocks on land. Rivers carry many of those dissolved minerals to the oceans, where most of the water eventually evaporates back into the atmosphere while the dissolved salts largely remain behind.

The result is a slow but continuous buildup of dissolved minerals that makes seawater salty.

Where Does the Salt Come From?

Most of the salt in the ocean originally comes from rocks on Earth's continents.

Rainwater is naturally slightly acidic because it absorbs small amounts of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. As rain falls onto the land, it slowly weathers rocks and dissolves tiny amounts of minerals.

These dissolved minerals are carried by streams and rivers into lakes, seas, and eventually the oceans.

The two most abundant ions in seawater are:

  • Sodium
  • Chloride

Together, they form sodium chloride, the same chemical compound commonly known as table salt.

Other dissolved minerals in seawater include magnesium, calcium, potassium, sulfate, and bicarbonate.

Do Volcanoes Contribute Salt?

Yes.

Volcanic eruptions and underwater hydrothermal vents also contribute minerals and dissolved chemicals to the oceans.

At the same time, hydrothermal vents remove some dissolved minerals through chemical reactions with newly formed ocean crust.

These natural processes help regulate the overall chemical composition of seawater over long periods of time.

Why Aren't Rivers Salty?

Rivers do contain dissolved salts and minerals, but the concentration is much lower than in the ocean.

Several factors keep rivers relatively fresh:

  • Water is constantly flowing rather than accumulating.
  • Minerals are carried onward toward the ocean.
  • Fresh rainfall continually dilutes dissolved minerals.
  • Rivers do not have enough time for salts to build up to ocean-like concentrations.

As a result, most rivers contain only a tiny fraction of the dissolved salts found in seawater.

Why Doesn't the Ocean Keep Getting Saltier?

It might seem that the oceans should become saltier every year as rivers continue delivering dissolved minerals.

In reality, the ocean exists in a long-term balance.

While rivers and volcanic activity add dissolved minerals, other natural processes remove them.

Salt and other dissolved elements become incorporated into:

  • Marine organisms that build shells and skeletons.
  • Ocean sediments.
  • Chemical reactions within the seafloor.
  • Newly forming rocks beneath the ocean floor.

Because minerals are continually added and removed, the average salinity of the oceans has remained relatively stable over millions of years.

How Salty Is the Ocean?

The average ocean contains about 35 grams of dissolved salts per liter of seawater, often expressed as 35 parts per thousand (35‰) or about 3.5% salt by weight.

However, salinity is not the same everywhere.

Why Does Ocean Salinity Vary?

Different parts of the ocean have different salt concentrations.

Salinity is influenced by several factors, including:

  • Evaporation.
  • Rainfall.
  • River inflow.
  • Melting sea ice.
  • Ocean currents.

For example, warm, dry regions where evaporation exceeds rainfall often have saltier water. Areas near large river mouths or regions with heavy rainfall tend to have lower salinity because freshwater dilutes the seawater.

Why Is the Ocean Important?

The salt dissolved in seawater does much more than affect its taste.

Ocean salinity influences:

  • Ocean currents.
  • Water density.
  • Marine ecosystems.
  • Global climate.
  • The movement of heat around the planet.

Small changes in salinity can affect how seawater sinks, rises, and circulates through the world's oceans, helping regulate Earth's climate.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that the ocean is salty because it reflects salt from the land or because marine animals produce the salt. In reality, most dissolved salts originate from the slow weathering of rocks and are transported to the ocean by rivers.

Another misconception is that rivers contain no salt. Rivers do carry dissolved minerals, but their concentrations are much lower because the water is constantly moving and is regularly replenished by rainfall.

It is also sometimes assumed that the ocean becomes noticeably saltier every year. Although rivers continuously deliver dissolved minerals, natural geological and biological processes remove many of them, keeping average ocean salinity relatively stable over long periods.

Conclusion

The ocean is salty because rain slowly dissolves minerals from rocks on land, rivers carry those dissolved minerals to the sea, and water continually evaporates while most of the dissolved salts remain behind. Over hundreds of millions of years, this process has produced the average salinity found in today's oceans.

Although rivers, volcanoes, and hydrothermal vents continue to add minerals to seawater, other natural processes remove them at nearly the same rate. This long-term balance has allowed the oceans to maintain a relatively stable salt concentration while playing a vital role in Earth's climate and marine ecosystems.
