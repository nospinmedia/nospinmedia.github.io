What Is Inflation?

July 25, 20264 min read

What Is Inflation?

Inflation is the general increase in the prices of goods and services over time. As prices rise, the purchasing power of money declines, meaning the same amount of money buys fewer goods and services than it did before.

A small amount of inflation is generally considered a normal part of a growing economy. However, inflation that rises too quickly can increase the cost of living and create economic challenges for households and businesses.

What Causes Inflation?

Inflation can occur for many reasons, often with several factors happening at the same time.

Common causes include:

  • Increased consumer demand for goods and services.
  • Rising costs for labor, energy, or raw materials.
  • Supply chain disruptions that reduce product availability.
  • Higher transportation or shipping costs.
  • Strong economic growth that increases spending.
  • Government policies or global events that affect supply and demand.

Economists often debate which factors have the greatest influence during a particular period.

How Is Inflation Measured?

In the United States, inflation is commonly measured using the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which is produced by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). The CPI tracks changes in the prices consumers pay for a broad basket of goods and services.

Another widely used measure is the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) Price Index, which is compiled by the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). The PCE Price Index is closely monitored by the Federal Reserve when making monetary policy decisions.

Because these indexes use different methods and categories, they may report slightly different inflation rates.

What Is the Difference Between Headline and Core Inflation?

Headline inflation includes price changes across all categories of goods and services.

Core inflation excludes food and energy prices because they can fluctuate significantly over short periods. Many economists monitor both measures to better understand underlying inflation trends.

Why Does Inflation Matter?

Inflation affects nearly every part of the economy.

Higher inflation may lead to:

  • Increased grocery prices.
  • Higher housing costs.
  • More expensive gasoline and energy.
  • Rising interest rates.
  • Higher borrowing costs for mortgages, auto loans, and credit cards.
  • Increased business operating expenses.

If wages do not increase as quickly as prices, consumers may lose purchasing power.

How Does the Federal Reserve Respond?

The Federal Reserve, often called the Fed, seeks to promote stable prices and maximum employment.

The Federal Reserve has a long-run inflation goal of 2% annually, measured using the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) Price Index. When inflation remains above that goal, the Fed may raise interest rates to slow borrowing and spending. When inflation falls and economic growth weakens, it may lower interest rates to encourage economic activity.

Interest rate decisions are only one of many factors that influence inflation.

Is Inflation Always Bad?

Not necessarily.

Most economists consider low, stable inflation to be a normal feature of a healthy economy.

Problems are more likely to occur when inflation rises rapidly, remains elevated for an extended period, or becomes unpredictable. Conversely, prolonged falling prices—known as deflation—can also create economic challenges by reducing spending and investment.

An extreme form of inflation, known as hyperinflation, occurs when prices rise extraordinarily rapidly over a short period, severely reducing the value of a country's currency.

Why Is Inflation Frequently in the News?

Inflation is regularly discussed in news involving:

  • Consumer prices.
  • Federal Reserve meetings.
  • Interest rate decisions.
  • Employment reports.
  • Housing markets.
  • Grocery prices.
  • Energy prices.
  • Wage growth.
  • Economic forecasts.
  • Cost-of-living changes.

Monthly inflation reports often influence financial markets and shape expectations about future interest rate decisions.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that inflation means every price rises by the same amount. In reality, some prices may increase rapidly, others may rise slowly, and some may even fall.

Another misunderstanding is that inflation is caused by a single event. Most periods of inflation result from multiple economic factors interacting over time.

Finally, inflation is not the same as a temporary increase in the price of one product. Inflation refers to a broad and sustained rise in prices across much of the economy.

Conclusion

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time, reducing the purchasing power of money. It is measured using indexes such as the Consumer Price Index and the Personal Consumption Expenditures Price Index, and it plays a major role in decisions involving interest rates, wages, government policy, and household budgets. Because inflation affects nearly every consumer and business, it remains one of the most closely watched indicators of the U.S. economy.
