What Is a Primary Election?

July 31, 20266 min read

What Is a Primary Election?

A primary election is an election held before the general election to determine which candidate will represent a political party on the general election ballot.

Instead of voters choosing between candidates from different political parties, a primary allows members—or, in some states, any eligible voter—to choose among candidates within the same party.

For example, if five people seek the Republican nomination for governor, Republican primary voters decide which one becomes the party's official nominee. The Democratic Party and other political parties typically hold their own primaries as well.

Primary elections are a key part of the American electoral system because they narrow a field of candidates into a single nominee for each political party.

Why Are Primary Elections Held?

Political parties often have multiple people who want to run for the same office. A primary election gives voters a chance to decide which candidate will represent the party in the general election.

Without primaries, party leaders would have far greater control over selecting candidates. Modern primaries give voters a much larger role in that process, although the exact rules differ from state to state.

Primary elections are used for many offices, including:

  • President
  • U.S. Senate
  • U.S. House of Representatives
  • Governor
  • State legislature
  • Many local offices

Some local elections are nonpartisan and do not use party primaries.

How Does a Primary Election Work?

Candidates from the same political party compete against one another.

Voters cast ballots for the candidate they prefer.

After the votes are counted, the winning candidate becomes that party's nominee for the general election. In most races, only one candidate from each major party advances, although some states use different systems.

The general election is then held later, where nominees from different parties compete against one another.

Types of Primary Elections

The United States does not have a single nationwide primary system. Each state establishes its own rules, often in cooperation with the political parties.

Because election laws are established by the states, the rules governing primary elections—including voter eligibility, registration deadlines, ballot access, and party participation—can vary significantly across the country.

Closed Primary

Only voters who are registered with a political party may vote in that party's primary.

For example, a voter registered as a Democrat may vote only in the Democratic primary, while a registered Republican votes only in the Republican primary.

Supporters say closed primaries allow party members to choose their own candidates without outside influence.

Open Primary

In an open primary, eligible voters do not have to be registered with a political party before voting. Instead, they choose which party's primary they want to participate in when they vote.

Although voters may choose either party's primary, they generally may vote in only one party's primary during that election.

Semi-Closed and Semi-Open Primaries

Some states use systems that fall between open and closed primaries.

Depending on the state's rules:

  • Independent or unaffiliated voters may choose either party's primary.
  • Registered party members may only vote in their own party's primary.
  • Voters may be allowed to change party affiliation under certain deadlines.

The rules vary considerably from state to state.

Top-Two and Top-Four Primary Systems

A small number of states use systems that do not separate candidates by political party during the primary.

In a top-two primary, all candidates appear on the same ballot regardless of party affiliation. The two candidates receiving the most votes advance to the general election, even if they belong to the same political party.

California and Washington use top-two primary systems.

Some states use a top-four system, where the four highest vote-getters advance to the general election. Alaska uses a top-four primary, followed by a ranked-choice voting general election. These systems are intended to give voters more choices and encourage broader competition, but they differ significantly from traditional party primaries.

Presidential Primaries

Presidential primaries work somewhat differently than primaries for most other offices.

Rather than directly selecting the party's presidential nominee, voters usually help determine how delegates will be awarded to candidates. Delegates then attend the party's national convention, where the presidential nominee is formally chosen according to the party's rules.

Each political party has its own delegate allocation rules, which can differ by state.

Primary Elections vs. Caucuses

Not every state uses a primary election.

Some political parties use caucuses instead.

A primary is a traditional election where voters cast secret ballots.

A caucus is a meeting organized by a political party where participants gather at a specific place and time to discuss candidates and choose delegates or nominees through procedures established by the party. Depending on the party's rules, participants may organize into preference groups, discuss candidates, or elect delegates.

Because caucuses require voters to be physically present for a scheduled meeting and often take significantly longer than casting a ballot, they have historically attracted lower turnout than primary elections.

Over time, many states have moved from caucuses to primaries because primaries are generally easier for more people to participate in.

Primary Election vs. General Election

These elections serve different purposes.

A primary election determines who will represent each political party.

A general election determines who will actually hold public office.

For example:

  1. Multiple Republican candidates compete in the Republican primary.
  2. Multiple Democratic candidates compete in the Democratic primary.
  3. The winners advance to the general election.
  4. Voters then choose among the nominees from each party, along with any qualified independent or third-party candidates.

Common Misunderstandings

Everyone Can Vote in Every Primary

Not necessarily.

Eligibility depends on the laws of the state and, in some cases, the rules of the political party. Some states limit participation based on party registration, while others allow broader participation.

Winning the Primary Means Winning the Election

No.

Winning a primary only makes a candidate that party's nominee. The candidate must still compete in the general election.

Primaries Are Only for Presidential Elections

No.

Primary elections are held for many federal, state, and local offices whenever political parties need to choose nominees.

Why Primary Elections Matter

Primary elections often determine which candidates voters will see on the general election ballot. In areas where one political party consistently wins, the primary may be the most competitive stage of the election because the eventual nominee is often strongly positioned for the general election.

Because of this, primary elections can have a significant influence on the policies, priorities, and leadership of political parties.

Conclusion

Primary elections are the process political parties use to choose their candidates for public office before the general election. While the overall purpose is the same across the United States, the rules vary by state. Understanding whether a state uses an open, closed, or another type of primary helps voters know who is eligible to participate and how candidates reach the general election ballot.
