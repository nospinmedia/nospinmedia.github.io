What Is an Indictment?

August 21, 202610 min read

What Is an Indictment?

An indictment is a formal written accusation charging a person or organization with one or more crimes. In the federal system, an indictment is returned by a grand jury after it determines that there is sufficient basis for criminal charges to proceed.

An indictment is not a finding of guilt. It begins or advances a criminal prosecution, but the government still must prove the charges through the criminal justice process. If the case goes to trial, the prosecution generally must prove the defendant's guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

This distinction is important because news reports frequently describe someone as having been indicted. That means the person has been formally charged through the grand-jury process. It does not mean the person has been convicted.

Who Issues an Indictment?

A grand jury returns an indictment.

Federal prosecutors investigate cases and present evidence to a grand jury. The grand jurors then decide whether the proposed charges should be approved.

Under Rule 6 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, a federal grand jury may return an indictment only if at least 12 grand jurors agree.

Prosecutors play a major role in presenting the case, but the formal indictment is the grand jury's charging action.

What Does a Grand Jury Decide?

The grand jury does not decide whether the accused is guilty.

Its function is to determine whether there is sufficient basis for the proposed criminal charges to proceed.

This is a much different question from the one asked at a criminal trial. A trial jury hears the prosecution and defense cases and determines whether guilt has been proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

Grand-jury proceedings occur earlier and generally do not include the same adversarial procedures as a trial.

What Does an Indictment Contain?

Under Rule 7 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, a federal indictment must contain a plain, concise, and definite written statement of the essential facts constituting the offense charged.

An indictment generally identifies the defendant, describes the alleged criminal conduct, identifies the offenses being charged, and cites the laws allegedly violated.

More complicated indictments may also contain background allegations explaining people, organizations, events, or transactions relevant to the charges.

The amount of detail varies considerably from case to case.

What Is a Count in an Indictment?

A count is an individual criminal charge within an indictment.

If a headline says someone was "indicted on 10 counts," it means the indictment contains 10 separate charged offenses.

That does not necessarily mean prosecutors are alleging 10 completely unrelated criminal events. Multiple counts can arise from related conduct, repeated alleged acts, different alleged offenses arising from the same series of events, or crimes involving multiple victims or transactions.

Each count identifies a particular offense the government intends to prosecute.

Does an Indictment Mean Someone Is Guilty?

No.

An indictment is an accusation, not a conviction.

The defendant remains presumed innocent unless and until guilt is established through the criminal justice process.

An indicted defendant may later be acquitted at trial. Charges may also be dismissed, withdrawn, resolved through a plea agreement, or otherwise end without a conviction on every count originally charged.

The fact that a grand jury approved charges should therefore never be reported as though a trial jury had determined guilt.

Is an Indictment the Same as Being Charged?

An indictment is one method of formally charging someone with a crime, but it is not the only charging mechanism.

Depending on the jurisdiction and type of offense, criminal cases may also begin through documents such as a complaint or an information.

The procedures are particularly important in federal felony cases because the Fifth Amendment generally requires a grand-jury indictment for serious federal offenses covered by its Grand Jury Clause unless the defendant validly waives indictment where waiver is permitted.

State procedures vary because the federal constitutional grand-jury requirement has not been applied to the states.

What Is the Difference Between an Indictment and a Criminal Complaint?

A criminal complaint is a written statement alleging that a person committed an offense and establishing a basis for initial judicial action in a criminal case.

Federal authorities can sometimes arrest and initially charge a person through a complaint before prosecutors obtain a grand-jury indictment.

The complaint and indictment therefore may appear at different stages of the same federal case.

For example, someone might be arrested on a criminal complaint, make an initial appearance before a judge, and later be indicted by a grand jury.

What Is the Difference Between an Indictment and an Information?

An information is another formal charging document, but it is filed by a prosecutor rather than returned by a grand jury.

Under federal Rule 7, an offense punishable by more than one year in prison generally must be prosecuted by indictment, with specified exceptions. A defendant may waive indictment in open court after being advised of the charge and the right to indictment, allowing prosecution by information instead.

Informations are commonly encountered in federal cases in which defendants waive indictment, including some cases resolved through negotiated guilty pleas.

State rules governing informations and indictments vary.

What Is the Difference Between an Indictment and an Arraignment?

An indictment is the charging document. An arraignment is a court proceeding.

At a federal arraignment, the defendant is formally informed of the charges and asked to enter a plea, such as guilty or not guilty.

The indictment therefore identifies what the government is accusing the defendant of doing, while the arraignment is one of the court proceedings that follows the filing or unsealing of those charges.

Does an Indictment Automatically Mean Someone Will Be Arrested?

Not necessarily.

After an indictment is returned, a federal court may issue an arrest warrant or, in appropriate circumstances, a summons directing the defendant to appear in court.

A defendant may also already be in custody because the person was arrested earlier in the investigation on a criminal complaint or another charge.

Being indicted and being arrested are therefore separate events, even though they often occur close together.

What Is a Sealed Indictment?

A sealed indictment is an indictment that is temporarily kept from public disclosure by court order.

Federal rules allow a magistrate judge to direct that an indictment remain secret until the defendant is in custody or has been released pending trial. The clerk must then seal the indictment, and people involved in processing it may not disclose its existence except as necessary to issue or execute a warrant or summons.

Sealing can prevent a defendant from learning about the charges before authorities are able to make an arrest or arrange a court appearance.

Once the reason for secrecy no longer applies, the indictment may be unsealed and become publicly accessible, subject to applicable court rules and orders.

What Is a Superseding Indictment?

A superseding indictment is a later indictment that replaces an earlier indictment in the same prosecution.

Prosecutors may seek a superseding indictment when an investigation produces additional evidence, when charges are added or modified, when another defendant is added, or when other changes to the charging document are necessary.

The fact that prosecutors obtain a superseding indictment does not itself establish that the new allegations are true. Like the original indictment, the superseding indictment contains criminal accusations that must still be resolved through the judicial process.

Can Charges Be Dismissed After an Indictment?

Yes.

An indictment does not guarantee that every charge will reach trial or result in a conviction.

Defendants can challenge indictments and prosecutions through pretrial motions when legally appropriate. Courts may dismiss charges for certain legal defects or violations, and prosecutors can seek dismissal of charges subject to the applicable rules and judicial process.

Charges may also be affected by plea agreements or later prosecutorial decisions.

Can Someone Be Acquitted After Being Indicted?

Yes.

An indictment and an acquittal answer completely different questions at different stages of a case.

The indictment allows criminal charges to proceed. At trial, the prosecution carries the much heavier burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

If the prosecution fails to meet that burden, the defendant may be acquitted even though a grand jury previously approved the indictment.

What Happens If a Grand Jury Refuses to Indict?

If the required number of grand jurors does not agree to the proposed federal indictment, the indictment is not returned.

That is not the same as an acquittal at trial.

Double jeopardy generally does not attach merely because a grand jury considered a proposed charge and declined to indict. Depending on the circumstances and applicable legal limits, prosecutors may continue investigating and may sometimes seek charges later, including after obtaining additional evidence.

Does Every Criminal Case Require an Indictment?

No.

The Fifth Amendment requires grand-jury indictment for certain serious federal criminal prosecutions, subject to its exceptions and permissible waiver procedures.

Federal Rule 7 generally requires an indictment for an offense punishable by death or imprisonment for more than one year, other than criminal contempt, unless indictment is properly waived where permitted.

The rule is different for states.

The Supreme Court has held that the Fifth Amendment's Grand Jury Clause does not constitutionally require states to use grand-jury indictments. States may use grand juries, informations, preliminary hearings, or other charging procedures established by state law.

How to Read an Indictment

Indictments in major federal cases are often released publicly. They can provide useful information, but readers should remember that they present allegations, not proven facts.

A federal indictment may contain:

  • The names of the defendants.
  • Background information about the alleged conduct.
  • Dates or approximate time periods.
  • Descriptions of alleged acts or transactions.
  • Numbered criminal counts.
  • Federal statutes allegedly violated.
  • Allegations involving forfeiture of property when applicable.

Some indictments contain extensive narratives while others are relatively short.

When reading one, the most important distinction is between what prosecutors and the grand jury allege and what has actually been established through evidence tested in court.

Common Misunderstandings

Indicted Means Guilty

No.

An indictment is a formal criminal accusation. It is not a conviction or finding of guilt.

A Judge Decides Whether to Issue the Indictment

Not in the federal grand-jury process.

Prosecutors present the case, but the grand jury votes on whether to return the indictment.

Prosecutors Have Already Proved the Case Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

No.

Beyond a reasonable doubt is the standard required for a criminal conviction at trial, not the standard for returning an indictment.

An Indicted Person Is Automatically Arrested

Not necessarily.

A court may issue a warrant or summons after an indictment, and some defendants were already arrested before the indictment was returned.

Ten Counts Means Ten Completely Separate Incidents

Not necessarily.

Each count is a separate charged offense, but multiple counts can arise from related conduct or a series of alleged acts.

If Charges Are in an Indictment, They Cannot Be Dismissed

False.

Charges can later be dismissed or otherwise resolved without conviction.

Every State Felony Requires a Grand-Jury Indictment

No.

The federal constitutional grand-jury requirement has not been applied to the states. State charging procedures vary.

Why Indictments Matter

An indictment marks an important stage in a criminal case because a grand jury has authorized prosecutors to proceed with formal charges.

But it is still near the beginning of the adjudicative process rather than the end.

After indictment, defendants have opportunities to challenge the prosecution, enter pleas, examine evidence through the discovery process, file appropriate motions, negotiate possible resolutions, and proceed to trial when the case is not otherwise resolved.

That is why the distinction between indictment and conviction is so important when reading news coverage. An indictment tells readers what crimes the government formally alleges. It does not tell them whether those allegations ultimately will be proved.

Conclusion

An indictment is a formal criminal accusation returned by a grand jury. In the federal system, at least 12 grand jurors must agree before an indictment can be returned.

The indictment describes the criminal charges the government intends to prosecute, with each separate charge generally appearing as a count. It may lead to an arrest warrant, summons, arraignment, pretrial proceedings, and potentially a trial.

Most importantly, an indictment is not a conviction. The defendant remains presumed innocent, and the government must still prove the charges through the criminal justice process before criminal guilt can be established.
