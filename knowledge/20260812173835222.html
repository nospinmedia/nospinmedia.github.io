What Happens If No Presidential Candidate Wins 270 Electoral Votes?

August 25, 202614 min read

What Happens If No Presidential Candidate Wins 270 Electoral Votes?

A presidential candidate normally wins the presidency by receiving a majority of the electoral votes in the Electoral College. With the current 538 electoral votes, that majority is 270.

But the Constitution also provides a procedure for an election in which no presidential candidate receives the required majority.

In that situation, the election moves to Congress under the 12th Amendment. The House of Representatives chooses the president, while the Senate chooses the vice president if no vice-presidential candidate has received an Electoral College majority.

This process is known as a contingent election.

It works very differently from an ordinary vote in Congress. Most importantly, when the House chooses a president, each state gets one vote regardless of population or the number of representatives it has.

Is 270 Always the Number Required to Win?

Not necessarily.

The Constitution does not specify the number 270. The 12th Amendment requires a presidential candidate to receive a majority of the whole number of electors appointed.

There are currently 538 electoral votes: 535 corresponding to the total number of senators and representatives from the states, plus three allocated to the District of Columbia under the 23rd Amendment.

With all 538 electors appointed, a majority is 270.

The number 270 therefore results from the current size of the Electoral College rather than being permanently written into the Constitution. If the number of electors appointed were different, the mathematical majority required by the Constitution could also be different.

How Could Nobody Win an Electoral College Majority?

Several circumstances could produce an election without an electoral-vote majority.

One straightforward possibility is a strong third-party or independent candidate winning enough electoral votes to prevent either major-party candidate from reaching a majority.

An electoral-vote tie could also produce the same result. With 538 electoral votes, for example, a 269-269 tie between two presidential candidates would leave neither candidate with a majority.

An election involving electoral votes divided among three or more candidates could produce many other combinations in which nobody reaches the required majority.

The constitutional question is not simply which candidate has the largest number of electoral votes. A candidate must have a majority of the electors appointed to win through the Electoral College.

What Happens If Nobody Has a Majority?

The 12th Amendment sends the presidential decision to the House of Representatives.

The House does not conduct another national election. Instead, it chooses the president from among the three people who received the highest numbers of electoral votes for president.

The House must begin choosing a president when no candidate has obtained the required Electoral College majority.

But the voting system is very different from normal House business.

Does Every House Member Get One Vote?

No.

When the House conducts a contingent presidential election, the vote is taken by state delegation.

Each state has exactly one vote.

That means a state with dozens of representatives has the same voting power in the final presidential selection as a state with only one representative.

For example:

  • California gets one state vote.
  • Texas gets one state vote.
  • New Hampshire gets one state vote.
  • Wyoming gets one state vote.

Members of each state's House delegation determine how their state's single vote is cast under the applicable House procedures.

This means the overall number of Democrats, Republicans, or other members in the House does not by itself determine the result. What matters is how the representatives are distributed among the 50 state delegations and how those delegations vote.

How Many States Are Needed to Elect the President?

The 12th Amendment requires a majority of all the states to elect the president.

With 50 states, a candidate currently needs the votes of 26 state delegations.

The Constitution also establishes a special quorum requirement. A member or members from two-thirds of the states must be present for the House to conduct the contingent presidential election.

The winning threshold is not merely a majority of the states that happen to cast votes. The Constitution requires a majority of all the states.

Does Washington, D.C. Get a Vote?

No.

The District of Columbia receives three electoral votes under the 23rd Amendment and participates in the Electoral College.

But the 12th Amendment specifies that the House vote for president is taken by states. The District of Columbia is not a state and does not have a state delegation in the House.

As a result, D.C. does not receive a vote in a House contingent presidential election.

Can the House Choose Anyone It Wants?

No.

The House is constitutionally limited to choosing among the three presidential candidates with the highest electoral-vote totals.

Congress cannot simply nominate another politician after the election and make that person president through the contingent-election process.

This can become important in a multi-candidate election. A candidate finishing fourth in electoral votes would not be eligible for selection by the House even if that candidate had substantial support in Congress.

Does the Popular Vote Determine How the House Votes?

The Constitution does not require the House to choose the national popular-vote winner.

It also does not give the national popular-vote winner additional voting power in a contingent election.

The constitutional mechanism is based on state delegations, with each state receiving one vote.

A state's representatives may face political pressure to consider how voters in their state or the country voted, but the 12th Amendment itself establishes the state-delegation voting system rather than a second popular election.

What Happens to the Vice-Presidential Election?

The vice president has a separate Electoral College vote.

If no vice-presidential candidate receives a majority of the electors appointed, the Senate chooses the vice president under the 12th Amendment.

The Senate chooses between the two people who received the highest numbers of electoral votes for vice president.

Unlike the House's presidential vote, the Senate does not vote by state delegation.

Each senator gets one vote.

How Many Senate Votes Are Needed to Choose the Vice President?

The 12th Amendment requires a quorum of two-thirds of the whole number of senators.

To elect the vice president, a candidate must receive a majority of the whole number of senators.

With a Senate consisting of 100 seats, that ordinarily means 51 votes are required.

This is different from simply requiring a majority of senators who happen to be present and voting.

The Constitution specifically requires a majority of the whole number.

Can the Vice President Break a 50-50 Senate Tie in a Contingent Election?

The Constitution does not clearly provide for the sitting vice president to resolve a 50-50 contingent-election vote in the same manner as an ordinary Senate tie.

The 12th Amendment specifically requires a vice-presidential candidate to receive a majority of the whole number of senators. With 100 senators, neither candidate receiving only 50 senatorial votes has obtained that required majority.

For that reason, the ordinary rule allowing the vice president to cast a tie-breaking vote in Senate business should not simply be assumed to turn 50 votes into the constitutionally required majority in a contingent vice-presidential election.

Could the President and Vice President Come From Different Parties?

Yes.

The House chooses the president and the Senate chooses the vice president through separate constitutional processes.

Nothing in the contingent-election mechanism requires the two chambers to select candidates from the same political party or ticket.

A House controlled by state delegations favoring one party could choose that party's presidential candidate while a Senate majority favoring another party could select the other party's vice-presidential candidate.

The result could therefore be a president and vice president from different parties.

Which Congress Makes the Decision?

Under the modern constitutional calendar, the new Congress elected in the same general election normally would be in office when a contingent election is conducted.

The 20th Amendment provides that terms of newly elected senators and representatives begin at noon on January 3.

Federal law provides for Congress to meet to count the electoral votes on January 6, unless the date is changed as permitted by law.

That means the incoming Congress normally takes office before the electoral votes are counted and before the need for a contingent election is formally established through that process.

This can have major political consequences. The congressional elections held alongside the presidential election can change the composition of the House state delegations or Senate that would decide a contingent election.

Why Do State Delegations Matter More Than the Overall House Majority?

Because each state receives one presidential vote.

Imagine a simplified example in which one party has a very large majority of representatives from several populous states while another party controls smaller majorities in a greater number of states.

The first party could have more House members nationwide but still control fewer state delegation votes.

A state with 40 representatives still contributes only one presidential vote, while a state with a single representative also contributes one.

Therefore, determining who would have an advantage in a contingent presidential election requires examining each state's House delegation, not simply the overall party totals in the House.

What If a State Delegation Is Tied?

A divided state delegation may be unable to cast its state vote for a candidate under the procedures governing the House election.

That does not lower the constitutional threshold required to elect a president. A candidate still needs a majority of all states.

This creates the possibility of prolonged deadlock if neither candidate can assemble the required state majority.

The House can continue voting in an effort to choose a president.

What If the House Still Has Not Chosen a President by January 20?

The 20th Amendment addresses this possibility.

If a president has not been chosen by the beginning of the presidential term at noon on January 20, but a vice president has qualified, the vice president-elect acts as president until a president qualifies.

This means the Senate's contingent-election decision could become extremely important if the House remains deadlocked.

The person chosen as vice president could temporarily exercise presidential powers while the House continues trying to select a president.

What If Neither a President Nor Vice President Has Qualified?

The 20th Amendment authorizes Congress to provide by law for a situation in which neither a president-elect nor vice president-elect has qualified by the beginning of the presidential term.

Congress has done so through the federal Presidential Succession Act.

Under the current succession statute, the Speaker of the House is first in the statutory line after the vice president, followed by the president pro tempore of the Senate and then eligible Cabinet officers in the order established by law, subject to the statute's eligibility and resignation requirements.

A person serving under these provisions would act as president until a president or vice president qualifies. This would not turn the acting official into the elected president for the four-year term.

Has the House Ever Chosen a President?

Yes.

The House has played the decisive role in two presidential elections, although the first occurred under the original constitutional system rather than today's 12th Amendment procedure.

The Election of 1800

Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr each received 73 electoral votes under the original Electoral College system.

At the time, electors did not cast separate ballots for president and vice president. The tie therefore sent the election to the House.

After 36 ballots, the House selected Jefferson as president in February 1801.

The crisis exposed a serious weakness in the original Electoral College design and contributed to adoption of the 12th Amendment in 1804, which created separate electoral votes for president and vice president and established the contingent-election procedure used today.

The Election of 1824

The 1824 election provides the principal historical example under the 12th Amendment.

Andrew Jackson received more electoral votes than any other presidential candidate, but he did not receive a majority.

The election therefore went to the House, which was constitutionally limited to choosing among the three candidates with the highest electoral-vote totals.

The House selected John Quincy Adams as president even though Jackson had received more electoral votes and more popular votes.

The episode demonstrates one of the most important features of a contingent election: receiving the most electoral votes is not sufficient if the candidate fails to obtain the constitutionally required majority.

Has the Senate Ever Chosen a Vice President?

Yes.

The Senate used its contingent-election authority following the 1836 election.

Presidential candidate Martin Van Buren received an Electoral College majority and became president, but his running mate, Richard Mentor Johnson, fell short of the required electoral-vote majority for vice president.

The decision therefore went to the Senate, which elected Johnson vice president in 1837.

It remains the only time the Senate has selected a vice president through the 12th Amendment's contingent-election procedure.

Common Misunderstandings

Whoever Gets the Most Electoral Votes Automatically Wins

No.

A presidential candidate must receive a majority of the electors appointed, not merely more electoral votes than every opponent.

If nobody has that majority, the 12th Amendment's contingent-election process applies.

The National Popular-Vote Winner Automatically Wins a Contingent Election

No.

The House chooses the president by state delegation. The Constitution does not award the presidency to the candidate who received the most votes nationwide when no Electoral College majority exists.

All 435 House Members Get One Presidential Vote Each

No.

The House votes by state. Each of the 50 states gets one vote regardless of population or delegation size.

The House Can Choose Anyone as President

No.

The House must choose from among the three people with the highest presidential electoral-vote totals.

The House Chooses Both the President and Vice President

No.

The House chooses the president. If necessary, the Senate separately chooses the vice president from the top two vice-presidential electoral-vote recipients.

Washington, D.C. Gets a House Vote Because It Has Electoral Votes

No.

D.C. has three Electoral College votes but is not a state. It therefore does not receive a state-delegation vote when the House chooses a president.

A 269-269 Tie Means Congress Simply Picks the Winner by Majority Vote

Not in the ordinary sense.

A 269-269 presidential tie would send the decision to the House, where each state gets one vote and a candidate currently needs 26 states to win.

The Party With the Most House Members Controls the Presidential Decision

Not necessarily.

What matters is the distribution of support among the 50 state delegations, not simply which party holds the most House seats nationwide.

The Number 270 Is Written Into the Constitution

No.

The Constitution requires a majority of the whole number of electors appointed. With the current 538 electoral votes, that majority is 270.

Why the Contingent-Election System Matters

A contingent election dramatically changes the political mathematics of a presidential election.

During the normal Electoral College process, states have different numbers of electoral votes largely based on population. California currently has 54 electoral votes while several states have only three.

In a House contingent election, those differences disappear. Every state gets exactly one vote.

The Senate process is different again: each senator votes individually to choose the vice president.

It is therefore possible for an election with no Electoral College majority to produce outcomes that would be impossible to predict simply by looking at the national popular vote, total House membership, or which presidential candidate received the largest plurality of electoral votes.

Conclusion

If no presidential candidate receives a majority of the appointed electors, the Constitution does not automatically award the presidency to whoever has the most electoral votes.

Under the 12th Amendment, the House of Representatives chooses the president from among the three presidential candidates with the highest electoral-vote totals. The House votes by state delegation, with each state receiving one vote, and a majority of all states is required to elect the president.

If no vice-presidential candidate receives an Electoral College majority, the Senate chooses between the top two candidates for vice president, with each senator casting one vote and a majority of the whole Senate required to elect.

If the House remains deadlocked when the presidential term begins on January 20, the 20th Amendment provides for a qualified vice president-elect to act as president until a president qualifies. If neither office has been filled, federal succession law provides a mechanism for an acting president until the constitutional deadlock is resolved.

With today's 538 electoral votes, 270 is the familiar number needed to win. But the underlying constitutional rule is more important: a presidential candidate must win a majority of the electors appointed. If nobody does, Congress—and ultimately the state delegations in the House—can determine who becomes president.
