Why Do Airplanes Leave White Trails in the Sky?

September 10, 20266 min read

The long white lines that sometimes appear behind airplanes are called contrails, short for condensation trails. They are essentially narrow, human-made clouds composed mostly of tiny ice crystals.

Contrails form because jet engines release water vapor as part of the combustion process. At the high altitudes where commercial aircraft typically cruise, the surrounding air can be extremely cold. Under the right conditions, water vapor from the engine exhaust condenses and quickly freezes, creating the visible white trail behind the aircraft.

Where Does the Water Come From?

Jet fuel is made primarily of hydrocarbons, which contain hydrogen and carbon. When the fuel burns in an engine, it reacts with oxygen from the air. Among the products of that combustion are carbon dioxide and water vapor.

Normally, water vapor is invisible. But when the hot, moist exhaust from a jet engine mixes with the very cold air at cruising altitude, conditions can become favorable for condensation.

Tiny droplets form around microscopic particles in the exhaust or particles already present in the atmosphere. Because temperatures at these altitudes are usually far below freezing, the droplets quickly freeze into tiny ice crystals.

Millions of those ice crystals together form the white line visible from the ground.

In that sense, a contrail is much more like a high-altitude cirrus cloud than a trail of smoke.

Why Don't All Airplanes Leave Contrails?

An airplane can fly across a perfectly clear sky without leaving a visible trail, while another aircraft nearby may produce one that stretches for many miles.

The difference is mainly caused by the temperature and humidity of the air at the airplane's altitude.

Contrails are most likely to form when the upper atmosphere is both cold enough and humid enough. If the air is too warm or too dry, the ice crystals either will not form or will disappear almost immediately.

Conditions on the ground do not necessarily tell you what conditions are like several miles above you. It can be dry at ground level while the atmosphere at cruising altitude contains enough moisture for persistent contrails.

Why Do Some Contrails Disappear Quickly?

Some contrails last only a few seconds or minutes.

This usually happens when the surrounding air is relatively dry. The newly formed ice crystals quickly change back into invisible water vapor through a process called sublimation, in which ice changes directly into water vapor without first becoming liquid water.

From the ground, it can look as though the white trail simply dissolves behind the airplane.

Why Do Some Contrails Last for Hours?

If the airplane passes through very humid air, the situation can be quite different.

Instead of disappearing, the ice crystals can remain and even grow by collecting additional water from the surrounding atmosphere. The contrail may then persist long after the airplane that created it has disappeared from view.

Upper-level winds can stretch and spread the trail. Over time, a thin white line may widen into a large, wispy cloud that resembles naturally occurring cirrus clouds.

Persistent contrails can sometimes remain visible for hours, and under unusually favorable atmospheric conditions they can last much longer.

Why Do Contrails Sometimes Start and Stop Suddenly?

A contrail can appear to begin in the middle of an otherwise clear sky, disappear, and then reappear farther along the airplane's path.

The airplane's engines did not turn on and off.

Instead, the aircraft is moving through different regions of the atmosphere. One area may contain enough moisture for ice crystals to form and persist, while a neighboring area may be too dry.

Those differences are often invisible from the ground, so the resulting contrail can look as though someone drew a broken white line across the sky.

Why Do Contrails Spread Across the Sky?

The atmosphere at cruising altitude is constantly moving. Winds at different heights can travel at different speeds or in somewhat different directions, a condition known as wind shear.

Once a persistent contrail forms, these winds can stretch, twist and widen it. A narrow trail may gradually lose its sharp edges and become a broad patch of thin cloud.

Eventually, an older contrail can become difficult to distinguish from natural cirrus clouds.

This also explains why a contrail may no longer appear to be near the aircraft that produced it. The airplane continues traveling while winds move and reshape the ice crystals left behind.

Are Contrails Smoke?

No. Although jet engines do produce combustion emissions, the visible white portion of a normal contrail consists primarily of ice crystals.

The process is somewhat similar to seeing your breath on a cold winter day. Your breath contains invisible water vapor. When warm, moist air from your lungs enters sufficiently cold air, tiny droplets can form and become visible.

A jet contrail occurs under much colder conditions and forms mainly from frozen water rather than liquid droplets.

Are Contrails Clouds?

Yes. Once formed, a contrail is a type of ice cloud.

Natural cirrus clouds and persistent contrails are both made largely of ice crystals. Their origins are different: natural cirrus clouds develop through atmospheric processes, while contrails begin because an aircraft introduces additional water vapor and particles into cold air.

After a persistent contrail spreads and ages, however, it can become physically similar to surrounding cirrus clouds.

Do Contrails Affect Climate?

Persistent contrails can increase the amount of high, thin cloud cover in the atmosphere, so scientists study their effect on Earth's climate.

Like natural cirrus clouds, contrails can influence the movement of energy through the atmosphere. They can reflect some incoming sunlight back toward space, which has a cooling effect, while also reducing the amount of infrared heat escaping from Earth, which has a warming effect.

Research indicates that persistent contrails and the cirrus clouds that develop from them can contribute to aviation's effect on climate. Scientists continue studying how strongly individual contrails affect temperature and how changes in aircraft operations, engines and fuels might reduce persistent contrail formation.

The Bottom Line

The white trails behind high-flying airplanes are usually contrails: narrow clouds of ice crystals created when hot, moist jet exhaust mixes with extremely cold air.

Whether a contrail forms, disappears quickly or remains for hours depends largely on the temperature and humidity of the surrounding atmosphere. In dry air, the ice crystals vanish quickly. In cold, humid air, they can persist, grow and spread until the original white line resembles a natural cirrus cloud.

So when one airplane leaves a long white streak while another seems to leave nothing at all, the difference is usually not the airplane. It is the invisible atmospheric conditions through which the aircraft is flying.
