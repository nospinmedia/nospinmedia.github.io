Why Does the Moon Cause Ocean Tides?

September 11, 20266 min read

Ocean tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the gravitational pull of the Moon, with the Sun also playing an important role.

The Moon does not simply pull the oceans upward wherever it happens to be overhead. Tides result from differences in the Moon's gravitational pull across Earth. Combined with Earth's rotation and the movement of the Moon around Earth, these differences produce the repeating pattern of high and low tides experienced along coastlines around the world.

The Moon's Gravity Pulls Unevenly on Earth

Gravity becomes weaker with distance. Because the Moon is relatively close to Earth, the side of Earth facing the Moon experiences a slightly stronger lunar gravitational pull than Earth's center does. The far side experiences a slightly weaker pull.

That difference is important.

On the side facing the Moon, ocean water is pulled slightly more strongly toward the Moon than Earth's center is. This contributes to a bulge of ocean water on the Moon-facing side of Earth.

On the opposite side, Earth's center is pulled toward the Moon slightly more strongly than the water on the far side. Relative to Earth's center, this produces another ocean bulge on the side facing away from the Moon.

These two bulges are the basic reason there are generally two high-tide regions around Earth at a given time.

Why Is There a High Tide on the Side Away From the Moon?

The second tidal bulge is sometimes incorrectly explained as the Moon somehow pushing water away from itself. It does not.

The key is that the Moon's gravity acts differently across the diameter of Earth.

Imagine three points lined up with the Moon: the near side of Earth, Earth's center and the far side. The Moon pulls most strongly on the near side, somewhat less strongly on the center and least strongly on the far side.

Compared with Earth's center, water on the near side is pulled toward the Moon, while water on the far side is left slightly farther away. Viewed relative to Earth itself, the result is a tidal stretching effect in both directions along the Earth-Moon line.

Why Do Most Places Have Two High Tides Each Day?

As Earth rotates, coastlines move through regions of higher and lower ocean levels associated with the tidal pattern.

In a simplified model, a location passes through two tidal bulges and two lower-water regions during each tidal cycle. This produces two high tides and two low tides.

But the timing is not exactly the same from one day to the next.

The Moon is also orbiting Earth in the same general direction that Earth rotates. Earth therefore has to rotate a little farther for a location to line up with the Moon again. A lunar day — the time between successive passages of the Moon over approximately the same longitude — is about 24 hours and 50 minutes.

As a result, successive high tides in places with a typical twice-daily tidal pattern occur roughly 12 hours and 25 minutes apart, although actual times vary.

Why Doesn't Every Coast Have the Same Tides?

The simple picture of two smooth ocean bulges is useful for understanding the cause of tides, but real oceans are much more complicated.

Continents block the movement of water. Ocean basins have different shapes and depths. Coastlines, bays, islands and underwater terrain can redirect or amplify tidal movement. Earth's rotation also affects how ocean water moves.

Because of these factors, tides behave differently around the world.

Some locations normally experience two similar high tides and two similar low tides each day. This is called a semidiurnal tide.

Other places experience one high tide and one low tide each day, called a diurnal tide. Still others have two high and two low tides of noticeably different heights, known as mixed tides.

Local geography can also make the difference between high and low tide relatively small in one location and extremely large in another.

What Role Does the Sun Play?

The Sun also produces tides because its gravity acts differently across Earth.

The Sun is enormously more massive than the Moon, but it is also much farther away. For tides, the difference in gravitational pull across Earth matters more than the total gravitational attraction alone. Because the Moon is so much closer, its tide-producing effect is stronger than the Sun's.

The solar and lunar tides continually interact.

Sometimes they reinforce each other. At other times they partially oppose each other.

What Are Spring Tides?

During a new moon or full moon, the Sun, Earth and Moon are approximately aligned.

Their tide-producing effects reinforce one another, producing a greater difference between high and low water. Higher high tides and lower low tides are known collectively as spring tides.

Despite the name, spring tides have nothing to do with the season of spring. The term refers to the water appearing to spring or rise more strongly than usual.

Spring tides occur roughly twice each lunar month.

What Are Neap Tides?

Around the Moon's first-quarter and third-quarter phases, the Sun and Moon appear roughly at right angles to each other as viewed from Earth.

Their tidal effects then partially counteract each other.

The result is a smaller difference between high and low tide. These are called neap tides.

The ocean still experiences tides during these periods; their range is simply reduced compared with spring tides.

Does the Moon's Distance Matter?

Yes.

The Moon's orbit around Earth is not a perfect circle, so its distance changes during each orbit. When the Moon is closer to Earth, its tide-producing effect is somewhat stronger. When it is farther away, the effect is weaker.

The Sun-Earth distance also changes during the year, although the Moon remains the dominant influence on ordinary ocean tides.

These astronomical variations combine with local ocean conditions to produce the tides measured at a particular coastline.

Are Waves and Tides the Same Thing?

No.

Ocean waves are usually generated by wind transferring energy to the water. Tides are large-scale changes in sea level driven primarily by the gravitational effects of the Moon and Sun.

Storms can also temporarily raise or lower coastal water levels. A storm surge, for example, can push water toward shore and produce water levels far above the predicted astronomical tide.

That is why actual coastal water levels can sometimes differ significantly from published tide predictions during severe weather.

Can Tides Be Predicted?

Yes. Because the motions of Earth, the Moon and the Sun are highly predictable, the astronomical components of tides can be calculated far in advance.

However, accurate predictions for a particular harbor or coastline also depend on observations of how that location responds to tidal forces. Local geography strongly influences both the timing and height of tides.

Weather introduces additional uncertainty. Atmospheric pressure and winds can change actual water levels even when the astronomical tide itself has been predicted accurately.

The Bottom Line

The Moon causes ocean tides because its gravitational pull is not exactly the same across all of Earth. The side nearest the Moon is pulled more strongly than Earth's center, while the far side is pulled less strongly. This difference stretches the oceans along the Earth-Moon direction and helps create two tidal bulges.

As Earth rotates through this tidal pattern, many coastlines experience roughly two high tides and two low tides during each lunar day. The Sun modifies the pattern, while continents, ocean depths, coastline shapes and weather determine what people actually observe at a particular location.

The familiar rise and fall of the ocean is therefore the visible result of something happening on an enormous scale: the gravitational interaction of Earth, the Moon and the Sun.
