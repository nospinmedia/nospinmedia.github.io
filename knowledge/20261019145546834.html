How Can the United States Produce So Much Oil and Still Import It?

October 28, 202613 min read

The United States produces more crude oil than any other country, yet American refineries continue to import millions of barrels of foreign oil every day.

At the same time, the United States exports large quantities of crude oil and refined petroleum products to other countries.

At first, this may seem contradictory. If America produces so much oil, why does it need to import any? And why would the country export oil while simultaneously buying it from abroad?

The answer involves differences in crude oil quality, the equipment installed at American refineries, transportation costs, and the economics of international trade.

Oil is not a single, interchangeable product. Different types of crude oil have different properties, and refineries are designed to process particular combinations of those types.

Understanding these differences explains why importing and exporting oil at the same time can make economic and operational sense.

How Much Oil Does the United States Produce?

The United States has been the world's largest producer of crude oil in recent years.

American crude oil production has exceeded 13 million barrels per day, supported by production from regions including Texas, New Mexico, North Dakota, Alaska, and the Gulf of Mexico.

A standard barrel of crude oil contains 42 U.S. gallons.

Much of the growth in American production over the past two decades has come from advances in horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing, commonly called fracking.

These technologies made it commercially practical to produce oil from geological formations that were previously difficult or expensive to develop.

The Permian Basin, which extends across parts of Texas and New Mexico, has become one of the country's most important oil-producing regions.

Despite this substantial domestic production, the United States continues to import crude oil because the type, location, and price of available oil matter as much as the total quantity produced.

Is All Crude Oil the Same?

No. Crude oil varies considerably depending on where it was formed and its chemical composition.

Two important characteristics are density and sulfur content.

Density is commonly described using API gravity, a measurement developed by the American Petroleum Institute.

Crude oil with higher API gravity is lighter, while crude with lower API gravity is heavier.

Sulfur content is another important characteristic. Crude oil containing relatively little sulfur is called sweet crude, while crude containing more sulfur is called sour crude.

These properties influence how difficult and expensive the oil is to refine and which petroleum products can be produced efficiently.

Light, sweet crude generally requires less intensive processing to produce certain high-value fuels than heavy, sour crude.

Heavy crude can still be valuable, particularly to refineries equipped with specialized processing units.

The distinction matters because American oil production and American refinery equipment have not always developed around the same types of crude.

What Kind of Oil Does the United States Produce?

A substantial portion of the oil produced from American shale and tight oil formations is relatively light crude.

Light crude is well suited to producing gasoline and other petroleum products, but its properties are not ideal for every refinery configuration.

The United States also produces heavier crude oils, including some from California and Alaska, as well as other varieties with different characteristics.

However, the rapid expansion of domestic production has been particularly important in increasing supplies of light crude oil.

This created a situation in which the country had growing amounts of one type of crude while some refineries continued to need other types.

Why Are Some American Refineries Designed for Heavy Oil?

Refineries are expensive industrial facilities built to operate for decades.

Their equipment is selected and upgraded based on expected crude oil supplies, product demand, and long-term economics.

During periods when American domestic oil production was lower, many refineries invested in equipment that allowed them to process heavier, higher-sulfur imported crude.

Heavy crude is more difficult to convert into valuable fuels without additional processing.

Specialized refinery equipment, including cokers and hydrocrackers, can convert heavier petroleum components into lighter products such as gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel.

These investments allow complex refineries to purchase crude oils that may trade at discounts relative to lighter grades.

A refinery equipped to process heavy crude may therefore find it economically attractive to continue importing that oil rather than switching entirely to domestic light crude.

Changing a refinery's crude oil supply is not necessarily as simple as substituting one barrel for another.

Why Can't Refineries Just Use American Oil Instead?

Many refineries can process several types of crude oil, and they routinely adjust the mixtures they purchase.

However, every refinery has technical and economic limits.

A facility designed to process a mixture containing substantial heavy crude may not operate as efficiently if it suddenly receives only light crude.

Different crude oils produce different proportions of gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and other products.

Refinery equipment also has limits involving processing capacity, sulfur removal, and the balance between different production units.

A major change in crude supply can require adjustments to equipment, operating conditions, or investments in additional processing capacity.

Such modifications can be expensive and may not be justified if suitable imported crude remains available at competitive prices.

This is one reason domestic production can increase without eliminating crude oil imports.

Where Does Most Imported Crude Oil Come From?

Canada has consistently been the largest foreign supplier of crude oil to the United States in recent years.

Canadian oil is particularly important to refineries in the Midwest and Gulf Coast regions.

Much of Canada's production comes from oil sands deposits in Alberta, which produce relatively heavy crude oil or bitumen that is processed or blended for transportation.

American refineries with equipment designed to handle heavy crude are important customers for these supplies.

Other sources of imported crude have included Mexico, Saudi Arabia, and additional producing countries.

The countries supplying American refineries can change over time as production, prices, transportation routes, and trade conditions change.

The important point is that imported oil does not all come from the Middle East. North American trade, particularly with Canada, accounts for a substantial share of U.S. crude oil imports.

Why Does America Export Oil While Importing It?

The United States produces different types of crude oil in different regions, while refineries have varying needs.

Suppose a refinery in the Midwest is designed to process heavy Canadian crude delivered through an established pipeline.

At the same time, an American producer in Texas has large quantities of light crude available near a Gulf Coast export terminal.

A refinery overseas may be willing to pay a competitive price for that light crude.

It can make economic sense for the American producer to export the light oil while the Midwestern refinery continues importing the heavier Canadian oil it is equipped to process.

Both transactions can occur at the same time without representing a shortage of American oil.

This is similar to a country exporting one variety of agricultural product while importing another variety that better meets a particular customer's needs.

The total quantity produced is only part of the picture. Product characteristics and location also matter.

Doesn't Shipping Oil Overseas Waste Money?

Transportation costs are important, but distance alone does not determine the least expensive supply option.

Oil moves through pipelines, tanker ships, barges, rail systems, and trucks.

A refinery near the Canadian border may have a direct pipeline connection to Canadian production facilities.

Obtaining crude oil from a distant American producing region could require a more complicated or expensive transportation arrangement.

Likewise, a refinery on the East Coast may find it economical to purchase imported crude delivered by tanker rather than obtain oil from a domestic region with limited pipeline connections.

Meanwhile, a Gulf Coast producer may have convenient access to export terminals and international shipping routes.

The relevant comparison is the delivered cost of the crude oil, including transportation, handling, quality differences, and any applicable trade costs.

Sometimes a foreign supplier can deliver the right type of crude to an American refinery more economically than a domestic producer can.

Why Doesn't America Keep All the Oil It Produces?

Most American crude oil is produced by commercial companies operating within a market economy.

Those companies sell oil to buyers based on contracts, prices, transportation arrangements, and other commercial considerations.

Crude oil exports are generally permitted under U.S. law, subject to applicable restrictions and regulations.

A broad federal restriction on most crude oil exports was lifted in 2015.

Exporting can provide American producers with access to additional customers and may help balance differences between domestic production and refinery demand.

Restricting exports would not automatically make every American refinery able to process every type of domestically produced oil.

It could also change the prices producers receive, investment incentives, and the operation of domestic and international petroleum markets.

Whether a particular trade restriction would reduce gasoline prices depends on the market conditions and the specific policy involved.

Does Importing Oil Mean America Is Not Energy Independent?

The term energy independence can have several meanings, which sometimes leads to confusion.

One definition focuses on whether a country produces at least as much energy as it consumes over a particular period.

Another focuses on whether the country can meet its energy needs without relying on imports.

These are not the same thing.

The United States has been a net exporter of total energy in recent years, meaning the energy content of its total energy exports has exceeded that of its total energy imports.

The country has also been a net exporter of total petroleum in recent years when crude oil and petroleum products are considered together.

However, the United States has continued to be a net importer of crude oil alone.

These statements can all be true simultaneously because they measure different categories of energy trade.

Being a net exporter does not mean a country has stopped importing energy. It means the measured value or volume of exports exceeds imports under the relevant accounting method.

What Is the Difference Between Crude Oil and Petroleum Products?

Crude oil is the raw material extracted from underground formations.

Petroleum products are materials manufactured from crude oil and other refinery feedstocks.

They include gasoline, diesel fuel, jet fuel, heating oil, lubricants, and numerous industrial products.

The United States exports substantial quantities of refined petroleum products.

For example, American refineries may import heavy crude oil, process it into gasoline and diesel, and export some of those finished products to international customers.

That trade can contribute to the United States being a net exporter of total petroleum even while it imports more crude oil than it exports.

When evaluating claims about American oil imports or exports, it is important to determine whether the figures refer to crude oil alone, finished petroleum products, or total petroleum.

Can America Produce Enough Oil to Meet Its Own Needs?

The answer depends on what is being measured and what assumptions are made about refining, transportation, and trade.

Comparing total crude oil production with refinery demand provides useful information, but it does not establish that every refinery can operate efficiently using only domestic supplies.

The country would also need the right combination of crude oil qualities in the right locations.

Transportation infrastructure would have to move those supplies to the facilities that need them.

Refineries might require investments to process different crude mixtures efficiently.

Changes in international trade could also affect production incentives and the prices paid by American consumers.

Therefore, producing a large quantity of crude oil does not automatically mean that eliminating all imports would be practical or economically beneficial.

Would Stopping Oil Imports Lower Gasoline Prices?

Not necessarily.

If American refineries were required to stop importing crude oil, some could face higher costs obtaining suitable replacement supplies.

Others might need to change operating conditions, reduce production, or invest in equipment modifications.

If refinery output declined or production costs increased, gasoline prices could rise rather than fall.

The effect would depend on which imports were restricted, how much domestic crude was available, and whether transportation and refining systems could adapt.

Domestic crude oil prices are also influenced by international markets.

Even if the United States imported no crude oil, global supply disruptions could still affect domestic prices through trade and market expectations.

Eliminating imports is therefore not a guaranteed way to reduce gasoline prices.

Why Do American Gas Prices Follow World Oil Prices?

Oil is traded internationally, and American producers and refiners participate in that market.

If international crude oil prices rise, domestic producers may receive higher prices for their output because foreign and domestic buyers are competing for available supplies.

American refiners purchasing that crude can face higher costs even when the oil was produced within the United States.

Those costs influence wholesale gasoline prices and eventually the prices consumers pay at gas stations.

The same principle applies in reverse when international oil prices decline, although retail gasoline prices do not always adjust at the same speed.

Domestic production can improve supply availability and affect prices, but it does not completely separate the American market from international petroleum prices.

Could Refineries Be Redesigned to Use More Domestic Oil?

Yes. Refineries can make investments that change the types and quantities of crude oil they process.

Some facilities have already adjusted their operations in response to increased American light crude production.

However, major refinery modifications can cost substantial amounts of money and require extensive engineering, construction, permitting, and operational changes.

Refinery owners must consider whether the investment will be economical over many years.

They also evaluate future crude oil supplies, demand for gasoline and diesel, environmental requirements, and the expected life of the facility.

A refinery designed for heavy crude may continue operating efficiently with imported supplies rather than undertaking a costly conversion.

These decisions are influenced by long-term economics, not simply by the current amount of oil produced in the United States.

Does Importing Oil Make the United States Vulnerable to Supply Disruptions?

Imports can create exposure to disruptions involving foreign production, transportation, trade restrictions, or geopolitical events.

However, domestic production and refining are also exposed to risks, including hurricanes, equipment failures, pipeline outages, and other emergencies.

Purchasing oil from multiple suppliers can provide flexibility when one source becomes unavailable.

The United States also maintains the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, an emergency stockpile of crude oil intended to help address significant supply disruptions under applicable federal law.

Energy security therefore involves more than the amount of oil produced domestically.

It also depends on infrastructure reliability, diversity of supply, storage capacity, refinery capabilities, and the ability to respond to disruptions.

The Bottom Line

The United States can be the world's largest crude oil producer while continuing to import substantial amounts of oil because not all crude oil is the same.

Much of the growth in American production has involved relatively light crude, while some domestic refineries are designed to process heavier varieties that are available from foreign suppliers, particularly Canada.

Transportation costs and refinery locations also influence which sources are most economical.

At the same time, American producers export crude oil to international buyers, and American refineries export substantial quantities of finished petroleum products.

These imports and exports are part of an interconnected market in which oil quality, location, equipment, and price determine where petroleum moves.

Producing large amounts of oil strengthens America's supply position, but it does not eliminate the economic reasons for importing different types of crude or the country's exposure to international oil prices.
