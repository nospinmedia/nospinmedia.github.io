What Is the Strait of Hormuz?

July 28, 20264 min read

What Is the Strait of Hormuz?

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. It is one of the world's most important maritime trade routes because a significant portion of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas (LNG) passes through it every day.

Whenever tensions rise between Iran and other countries, the Strait of Hormuz often becomes a focus of international attention because any disruption to shipping can affect global energy supplies and shipping costs.

Where Is the Strait of Hormuz?

The Strait of Hormuz lies between Iran to the north and Oman and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) to the south.

It serves as the only sea passage connecting the Persian Gulf with the open ocean.

Countries such as Bahrain, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates rely on the strait to export much of their oil and natural gas by sea.

Why Is the Strait of Hormuz Important?

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most strategically important waterways because it is a major route for global energy exports.

Approximately 20 million barrels of oil and petroleum products pass through the strait each day. That represents about 20% of global oil consumption and more than one-quarter of the world's seaborne oil trade. The strait also carries approximately 20% of global liquefied natural gas (LNG) trade.

Because so much global energy trade depends on this single passage, disruptions can influence fuel prices, shipping insurance costs, and international trade.

How Narrow Is the Strait?

At its narrowest point, the Strait of Hormuz is approximately 21 miles (34 kilometers) wide.

Commercial shipping is concentrated into designated traffic separation lanes. Ships entering the Persian Gulf use a two-mile-wide inbound lane, while departing vessels use a two-mile-wide outbound lane. The two lanes are separated by a two-mile-wide buffer zone to improve navigation safety and reduce the risk of collisions.

Because commercial shipping is concentrated into these relatively narrow lanes, the Strait of Hormuz is considered one of the world's most important maritime chokepoints.

Why Is Iran Often Mentioned With the Strait of Hormuz?

Iran controls the northern shoreline of the strait and has long viewed the waterway as strategically important.

During periods of heightened political or military tensions, Iranian officials have occasionally warned that they could restrict or interfere with shipping through the strait.

Such statements often receive global attention because even the possibility of disruption can affect energy markets and international shipping.

In practice, a complete closure of the Strait of Hormuz would be difficult and could have significant economic and military consequences for many countries in the region, including Iran itself.

Who Protects Shipping in the Strait?

Responsibility for maritime security is shared among many nations.

Countries with naval forces operating in or near the region—including the United States and several allied nations—conduct patrols and security operations to help ensure freedom of navigation and protect commercial shipping.

The U.S. military typically conducts these operations through U.S. Central Command (CENTCOM) and its naval forces assigned to the region, working alongside partner nations.

What Happens If Shipping Is Disrupted?

Even temporary disruptions can have global effects.

Potential impacts include:

  • Higher oil prices.
  • Increased gasoline and diesel costs.
  • Higher shipping and insurance expenses.
  • Delays in global trade.
  • Increased military activity in the region.

Financial markets often react quickly to developments involving the Strait of Hormuz because energy supplies affect economies around the world.

Has the Strait Ever Been Closed?

Despite numerous regional conflicts and periods of heightened tension, the Strait of Hormuz has remained open to commercial shipping.

However, there have been incidents involving attacks on ships, seizures of commercial vessels, naval escorts, and military confrontations that temporarily increased risks for vessels traveling through the area.

These events have reinforced the strait's importance to global trade and international security.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that the Strait of Hormuz belongs entirely to one country. In reality, it is bordered by multiple nations, and international maritime law governs many aspects of navigation through the waterway.

Another misunderstanding is that every military incident in the region closes the strait. While conflicts can increase risks or briefly affect shipping operations, commercial traffic has generally continued even during periods of heightened tensions.

Finally, the Strait of Hormuz is important for more than oil. It is also a major route for liquefied natural gas exports and other commercial shipping.

Conclusion

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most important maritime chokepoints, connecting the Persian Gulf to the open ocean. Because roughly one-fifth of the world's oil consumption and a substantial share of global liquefied natural gas exports pass through this narrow waterway, events affecting the strait can influence energy prices, international trade, and global security. This is why it is frequently mentioned in news about Iran, the Middle East, and international military operations.
