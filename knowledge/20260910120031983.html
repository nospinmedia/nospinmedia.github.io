Can Fracking Cause Earthquakes?

September 16, 20267 min read

Yes. Hydraulic fracturing, commonly called fracking, can cause earthquakes. However, most seismic events directly caused by the fracturing process are extremely small and cannot be felt at the surface.

There is also an important distinction that is often lost when earthquakes are discussed alongside oil and gas production. Some of the more significant increases in earthquake activity associated with oil and gas operations have been caused not by hydraulic fracturing itself, but by the underground disposal of wastewater produced by oil and gas wells.

Both activities involve injecting fluids underground, but they serve different purposes and can affect faults differently.

What Happens During Fracking?

Hydraulic fracturing is used to extract oil or natural gas from rock formations that do not allow those resources to flow easily into a well.

A well is drilled into the target formation, often vertically at first and then horizontally through the rock. A mixture consisting primarily of water, along with sand and smaller amounts of chemical additives, is pumped into the well at high pressure.

The pressure creates or enlarges fractures in the rock. Sand or another material known as a proppant remains inside the fractures and helps keep them open after the pressure is reduced.

Oil or natural gas can then move through those fractures toward the well.

Breaking rock in this way releases energy. Sensitive seismic instruments can detect many of these tiny events even though people at the surface cannot feel them.

How Can Fracking Trigger a Larger Earthquake?

The fractures intentionally created during hydraulic fracturing are generally relatively small. Problems can arise if the operation affects a larger existing fault nearby.

Faults throughout Earth's crust are subjected to natural geological stresses. Some are stable, while others may already be close to the point where they could slip.

If injected fluid reaches a fault, increased fluid pressure can reduce the effective friction holding the rocks on either side together. If the fault is already sufficiently stressed, that change can allow it to move.

The resulting earthquake is not powered simply by the pressure of the fracking equipment. Much of the energy released by an induced earthquake was already stored as geological stress in the surrounding rock. The human activity can provide the change that allows the fault to slip.

This is why the geology surrounding a well is important. Fracking does not produce the same seismic response everywhere.

Most Fracking Earthquakes Are Very Small

Hydraulic fracturing routinely creates microseismic events as rock fractures underground. These are useful to operators and scientists because instruments can use them to study where fractures are developing.

Most are far below the magnitude necessary to be noticed by people.

Occasionally, however, hydraulic fracturing has triggered larger earthquakes when operations interacted with existing faults. Some of these events have been strong enough to be felt at the surface and, in unusual cases, cause minor damage.

That makes it inaccurate to say that fracking cannot cause noticeable earthquakes. It can. But it is equally misleading to assume that every earthquake associated with an oil or gas field was directly caused by the hydraulic-fracturing process.

Fracking and Wastewater Injection Are Not the Same Thing

This distinction is central to understanding induced earthquakes associated with oil and gas production.

Hydraulic fracturing involves injecting fluid under high pressure for a relatively limited period to fracture a particular underground rock formation.

Wastewater disposal is different.

Oil and gas wells can bring large amounts of naturally occurring underground water to the surface along with oil and gas. Additional fluids used during well operations may also return to the surface. This wastewater can contain salts, minerals, hydrocarbons and other substances and must be managed appropriately.

One method is to inject wastewater into deep underground formations through disposal wells.

A disposal well may receive large quantities of fluid over an extended period. That can change underground fluid pressures across an area much larger than the immediate vicinity of the well.

If those pressure changes reach a fault that is already under substantial stress, they can increase the likelihood that it will slip.

Why Has Wastewater Disposal Caused Larger Earthquake Problems?

The important difference is not simply pressure at the wellhead. The volume of fluid, duration of injection, underground pathways and nearby geology all matter.

Wastewater disposal can introduce very large volumes of fluid into underground formations over long periods. Pressure changes can migrate through permeable rock and fractures beyond the injection site.

That means a fault does not necessarily have to pass directly through a disposal well for injection to affect it.

In parts of the United States, substantial increases in earthquake activity have been linked to wastewater disposal associated with oil and gas production. Oklahoma became a particularly well-known example after experiencing a dramatic increase in earthquakes during periods of extensive wastewater injection.

Studies and monitoring have shown that changes in injection practices can affect earthquake activity, demonstrating that at least some of these earthquake sequences were induced rather than simply coincidental natural events.

Does Fracking Create New Faults?

Hydraulic fracturing intentionally creates small fractures in the target rock, but the larger earthquakes of concern generally involve movement on existing geological faults.

Many faults are buried and cannot be seen at Earth's surface. Some may not have been previously mapped because they have shown little recent activity.

A fault can also be naturally stressed without currently producing earthquakes. Changing underground pressure can sometimes bring such a fault closer to failure.

This is one reason geological surveys and seismic monitoring are important when evaluating areas where fluid injection will occur.

Can Earthquakes Happen After Injection Stops?

Yes.

Changes in underground fluid pressure do not necessarily disappear immediately when pumping stops. Pressure can continue spreading through underground formations, and faults can respond later.

As a result, induced earthquakes may sometimes continue after injection has been reduced or stopped.

The timing depends on the geology, the amount of fluid injected, the location of nearby faults and how pressure moves through the underground rock.

How Do Scientists Know an Earthquake Was Human-Induced?

Determining the cause of an individual earthquake is not always simple. Natural earthquakes occur in many of the same regions where industrial activities take place.

Scientists examine several kinds of evidence, including where and when earthquakes occur, changes in earthquake frequency, the locations and depths of injection wells, injection volumes and pressures, known faults and models of how underground pressure is changing.

A sudden increase in earthquakes following substantial fluid injection can be important evidence, particularly when seismic activity changes after injection practices are modified.

Researchers can also use networks of seismometers to locate earthquakes precisely and determine how faults are moving.

The conclusion that an earthquake was induced is therefore normally based on multiple lines of geological and operational evidence rather than simply the fact that an oil or gas well happens to be nearby.

Are Induced Earthquakes Different From Natural Earthquakes?

Once a fault slips, an induced earthquake works according to the same basic physics as a natural tectonic earthquake.

Rock moves along a fault and releases stored energy as seismic waves. Those waves travel through the ground and can produce shaking at the surface.

The distinction concerns what triggered the fault to move.

In a natural earthquake, geological forces such as tectonic plate movement eventually overcome the friction holding a fault in place. In an induced earthquake, human activity changes underground stresses or fluid pressures enough to help trigger the movement.

The shaking itself does not carry a signature that makes it harmless simply because human activity helped initiate it.

Can the Risk Be Reduced?

Induced-earthquake risk can be managed, although it cannot always be eliminated completely.

Operators and regulators can examine local geology, monitor seismic activity, control where and how fluids are injected, limit injection volumes or pressures, and modify or stop operations when earthquake activity increases.

Some areas use monitoring systems sometimes described as traffic-light systems. Small seismic events may trigger additional monitoring or operational changes, while larger events can require injection to be reduced or stopped.

Understanding the locations of faults is particularly important, although not every underground fault has been identified.

The Bottom Line

Fracking can cause earthquakes, but the details matter.

Hydraulic fracturing intentionally breaks underground rock and routinely produces tiny seismic events that are generally too small to feel. In some cases, however, injected fluid can affect an existing stressed fault and trigger a larger earthquake.

Many of the more significant earthquake increases associated with oil and gas production have instead been linked to deep wastewater disposal. Injecting large volumes of wastewater over long periods can change underground fluid pressures and trigger movement on faults that were already under geological stress.

So it is accurate to say that human oil and gas operations can cause earthquakes. But understanding whether hydraulic fracturing itself or wastewater disposal is responsible is essential to understanding how and why those earthquakes occur.
