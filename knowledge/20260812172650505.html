What Is a State of Emergency?

August 19, 202611 min read

What Is a State of Emergency?

A state of emergency is a legal declaration that allows a government to use special authorities, procedures, or resources to respond to an emergency or an imminent threat.

The phrase can sound as though it describes a particular level of danger, but that is not necessarily what it means. A state of emergency is primarily a legal status. What powers become available depends on who issued the declaration and the law under which it was issued.

Emergencies may be declared for hurricanes, floods, wildfires, disease outbreaks, civil disturbances, infrastructure failures, energy shortages, or other serious situations. In some cases, an emergency can be declared before the expected event occurs so officials can prepare and move resources into place.

Who Can Declare a State of Emergency?

Different officials can have emergency-declaration authority under different laws.

At the state level, governors generally have authority under their state's laws to declare emergencies. State law determines what conditions justify a declaration and what powers become available afterward.

Local governments may also give mayors, county officials, or other authorities the power to declare local emergencies.

At the federal level, the president and certain federal officials have separate emergency authorities established by federal law. These federal declarations are legally distinct from a governor's state emergency declaration.

There is therefore no single nationwide legal definition that governs every use of the phrase "state of emergency."

What Does Declaring an Emergency Actually Do?

It depends on the law being invoked.

An emergency declaration does not create unlimited government power. Instead, it generally activates specific authorities that lawmakers have previously established for emergency situations.

Depending on the jurisdiction and type of emergency, a declaration may allow government officials to:

  • Activate emergency response plans.
  • Mobilize personnel and equipment.
  • Access emergency funds.
  • Request assistance from other governments.
  • Temporarily modify certain administrative procedures.
  • Use expedited purchasing or contracting procedures.
  • Coordinate emergency services across agencies.
  • Activate certain National Guard resources.
  • Issue evacuation or other emergency orders when authorized by law.
  • Temporarily suspend or modify particular regulatory requirements when statutes permit it.

The declaration itself does not necessarily activate all of these powers. Officials must have legal authority for the specific actions they take.

Can an Emergency Be Declared Before a Disaster Happens?

Yes.

Emergency declarations can sometimes be issued when a serious threat is imminent rather than waiting for damage to occur.

For example, officials may declare an emergency before a hurricane makes landfall. Doing so can allow governments to position emergency personnel, equipment, supplies, shelters, and other resources before conditions deteriorate.

Federal emergency declarations under the Stafford Act can also be used in anticipation of certain threats so federal assistance can support preparations before an incident occurs.

This means an emergency declaration does not necessarily indicate that a disaster has already happened.

Is a State Emergency the Same as a National Emergency?

No.

A governor's state emergency declaration and a presidential national emergency declaration are separate legal actions based on different laws.

At the federal level, the National Emergencies Act establishes procedures for presidential declarations of national emergencies.

The declaration of a national emergency does not itself give the president unlimited emergency authority. Instead, it generally allows the president to invoke specific emergency powers that Congress has provided in other federal statutes.

The president must identify the statutory authorities being used, and national emergencies are subject to procedures involving publication, reporting, renewal, and termination.

A state emergency declared by a governor operates under that state's constitution and laws rather than the National Emergencies Act.

What Is a Stafford Act Emergency Declaration?

The Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act, commonly called the Stafford Act, provides a separate federal emergency and disaster framework.

Under the Stafford Act, the president can issue an emergency declaration when federal assistance is needed to supplement state, tribal, territorial, or local efforts to protect lives, public health, safety, or property.

In most situations, a governor or tribal chief executive requests the federal declaration after determining that the situation exceeds available capabilities. Federal law also permits the president to issue a Stafford Act emergency declaration without such a request in certain situations where the federal government has primary responsibility for the response.

A Stafford Act emergency declaration can authorize federal assistance through agencies such as the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA).

Is an Emergency Declaration the Same as a Major Disaster Declaration?

No.

The Stafford Act distinguishes between an emergency declaration and a major disaster declaration.

Both can provide federal assistance, but they have different statutory requirements and can make different forms of assistance available.

Major disaster declarations are commonly associated with severe events such as hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, earthquakes, fires, and other catastrophes that cause damage beyond the ability of state, tribal, territorial, and local governments to handle effectively without federal assistance.

A governor or tribal chief executive generally must request a major disaster declaration. Federal assistance is intended to supplement rather than replace state and local response efforts.

What Is a Public Health Emergency?

A public health emergency is another distinct type of declaration.

Under federal law, the Secretary of Health and Human Services can determine that a public health emergency exists under Section 319 of the Public Health Service Act.

Such a declaration can activate specific federal health authorities and provide additional flexibility for responding to disease outbreaks, health threats, or other qualifying emergencies.

A public health emergency is not automatically the same thing as a presidential national emergency or a Stafford Act emergency. Multiple types of declarations can sometimes exist at the same time because they activate different legal authorities.

Does a State of Emergency Mean Martial Law?

No.

A declaration of emergency and martial law are different concepts.

An emergency declaration normally activates authorities established by emergency-management laws. It does not automatically transfer civilian government functions to the military or place the population under military rule.

Military or National Guard forces may assist during an emergency, but their involvement does not by itself mean martial law has been imposed.

Does an Emergency Suspend the Constitution?

No.

Declaring an emergency does not automatically suspend the Constitution or eliminate constitutional rights.

Government may possess additional powers during an emergency because laws enacted in advance authorize particular actions under emergency conditions. Those actions remain subject to constitutional limits.

Courts can review challenged emergency measures when an appropriate legal case is brought before them.

The existence of an emergency can be relevant when courts evaluate particular government actions, but an emergency declaration does not create a general exception to the Constitution.

Does a State of Emergency Automatically Activate the National Guard?

No.

A governor may activate National Guard forces as part of an emergency response, but the declaration of an emergency and the activation of the Guard are legally separate actions.

Governors frequently use Guard personnel during natural disasters and other emergencies because Guard units can provide transportation, engineering, medical, logistics, communications, security, and other capabilities.

The legal authority, command structure, and funding for a National Guard deployment depend on whether Guard members are serving under State Active Duty, Title 32, or federal Title 10 status.

Can Emergency Declarations Change Normal Government Rules?

Sometimes.

One purpose of emergency laws is to give governments flexibility when ordinary procedures would interfere with a rapid response.

For example, emergency authority may permit expedited contracting, purchasing, movement of government resources, or temporary modifications to particular regulatory requirements.

But officials cannot simply ignore any law they find inconvenient. The emergency statute or another lawful authority must permit the particular action.

The scope of those powers varies substantially among jurisdictions and types of emergencies.

Can Emergency Orders Restrict What People Do?

Sometimes, when authorized by law and consistent with constitutional requirements.

Depending on the type of emergency and the jurisdiction, government officials may have authority to issue evacuation orders, restrict access to dangerous areas, close certain government facilities, regulate use of particular resources, or take other temporary protective measures.

The precise authority depends on applicable federal, state, or local law.

An emergency declaration alone does not mean officials automatically possess every conceivable emergency power.

Can Courts Block Emergency Actions?

Yes.

Emergency actions can be challenged in court just like other government actions when the requirements for bringing a legal case are satisfied.

Courts may consider whether an official actually possessed the claimed emergency authority, whether statutory requirements were followed, and whether the government's actions violated constitutional protections or other laws.

Emergency powers can be broad, but they are not automatically beyond judicial review.

How Long Does a State of Emergency Last?

There is no universal time limit.

The answer depends on the law governing the particular declaration.

Some state and local emergencies automatically expire after a specified period unless renewed. Others continue until the governor or another authorized official terminates them, or until procedures established by state law bring them to an end.

Federal declarations have their own rules.

Under the National Emergencies Act, a presidential national emergency generally terminates after one year unless the president renews it. The president can also terminate the emergency, and Congress has a statutory process for attempting to terminate a national emergency through legislation.

Stafford Act emergency and major disaster declarations operate under a different framework. The declaration itself does not have the same automatic one-year expiration rule, although particular forms of federal assistance and specific incident periods have their own limits.

This is another reason the type of emergency declaration matters.

Can More Than One Emergency Declaration Exist at the Same Time?

Yes.

A single event can lead to multiple declarations under different laws.

For example, a governor could declare a state emergency while the president separately issues a Stafford Act declaration providing federal assistance. A serious health crisis could also involve a federal public health emergency or another federal emergency declaration.

These declarations do not necessarily duplicate one another. Each can activate different legal authorities, funding sources, agencies, or procedures.

Common Misunderstandings

A State of Emergency Means Martial Law

No.

An emergency declaration does not automatically transfer civilian authority to the military or impose martial law.

The Constitution Is Suspended During an Emergency

No.

Constitutional protections continue to apply. Emergency laws may provide government with additional authorities, but those authorities remain subject to constitutional limits.

Declaring an Emergency Gives the President or Governor Unlimited Power

No.

Emergency powers come from constitutions and statutes. Officials must have legal authority for the actions they take.

The National Guard Automatically Takes Over

No.

National Guard activation is a separate action. Guard forces may assist with an emergency response when properly activated under state or federal authority.

An Emergency Declaration Means a Disaster Has Already Happened

No.

Officials may sometimes declare an emergency in anticipation of an imminent threat so preparations and emergency resources can be put in place beforehand.

Every Emergency Declaration Does the Same Thing

No.

A governor's state emergency, a presidential national emergency, a Stafford Act emergency, a major disaster declaration, and a federal public health emergency are different legal mechanisms that can activate different authorities.

Why the Type of Emergency Matters

When a government declares an emergency, the most important question is not simply whether the word emergency appears in the announcement.

The important questions are:

  • Who issued the declaration?
  • What law was used?
  • What specific powers does that law activate?
  • How long can those powers remain in effect?
  • What limits apply?

Those details determine what the government can actually do.

A hurricane emergency issued by a governor may primarily mobilize state resources and emergency responders. A Stafford Act declaration may unlock federal disaster assistance. A national emergency may activate particular statutory powers available to the president. A public health emergency may provide health agencies with authorities specifically designed for medical threats.

The phrase state of emergency therefore describes a legal response mechanism, not a single predetermined level of danger or government power.

Conclusion

A state of emergency is a legal declaration used to activate authorities and resources for responding to a serious situation or imminent threat. It does not automatically impose martial law, suspend the Constitution, activate the National Guard, or give government officials unlimited power.

The effects of an emergency declaration depend on who issued it and the law being invoked. State emergencies, presidential national emergencies, Stafford Act declarations, major disaster declarations, and public health emergencies are separate legal mechanisms with different purposes and powers.

Understanding an emergency declaration therefore requires looking beyond the word emergency and identifying the specific legal authority behind it.
