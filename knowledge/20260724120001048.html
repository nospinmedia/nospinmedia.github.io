What Is an Injunction?

July 24, 20265 min read

What Is an Injunction?

An injunction is a court order that requires a person, business, or government agency to either do something or stop doing something. Unlike a judgment that awards money after a case is over, an injunction is intended to prevent harm while a legal dispute is still being decided or to permanently prohibit unlawful conduct after the case concludes.

Injunctions are common in civil lawsuits and frequently appear in cases involving government policies, constitutional rights, environmental regulations, elections, business disputes, and intellectual property. Because they can immediately affect how laws and policies are carried out, injunctions often receive significant media attention.

Why Do Courts Issue Injunctions?

Courts issue injunctions when monetary damages alone would not adequately fix the harm.

For example, if a government policy is alleged to violate constitutional rights, waiting months or years for a final trial could allow irreversible harm to occur. An injunction can temporarily preserve the status quo while the court considers the legal issues.

Likewise, a court may prevent a company from using confidential trade secrets or stop construction that could permanently damage protected land until the dispute is resolved.

What Are the Different Types of Injunctions?

Courts generally issue three main types of injunctions.

Temporary Restraining Order (TRO)

A Temporary Restraining Order (TRO) is an emergency court order issued to prevent immediate harm.

A TRO usually lasts only a short time—often days or weeks—until the court can hold a more complete hearing.

Because of their urgent nature, TROs may sometimes be issued before all parties have had an opportunity to fully present their arguments.

Preliminary Injunction

A preliminary injunction is issued early in a lawsuit after both sides have had an opportunity to present evidence and legal arguments.

Its purpose is to preserve the existing situation while the lawsuit continues.

Preliminary injunctions often remain in effect until the court reaches a final decision on the case.

Permanent Injunction

A permanent injunction is issued after the court has fully decided the case.

Rather than temporarily preserving the status quo, it permanently requires or prohibits certain conduct unless a higher court later overturns or modifies the order.

How Does a Judge Decide Whether to Grant an Injunction?

Although the exact legal standards can vary depending on the court and the type of injunction requested, judges generally consider several important factors.

These often include:

  • Whether the party requesting the injunction is likely to succeed in the lawsuit.
  • Whether that party would suffer irreparable harm without immediate court action.
  • Whether the balance of hardships favors granting the injunction.
  • Whether granting the injunction serves the public interest.

The judge weighs all of these factors before deciding whether an injunction is appropriate.

What Does "Irreparable Harm" Mean?

A key concept in injunction cases is irreparable harm.

Irreparable harm refers to injury that cannot be adequately repaired by money alone.

Examples may include:

  • Violations of constitutional rights.
  • Disclosure of confidential trade secrets.
  • Permanent environmental damage.
  • Loss of voting rights.
  • Destruction of unique property.

If the harm can be fully compensated with money after the case ends, an injunction may be less likely to be granted.

Why Are Injunctions Common in Lawsuits Against the Government?

When governments adopt new laws, regulations, or executive actions, opponents sometimes argue that the policy violates the Constitution or federal law.

Rather than waiting until the lawsuit is finished, plaintiffs often ask the court to temporarily block the policy from taking effect.

If the judge grants an injunction, the challenged policy may be paused while the litigation continues.

This is why news reports often state that a federal judge has "blocked," "halted," or "temporarily stopped" a government action. In many cases, the court has issued a preliminary injunction while the lawsuit proceeds.

Can an Injunction Be Appealed?

Yes.

Orders granting or denying injunctions are often appealed because they can have immediate and significant consequences.

Under federal law, many orders granting or denying preliminary injunctions may be appealed immediately, even though the underlying lawsuit has not yet been resolved. This is an exception to the general rule that most appeals occur only after a final judgment.

An appellate court may:

  • Leave the injunction in place.
  • Modify its scope.
  • Suspend the injunction while the appeal proceeds.
  • Reverse the lower court's decision.

In high-profile cases, injunction disputes sometimes reach the U.S. Supreme Court before the underlying lawsuit has been fully resolved.

What Is a Nationwide Injunction?

In some cases, a federal judge issues an injunction that applies across the entire country rather than only to the parties involved in the lawsuit. These are commonly called nationwide injunctions or universal injunctions. Their use has become the subject of significant legal debate, and courts continue to evaluate when such broad relief is appropriate. Because the legal standards continue to evolve, nationwide injunctions are considered on a case-by-case basis.

Does an Injunction Mean Someone Has Won the Case?

No.

This is one of the most common misunderstandings.

A temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction does not determine who ultimately wins the lawsuit.

Instead, it is an interim ruling intended to prevent potential harm while the court considers the legal issues.

Only after the case is fully litigated—or otherwise resolved—does the court issue a final judgment.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that every injunction permanently blocks a law or policy. In reality, many injunctions are temporary and remain in effect only until the court reaches a final decision.

Another misunderstanding is that an injunction automatically means a law is unconstitutional. The court may simply conclude that the legal questions deserve further review before allowing the challenged action to proceed.

Finally, an injunction does not necessarily stop a policy everywhere. Depending on the circumstances and the court's order, an injunction may apply only to specific parties, specific jurisdictions, or, in some cases, nationwide.

Conclusion

An injunction is a court order used to prevent harm while a legal dispute is resolved or to permanently prohibit unlawful conduct after a final judgment. Whether issued against individuals, businesses, or government agencies, injunctions play an important role in protecting legal rights and preserving the status quo. Because they can immediately affect the enforcement of laws and public policies, injunctions are among the most closely watched decisions in the American legal system.
