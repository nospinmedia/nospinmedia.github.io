What Is Mail-In Voting and How Does It Work?

August 13, 20269 min read

What Is Mail-In Voting and How Does It Work?

Mail-in voting allows eligible voters to receive a ballot before Election Day, complete it outside a polling place, and return it to election officials. Depending on state law, the ballot may be returned through the mail, delivered directly to an election office, or placed in an authorized ballot drop box.

Mail voting is used in both primary and general elections. Every state offers some form of absentee or mail voting, but the rules governing who may use it, how ballots are requested, how they are verified, and when they must be returned vary by state.

Is Mail-In Voting the Same as Absentee Voting?

The terms are often used interchangeably, but they can describe somewhat different systems.

Traditionally, absentee voting referred to a voter requesting a ballot because the voter could not appear at a polling place on Election Day. Some states still require voters to provide an approved reason, or excuse, to receive an absentee ballot.

Other states offer no-excuse absentee voting, meaning any eligible voter may request a mail ballot without providing a specific reason.

Some states use broader vote-by-mail systems in which ballots are automatically mailed to registered voters rather than requiring each voter to request one for that election.

Because states use different terminology, a ballot described as an absentee ballot in one state may be very similar to what another state calls a mail-in or vote-by-mail ballot.

How Does Someone Get a Mail Ballot?

The process depends on the state.

In some states, voters must submit an application requesting an absentee or mail ballot. Election officials verify the request and the voter's eligibility before issuing the ballot.

Other states automatically mail ballots to registered voters under their vote-by-mail systems.

Deadlines for requesting ballots also vary. Voters need to follow the requirements established by their state or local election officials.

How Does Someone Vote by Mail?

Although procedures differ by state, the basic process generally includes several steps:

  1. The voter receives an official ballot and voting instructions.
  2. The voter marks the ballot privately.
  3. The completed ballot is placed into the required ballot envelope or envelopes.
  4. The voter completes any required information on the return envelope, which may include a signature or other identifying information.
  5. The ballot is returned according to the state's rules.
  6. Election officials receive and verify the ballot before it is accepted for counting.

Some states have additional requirements, such as witness signatures, identification information, or notarization.

How Can a Mail Ballot Be Returned?

Depending on state and local law, voters may be able to return a mail ballot by:

  • Mailing it through the U.S. Postal Service.
  • Delivering it directly to an election office.
  • Using an authorized ballot drop box.
  • Returning it to another location specifically authorized by election officials.

Not every return method is available everywhere, so voters must follow the rules for their jurisdiction.

What Is a Ballot Drop Box?

A ballot drop box is a secure container operated by election officials where voters can directly return completed mail or absentee ballots without using the postal system.

States establish their own requirements for drop boxes. Security measures may include locks, tamper-evident seals, surveillance, controlled access, and documented chain-of-custody procedures for collecting and transporting ballots.

Authorized election workers retrieve the ballots and deliver them for processing under procedures established by the jurisdiction.

What Is the Deadline for a Mail Ballot?

There is no single nationwide deadline for returning mail ballots.

Some states require election officials to receive a ballot by a particular time on Election Day. Other states may count ballots received after Election Day if they were postmarked by a specified deadline and arrive within the period allowed by state law.

This distinction is important. A voter who places a ballot in the mail on Election Day may be too late in a state with a receipt deadline even though the ballot was mailed before polls closed.

Voters should always check their state's current return deadline and allow enough time for postal delivery when returning a ballot by mail.

How Do Election Officials Verify Mail Ballots?

Mail ballots are not simply removed from envelopes and automatically counted.

Election officials first determine whether a returned ballot satisfies the state's requirements.

Depending on the jurisdiction, verification may include:

  • Confirming that the ballot was issued to an eligible voter.
  • Checking required information on the return envelope.
  • Comparing the voter's signature with a signature already on file.
  • Checking identification information where required.
  • Confirming required witness or notarization information in states that use those procedures.

The ballot itself is kept separate from identifying information during the counting process so election officials can verify that an eligible voter returned a ballot without connecting that voter to the choices marked on the ballot.

What Is Ballot Curing?

Sometimes a mail ballot has a problem that prevents it from being immediately accepted.

For example, the voter may have forgotten to sign the envelope, or election officials may determine that a required signature does not sufficiently match the signature on file.

Many states provide a process known as ballot curing. This gives the voter an opportunity to correct certain problems so the ballot can be counted.

The procedures and deadlines for curing a ballot vary by state. Election officials may contact affected voters and provide instructions for verifying their identity or correcting the missing information.

Not every ballot problem can necessarily be cured, and not every state uses identical procedures.

Can Someone Vote by Mail and Then Vote Again in Person?

Only one ballot may be counted for each voter in an election.

Election officials maintain records showing when voters have been issued mail ballots and whether those ballots have been returned or accepted.

If someone who received a mail ballot later attempts to vote in person, state procedures are designed to determine whether that person has already voted.

Depending on state law and the circumstances, the voter may surrender an unused mail ballot, cast a regular ballot after eligibility is confirmed, or be given a provisional ballot. A provisional ballot is kept separate and is counted only after election officials determine that the voter is eligible and that another ballot from that voter has not already been counted.

When Are Mail Ballots Counted?

State laws determine when election officials may begin processing and counting mail ballots.

Processing may involve verifying the return envelope, checking the voter's eligibility, separating the ballot from identifying information, and preparing the ballot for tabulation.

Some states allow portions of this work to begin before Election Day. Others restrict when particular processing or counting steps may begin.

Because these rules differ, states with large numbers of mail ballots may report results at different speeds. A delay in reporting mail ballots does not by itself indicate a problem with the ballots; it may simply reflect the state's processing rules and the number of ballots that must be verified.

What Happens to a Rejected Mail Ballot?

A ballot may be rejected if it does not satisfy requirements established by state law.

Possible reasons include:

  • Arriving after the applicable deadline.
  • Missing a required signature.
  • Failing a signature verification process.
  • Missing required identification or documentation.
  • Missing a required witness signature in states that require one.
  • Other failures to comply with the state's ballot-return requirements.

Where ballot curing is available, the voter may have an opportunity to correct certain problems before a deadline. If the issue cannot be corrected or the voter does not complete the cure process in time, the ballot may not be counted.

How Is Mail-In Voting Secured?

Election officials use multiple safeguards rather than relying on a single security measure.

Depending on the jurisdiction, those safeguards can include:

  • Voter registration and eligibility checks.
  • Records showing which voters were issued ballots.
  • Signature or identity verification.
  • Ballot tracking systems.
  • Secure ballot envelopes.
  • Controlled ballot drop boxes.
  • Chain-of-custody procedures.
  • Reconciliation of ballots issued, returned, accepted, and rejected.
  • Post-election audits and other verification procedures.

The exact safeguards vary because elections are administered primarily under state and local laws.

Can Voters Track Their Mail Ballots?

Many states and local election jurisdictions provide online ballot tracking.

Depending on the system, a voter may be able to see when a ballot was mailed, when election officials received the returned ballot, and whether it was accepted for counting or requires additional action.

Availability and the information provided vary by jurisdiction.

Common Misunderstandings

Mail-In Voting and Absentee Voting Are Always Different

Not necessarily.

States use different terminology, and the processes often overlap. Some call the system absentee voting, while others use terms such as mail-in voting or vote by mail.

A Mail Ballot Is Automatically Counted When It Arrives

No.

Election officials generally verify that the ballot satisfies the state's requirements before accepting it for counting.

Receiving a Mail Ballot Means Someone Can Vote Twice

No.

Election officials maintain records designed to ensure that only one ballot is counted for each eligible voter.

Every State Uses the Same Deadline

No.

Some states use receipt deadlines, while others permit qualifying ballots to arrive after Election Day under specified conditions. Voters must follow the rules in their own state.

Election Workers Always Wait Until Election Day to Process Mail Ballots

No.

State laws determine when different stages of mail-ballot processing and counting may begin.

Why Mail-In Voting Matters

Mail-in voting gives eligible voters another way to participate in elections without appearing at a polling place on Election Day. It can be particularly useful for people who are traveling, serving in the military, living overseas, dealing with illness or disability, or simply prefer to vote before Election Day where state law allows it.

Because election laws differ significantly among states, understanding mail-in voting requires distinguishing the basic process from the specific rules governing an individual election.

Conclusion

Mail-in voting allows eligible voters to receive and complete ballots outside a polling place and return them to election officials for verification and counting. Depending on the state, voters may need to request a ballot or may automatically receive one.

Election officials verify returned ballots using procedures established by state law, maintain records designed to prevent more than one ballot from being counted for the same voter, and may provide voters an opportunity to correct certain ballot problems. Deadlines, verification methods, ballot-curing procedures, and return options vary by state, making state and local election rules the final authority for any particular election.
