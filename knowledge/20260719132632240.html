How Does the FDA Recall Food?

Food recalls help remove products from stores and homes when they may pose a risk to public health. While many recalls receive national attention, they are a routine part of the U.S. food safety system. The goal is simple: prevent illness or injury by quickly identifying and removing potentially unsafe food.

Although the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) oversees most foods sold in the United States, not every recall begins with the agency. Food manufacturers, distributors, retailers, state health departments, and consumers all play a role in identifying potential problems.

What Is a Food Recall?

A food recall is the process of removing a food product from the marketplace because it may be unsafe to eat. Recalls can involve products that have already reached grocery stores, restaurants, warehouses, or consumers' homes.

Most recalls are voluntary. After discovering a problem, the manufacturer typically works with the FDA to remove the affected product and notify the public. In rare situations where a company refuses to act and the risk is serious, the FDA has legal authority to order a mandatory recall for certain foods.

Why Are Foods Recalled?

Food recalls happen for many reasons, including:

  • Harmful bacteria such as Salmonella, Listeria, or E. coli.
  • Undeclared allergens, including milk, eggs, peanuts, tree nuts, soy, wheat, fish, sesame, or shellfish.
  • Foreign objects such as metal fragments, glass, plastic, or rubber.
  • Chemical contamination.
  • Packaging or labeling mistakes that could mislead consumers.
  • Manufacturing problems that make food unsafe.

Undeclared allergens are among the most common reasons for FDA food recalls because even a small amount of an ingredient can cause a severe allergic reaction in sensitive individuals.

How Does the Recall Process Work?

A typical FDA food recall follows several steps.

First, a potential problem is identified. This may come from routine company testing, FDA inspections, state health officials, consumer complaints, or investigations into foodborne illness outbreaks.

Next, the manufacturer investigates the issue to determine which products may be affected. Working with the FDA, the company identifies production dates, lot numbers, package sizes, and distribution areas.

The company then begins removing affected products from warehouses, retailers, and distributors. Stores may pull the items from shelves, while distributors stop shipping them.

If consumers may already have the product, the FDA publishes a public recall announcement. The notice typically includes:

  • Product name
  • Brand name
  • Package size
  • Lot or batch numbers
  • Expiration or "Best By" dates
  • Photos of the packaging when available
  • The reason for the recall
  • Instructions for consumers

The FDA continues monitoring the recall until it determines that reasonable efforts have been made to remove the affected product.

What Should Consumers Do?

If a recalled food is in your home, carefully read the recall notice.

Depending on the situation, you may be instructed to:

  • Throw the product away.
  • Return it to the retailer for a refund.
  • Contact the manufacturer for replacement instructions.

Do not taste food to determine whether it is safe. Some harmful bacteria, toxins, or allergens cannot be detected by sight, smell, or taste.

If you believe you became ill after eating a recalled product, contact a healthcare provider. You may also report the incident to the FDA through its consumer complaint system.

Are All Food Recalls Dangerous?

Not every recall represents the same level of risk. Some involve life-threatening contamination, while others correct labeling errors that pose little danger to most people.

The FDA classifies recalls based on the potential health risk. These classifications help regulators prioritize response efforts but are generally assigned after the recall has already begun.

The three recall classifications are:

  • Class I: A reasonable chance that using the product could cause serious health problems or death.
  • Class II: Use of the product may cause temporary or medically reversible health problems, or the chance of serious harm is remote.
  • Class III: The product is unlikely to cause adverse health effects but violates FDA regulations in some way.

Each classification reflects the potential risk rather than how widely the product was distributed.

Does the FDA Recall All Foods?

No. The FDA regulates most foods sold in the United States, including:

  • Packaged foods
  • Produce
  • Seafood
  • Dairy products
  • Beverages
  • Dietary supplements

However, the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) oversees recalls involving most meat, poultry, and certain egg products. As a result, consumers may see recall announcements issued by either agency depending on the product involved.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that every recalled product has caused illnesses. In many cases, recalls are issued before anyone becomes sick because testing identified a potential hazard.

Another misunderstanding is that a recalled product is always contaminated. Some recalls involve labeling mistakes, packaging defects, or undeclared allergens rather than bacteria or foreign objects.

Finally, not every food safety investigation leads to a recall. If testing determines that products are safe or the affected items never entered commerce, a recall may not be necessary.

Conclusion

Food recalls are an important part of the nation's food safety system. By working with manufacturers, state agencies, retailers, and public health officials, the FDA helps remove potentially unsafe products before they can cause additional illnesses. Understanding how recalls work—and knowing how to identify affected products—can help consumers make informed decisions and protect themselves and their families when a recall occurs.
