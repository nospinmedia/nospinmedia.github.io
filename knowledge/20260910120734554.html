Why Do Leaves Change Color in the Fall?

September 20, 20267 min read

Leaves change color in the fall because many trees are preparing for winter. As days become shorter and temperatures begin to fall, deciduous trees gradually stop maintaining the green pigment chlorophyll in their leaves. As chlorophyll breaks down, other pigments that were already present become visible, while some trees also produce additional pigments that create red and purple colors.

The familiar yellow, orange and red landscapes of autumn are therefore not caused by a single chemical change. Several pigments and biological processes are involved.

Why Are Leaves Green During the Summer?

Most leaves appear green because they contain large amounts of chlorophyll.

Chlorophyll is essential to photosynthesis, the process plants use to capture energy from sunlight and convert carbon dioxide and water into energy-rich sugars. These sugars provide chemical energy and material the plant can use for growth and other biological functions.

Chlorophyll absorbs light particularly well in the red and blue portions of the visible spectrum. It reflects and transmits more green light, which is why leaves generally look green to us.

During the growing season, chlorophyll is continually produced and replaced. Its strong green color masks many of the other pigments inside the leaf.

What Changes in the Fall?

The most reliable signal that autumn is approaching is not cold weather. It is the shortening length of daylight.

As nights become longer, trees respond to seasonal changes by beginning the process that will eventually allow them to shed their leaves.

Chlorophyll production slows and eventually stops. Existing chlorophyll molecules break down, and the tree recovers some of their useful nutrients before the leaf is discarded.

As the amount of chlorophyll decreases, the green color fades.

Pigments that had been hidden by the chlorophyll can then become much easier to see.

Where Do Yellow and Orange Colors Come From?

Yellow and orange colors are produced mainly by pigments called carotenoids.

Carotenoids are present in many leaves throughout the growing season. They help plants interact with light and protect the photosynthetic system from excess energy.

Because green chlorophyll is so abundant during spring and summer, carotenoid colors are usually overwhelmed by it.

When chlorophyll disappears in autumn, the yellow and orange pigments become visible.

Carotenoids are also responsible for familiar colors in many foods, including the orange of carrots and yellow tones found in corn and other plants.

Where Do Red and Purple Leaves Come From?

Red and purple autumn colors often involve pigments called anthocyanins.

Unlike carotenoids, which are generally already present in leaves, anthocyanins can be produced in significant amounts in some leaves during autumn.

As a tree begins shutting down a leaf, sugars can become trapped within it. Under certain conditions, chemical processes involving those sugars promote anthocyanin production.

Anthocyanins can produce brilliant reds, deep crimson colors and purplish shades.

Different tree species produce different amounts of these pigments. This is why some species are known for intense red foliage while others primarily turn yellow or brown.

Why Do Some Leaves Turn Brown?

Brown colors become prominent as leaf tissues die and pigments break down.

Compounds called tannins and other remaining materials contribute to the brown color of dead or dying leaves.

Some trees produce relatively little bright yellow, orange or red coloration and transition fairly quickly from green to brown.

Brown is also what often remains after the brighter autumn pigments have degraded.

Why Do Trees Drop Their Leaves?

Leaves are valuable during the growing season, but they can become a liability during winter in climates where water may freeze and sunlight is limited.

Broad leaves lose water through evaporation and require resources to maintain. Frozen soil can make it difficult for roots to replace that lost water. Leaves can also collect snow and ice, increasing stress on branches.

As autumn progresses, deciduous trees form a specialized layer of cells near the base of each leaf stem called the abscission layer.

This layer gradually restricts movement of water and nutrients between the leaf and the tree. Eventually the connection weakens enough that wind or gravity causes the leaf to fall.

The tree seals the remaining attachment point, helping protect itself from water loss and infection.

Dropping leaves allows the tree to conserve resources until favorable growing conditions return.

Why Don't Evergreen Trees Lose All Their Leaves?

Evergreens have evolved a different strategy.

Needles and other evergreen leaves are generally smaller, tougher and better protected against water loss and freezing conditions than the broad leaves of deciduous trees. Many have waxy coatings and other adaptations that allow them to survive for several years.

Evergreens still lose leaves or needles. They simply replace them gradually rather than dropping nearly all of them during one season.

Some needle-bearing trees are exceptions. Larches, for example, are deciduous conifers whose needles change color and fall each year.

Why Are Fall Colors Better Some Years Than Others?

Day length controls much of the seasonal timing, but weather can strongly influence the intensity and duration of autumn color.

Temperature, sunlight, rainfall and soil moisture all affect leaves and their pigments.

Warm, sunny days combined with cool nights that remain above freezing can favor strong red coloration in species that produce anthocyanins. Sunlight allows leaves to continue producing sugars, while cool nights can slow their movement out of the leaf.

Drought can cause leaves to dry out or fall prematurely. Severe frost can damage leaves and shorten the display. Strong wind and heavy rain can knock colorful leaves from trees before the normal autumn season is finished.

There is therefore no simple formula guaranteeing spectacular foliage in a particular year.

Does Cold Weather Cause Leaves to Change Color?

Cold temperatures influence fall foliage, but they are not the primary seasonal clock.

Shortening daylight provides trees with a predictable signal that winter is approaching. Temperature and other environmental conditions then affect how quickly the process develops and how vivid the colors become.

This explains why autumn changes can begin even during periods of relatively warm weather.

An early hard freeze can actually reduce the quality of fall color by damaging leaf tissues rather than creating brighter colors.

Why Do Different Trees Change at Different Times?

Tree species respond differently to changes in daylight and temperature.

Their genetics determine when they begin preparing for winter, which pigments they contain and how readily they produce anthocyanins.

Individual trees of the same species can also behave differently because of their age, health, access to water, exposure to sunlight and local growing conditions.

Even different branches on the same tree may change color at slightly different times if they receive different amounts of sunlight.

This staggered timing helps create the mixture of green, yellow, orange, red and brown commonly seen during the transition into autumn.

Why Are Fall Colors Different in Different Regions?

The intensity and variety of fall foliage depend partly on which tree species grow in a region.

Forests containing a diverse mixture of deciduous species can produce many colors at once because different trees contain and produce different pigments.

Climate also matters. Regions with pronounced seasonal changes in daylight and temperature often produce widespread autumn foliage, while trees growing in different climates may follow different seasonal patterns.

Elevation can affect timing as well. Higher and cooler locations may begin changing earlier than nearby lower elevations.

Do Leaves Change Color Because They Are Dying?

Partly, but that description misses much of what is happening.

The leaf is approaching the end of its useful life, but the tree is actively managing the process rather than simply allowing the leaf to die unexpectedly.

Before dropping the leaf, the tree breaks down chlorophyll and recovers valuable nutrients, particularly nitrogen and other materials that can be stored and reused later.

It then gradually seals the connection between the leaf and branch.

Autumn leaf color is therefore visible evidence of an organized seasonal process that helps deciduous trees survive winter and prepare for the next growing season.

The Bottom Line

Leaves change color in the fall as deciduous trees prepare for winter. Shorter days signal the tree to reduce chlorophyll production and begin shutting down its leaves.

As green chlorophyll breaks down, yellow and orange carotenoid pigments that were already present become visible. Some trees also produce anthocyanins, creating brilliant red and purple colors. Eventually, remaining pigments break down and brown colors become dominant.

Weather affects how vivid and long-lasting the display becomes, but the underlying process is driven largely by the seasonal change in daylight.

The colors of autumn are therefore more than a visual change in the landscape. They are part of a carefully regulated biological process that allows trees to recover nutrients, shed leaves they will not need during winter and conserve resources until growth begins again in spring.
