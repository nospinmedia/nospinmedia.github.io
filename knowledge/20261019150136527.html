What Is OPEC and How Does It Influence Oil Prices?

October 31, 202616 min read

When gasoline prices rise or fall, news reports frequently mention OPEC, a group of oil-producing countries whose decisions can influence the international petroleum market.

OPEC does not own all the world's oil, and it does not directly set the price displayed at American gas stations. However, its member countries collectively produce a substantial share of the world's crude oil and hold a large portion of its proven petroleum reserves.

By coordinating production policies, OPEC members can influence how much oil reaches international markets.

When the group reduces production, it can place upward pressure on crude oil prices. When members increase production, additional supplies can place downward pressure on prices.

The actual results depend on worldwide demand, production by countries outside the group, available inventories, and whether participating countries follow their announced commitments.

Understanding OPEC helps explain why decisions made by foreign governments can affect American fuel prices, even though the United States is one of the world's largest oil producers.

What Does OPEC Stand For?

OPEC stands for the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries.

It is an intergovernmental organization whose members are countries that produce and export petroleum.

OPEC was established in September 1960 in Baghdad, Iraq.

Its five founding members were Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela.

The organization was created to coordinate petroleum policies among participating governments and strengthen their ability to influence the terms under which their oil was produced and sold.

At the time, major international oil companies exercised considerable influence over petroleum production and pricing in many exporting countries.

OPEC's founders sought greater cooperation among producing nations and more stability in petroleum markets and government revenues.

The organization's headquarters are in Vienna, Austria.

Which Countries Belong to OPEC?

OPEC membership has changed over time as countries have joined, withdrawn, or suspended their participation.

Its membership has included major producers in the Middle East, Africa, and South America.

As of the mid-2020s, members included Algeria, the Republic of the Congo, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Libya, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Venezuela.

Not every major oil-producing country belongs to OPEC.

The United States, Canada, China, Brazil, and Norway are examples of significant producers outside the organization.

Russia is also not an OPEC member, although it cooperates with OPEC through a separate arrangement known as OPEC+.

Membership matters because OPEC's formal decisions apply to participating countries under the organization's arrangements, not automatically to every oil producer worldwide.

Why Was OPEC Created?

Oil-producing countries depend on petroleum exports for varying portions of their government revenues and national economies.

Large changes in oil prices can therefore affect government budgets, public spending, investment, and economic stability.

Before OPEC was established, individual producing countries often had less influence over international petroleum pricing arrangements than the major companies operating in their territories.

By coordinating policies, member governments sought to improve their collective position and reduce instability in the market.

Over time, OPEC became an important forum for discussing production levels and international oil market conditions.

Its members do not necessarily have identical interests. Some countries depend more heavily on oil revenues, while others have different production costs, reserve levels, investment needs, and economic priorities.

The organization must therefore balance differing national circumstances when developing collective policies.

How Does OPEC Influence Oil Prices?

OPEC's principal influence comes from its ability to coordinate the supply of crude oil.

Oil prices are determined through interactions between buyers and sellers in international markets.

When demand remains relatively stable, reducing the amount of oil available can increase competition among buyers and place upward pressure on prices.

Increasing supply can have the opposite effect.

Consider a simplified example.

Suppose the world consumes approximately 100 million barrels of petroleum and other liquid fuels per day.

If major producers collectively reduce their output by 2 million barrels per day, the market may have less available supply than buyers previously expected.

If other producers do not replace those barrels and demand remains strong, prices may rise.

If producers later restore the 2 million barrels per day, additional supply may reduce upward price pressure.

These figures are hypothetical and illustrate the mechanism rather than a specific OPEC decision.

The price effect depends on the size of the change relative to global supply and demand, the amount of oil already in storage, and expectations about future production.

Does OPEC Set the Price of Oil?

No. OPEC does not generally announce a single mandatory selling price that controls every crude oil transaction worldwide.

Crude oil prices emerge from international markets, including physical supply contracts and financial trading.

Important benchmarks include Brent crude oil and West Texas Intermediate, commonly called WTI.

OPEC members can influence these prices by changing actual or expected production, but they cannot independently determine the final market price.

For example, an OPEC production cut may be outweighed by weaker global demand or increased production from nonmember countries.

Similarly, prices may rise despite an OPEC production increase if an unexpected disruption removes supplies elsewhere.

OPEC's influence is significant, but it operates within a much larger global market.

What Are OPEC Production Quotas?

OPEC has used production targets and quotas to coordinate how much crude oil participating countries are expected to produce.

A production target specifies an intended level of output for a country or group of countries.

When participating producers agree to reduce output, the arrangement is commonly described as a production cut.

When they agree to increase output, the arrangement may involve raising production targets or gradually reversing earlier cuts.

However, production policies are not always applied identically to every member.

Some countries may receive exemptions or operate under different arrangements because of sanctions, political instability, production disruptions, or other circumstances.

The announced target is also different from actual production.

A country may produce less than its target because of infrastructure problems or limited capacity. Another may exceed its commitment.

For this reason, analysts compare announced production policies with actual oil output when assessing OPEC's effect on supply.

What Is OPEC+?

OPEC+ is a broader cooperation arrangement involving OPEC members and several oil-producing countries that are not members of the organization.

The arrangement developed in 2016, when OPEC and participating non-OPEC producers began coordinating production adjustments through the Declaration of Cooperation.

Russia is one of the most prominent non-OPEC participants.

Other participating producers have included Kazakhstan, Oman, Azerbaijan, Bahrain, Brunei, Malaysia, Sudan, and South Sudan.

OPEC+ is not the same as OPEC membership.

Countries participating through OPEC+ do not automatically become full members of OPEC or acquire the same organizational status.

The broader arrangement allows a larger group of producers to coordinate policies affecting international oil supplies.

Because the participating countries collectively account for a substantial portion of world production, their decisions can attract considerable attention in petroleum markets.

Why Does OPEC+ Matter More Than OPEC Alone?

The addition of major non-OPEC producers increases the amount of oil potentially covered by coordinated production decisions.

Russia, for example, is a significant oil producer and exporter.

If OPEC members reduce output while major nonmember producers increase their own production, some of the intended supply reduction may be offset.

Cooperation with non-OPEC countries can reduce that possibility by bringing more producers into the arrangement.

However, a broader group also creates coordination challenges.

Participating countries have different economic needs, production costs, political relationships, and domestic energy policies.

Their ability and willingness to follow common targets can vary.

OPEC+ announcements therefore need to be evaluated alongside actual production data rather than treated as guarantees about future supply.

Why Would Oil-Producing Countries Cut Production?

One reason is to respond to an expected or existing imbalance between supply and demand.

If oil production exceeds consumption for an extended period, inventories may increase and prices may decline.

Lower prices can reduce revenues for producing countries.

By reducing output, participating producers may seek to limit excess supply and support a more balanced market.

Production cuts can also reflect concerns about future demand, economic conditions, or the amount of oil already in storage.

However, cutting production has tradeoffs.

A country that produces fewer barrels may receive a higher price for each barrel sold, but it also sells less oil.

Whether total revenue increases depends on how prices respond and how much production is reduced.

Higher prices may also encourage competing producers outside OPEC to increase their output over time.

These competing incentives make production policy a complex economic decision.

Why Would OPEC Increase Production?

OPEC or OPEC+ participants may increase production when demand is growing, inventories are declining, or previously imposed restrictions are being relaxed.

Additional production can help meet rising consumption and reduce the risk of shortages.

Countries may also seek to increase export revenues by selling more barrels or to maintain their share of the international oil market.

But increasing production can place downward pressure on prices if supply grows faster than demand.

That means producers must consider both the number of barrels they sell and the price they receive.

A larger production volume does not automatically result in greater total revenue.

What Is Spare Oil Production Capacity?

Spare production capacity is the amount of additional crude oil a producer can bring to market within a defined period and sustain for a specified duration.

It provides flexibility when global supplies are unexpectedly disrupted.

For example, if a major oil-producing region experiences an interruption, countries with available spare capacity may be able to increase output and replace some of the lost supply.

Saudi Arabia has historically been an important holder of spare production capacity, although available amounts change with production levels, investment, maintenance, and other conditions.

Not every OPEC country can rapidly increase production.

Some already operate near their technical limits, while others face aging infrastructure, investment constraints, or political disruptions.

The amount of usable spare capacity matters because it affects how easily the market can respond to an unexpected shortage.

When spare capacity is limited, a disruption may create greater upward pressure on prices.

What Happens if OPEC Members Ignore Their Production Targets?

OPEC and OPEC+ rely on cooperation among sovereign governments.

Their production arrangements do not function like a single company directing all its facilities from one headquarters.

Individual countries retain control over their petroleum industries and national production decisions.

Some participants may produce above agreed levels, while others may produce less than their targets.

Compliance can be influenced by economic pressures, production capacity, technical problems, and political circumstances.

The group may discuss compliance, request adjustments, or establish plans for compensating for previous overproduction.

However, the effectiveness of coordinated policy ultimately depends on what countries actually produce and export.

If announced cuts are not fully implemented, the reduction in global supply may be smaller than markets initially expected.

Can OPEC Control American Oil Production?

No. The United States is not an OPEC member, and OPEC does not set production quotas for American oil companies.

American producers make investment and production decisions within the framework of U.S. laws, regulations, contracts, and market conditions.

However, OPEC decisions can indirectly influence those choices through international prices.

When oil prices rise, some American production projects may become more profitable, encouraging additional drilling and investment.

When prices fall, producers may reduce investment or postpone projects that are no longer economically attractive.

These responses are not immediate. Developing wells and infrastructure takes time, and companies consider expected long-term prices rather than only daily market changes.

OPEC cannot directly order American companies to increase or decrease output, but its actions can influence the economic environment in which they operate.

Why Do OPEC Decisions Affect American Gasoline Prices?

Crude oil is the primary raw material used to manufacture gasoline.

When international crude oil prices rise, American refineries generally face higher costs for the oil they process.

Those costs influence wholesale gasoline prices and eventually retail prices at gas stations.

The United States produces substantial quantities of crude oil, but American petroleum markets remain connected to international trade.

Domestic producers can sell oil to buyers at prices influenced by global supply and demand.

Consequently, an OPEC production cut may contribute to higher American gasoline prices even if the United States does not import additional oil from OPEC countries.

The effect is indirect and not always proportional.

Refinery outages, seasonal gasoline demand, fuel specifications, taxes, transportation costs, and retail competition also influence the final pump price.

OPEC decisions are one factor in gasoline pricing, not the only factor.

Does OPEC Affect Diesel and Heating Oil Too?

Yes. Crude oil is also used to produce diesel, heating oil, jet fuel, and other petroleum products.

Changes in crude oil costs can therefore influence several types of fuel.

However, the price of each refined product depends on its own supply and demand conditions.

Diesel demand is strongly connected to trucking, agriculture, construction, and industrial activity.

Heating oil demand can increase during cold weather, particularly in regions where oil-fired heating systems are common.

These factors may cause diesel and heating oil prices to move differently from gasoline, even when all three are affected by the same international crude oil market.

Can OPEC Prevent Oil Prices From Falling?

Not always.

OPEC can attempt to influence supply, but it cannot guarantee a particular price outcome.

If global demand weakens significantly, reducing production may not be enough to prevent prices from declining.

Economic recessions, improvements in energy efficiency, changes in transportation demand, and increased production outside OPEC can all affect petroleum markets.

OPEC members must also consider whether restricting their own production would allow competitors to gain market share.

The organization has considerable influence because of the scale of its members' production and reserves, but it does not have unlimited control over world prices.

Why Do Oil Prices Sometimes Change Before an OPEC Meeting?

Oil markets respond to expectations about future supply and demand.

Before a scheduled OPEC or OPEC+ meeting, traders and petroleum buyers may anticipate whether producers will announce cuts, increases, or extensions of existing policies.

If market participants expect a significant production reduction, prices may begin rising before the decision is formally announced.

If the eventual announcement matches expectations, the immediate price reaction may be relatively small.

If the decision differs substantially from expectations, prices may move more sharply.

This is why oil prices can change in response to reports, forecasts, and announcements even before the physical supply of crude oil changes.

The actual market response also depends on other developments occurring at the same time.

What Role Did OPEC Play in the 1970s Oil Crisis?

The oil crises of the 1970s demonstrated the importance of petroleum-producing countries in the international economy.

During the 1973–1974 crisis, certain Arab oil-producing countries imposed an oil embargo and production restrictions in response to events surrounding the Arab-Israeli War.

The embargo was carried out by Arab oil-exporting countries through arrangements associated with the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries, rather than by all OPEC members acting as one organization.

This distinction matters because OPEC and the Arab oil-exporting countries involved in the embargo were not identical groups.

The crisis contributed to major increases in oil prices and exposed the vulnerability of countries dependent on imported petroleum.

It also encouraged governments, including the United States, to develop policies intended to improve energy security.

The American Strategic Petroleum Reserve was established in 1975 partly in response to the supply disruptions experienced during that period.

Is OPEC a Cartel?

OPEC is often described as a cartel because its members coordinate production policies that can influence market supply and prices.

In economics, a cartel generally refers to a group of producers that cooperate to influence prices or output rather than acting entirely independently.

However, OPEC is an intergovernmental organization made up of sovereign states, not a conventional association of private companies.

Its members coordinate petroleum policies through governmental agreements and decisions.

The legal treatment of such arrangements differs from the treatment of private companies under domestic competition laws.

OPEC's ability to act collectively also differs from that of a single corporation because its members retain separate national interests and production capabilities.

Describing OPEC as a cartel explains an important aspect of its economic behavior, but it does not mean the organization can perfectly control supply or prices.

Why Don't Oil-Producing Countries Always Agree?

OPEC members have different economic circumstances.

Some countries have large financial reserves and may be better able to tolerate periods of lower oil revenue.

Others depend heavily on petroleum exports to finance government operations and may prefer higher production volumes or higher prices.

Production costs, infrastructure, population needs, and investment priorities also vary.

Countries with substantial spare capacity may have different interests from those unable to increase production quickly.

Members may also disagree about how production restrictions should be distributed.

These differences can complicate negotiations and make collective decisions difficult.

OPEC's policies therefore reflect compromises among countries rather than the preferences of a single producer.

How Does OPEC Compare With the United States as an Oil Producer?

The United States is a major crude oil producer, but its production system differs from that of many OPEC countries.

American oil production is largely carried out by commercial companies making decisions based on investment returns and market conditions.

In many OPEC countries, national oil companies and governments play a more direct role in production policy.

This allows some OPEC governments to coordinate output through national decisions, although technical and financial constraints still apply.

American production can respond to price incentives, but it is not ordinarily managed through a national crude oil production quota designed to influence global prices.

The distinction helps explain why OPEC can announce coordinated output adjustments while changes in American production usually emerge through decisions made by numerous individual companies.

What Should Consumers Watch When OPEC Announces a Production Change?

An announced production cut or increase is only the starting point for understanding its potential effect.

The most important questions include how much production is expected to change, when the adjustment will occur, and whether participating countries can and will implement it.

It also matters whether the change was already expected by international markets.

Global petroleum demand, inventories, spare production capacity, and output from nonparticipating countries can all affect the result.

For American drivers, wholesale gasoline prices and refinery conditions provide additional information about whether changes in crude oil markets are reaching retail fuel prices.

A headline announcing an OPEC production cut does not establish exactly how much gasoline prices will rise, or whether they will rise at all.

The Bottom Line

OPEC is an organization of oil-producing countries established in 1960 to coordinate petroleum policies among its members.

Its influence comes primarily from the large amount of crude oil its members produce and their ability to coordinate production decisions.

Through OPEC and the broader OPEC+ arrangement, participating countries can agree to reduce or increase oil output, affecting the amount of petroleum available to international markets.

Those changes can influence crude oil prices and, indirectly, the cost of gasoline, diesel, heating oil, and other petroleum products in the United States.

However, OPEC does not directly set American gasoline prices and cannot guarantee a particular international oil price.

Worldwide demand, production outside the group, available inventories, spare capacity, and compliance with announced targets all influence the outcome.

OPEC is an important participant in the global petroleum market, but oil prices ultimately reflect the combined actions of producers and consumers around the world.
