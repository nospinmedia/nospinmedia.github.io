Why Is Diesel Sometimes More Expensive Than Gasoline?

October 30, 202614 min read

Gasoline and diesel fuel are both made from crude oil, yet diesel frequently costs more per gallon at American fuel stations.

That can seem surprising. Diesel engines are often more fuel-efficient than gasoline engines, and diesel fuel was once commonly associated with lower prices at the pump.

But diesel and gasoline are different products with different markets, production requirements, and patterns of demand.

Gasoline is primarily used in passenger vehicles, while diesel powers much of the equipment responsible for transporting goods, producing food, constructing buildings, and operating industrial machinery.

Diesel also shares important characteristics with heating oil, creating additional demand during colder months.

The difference in price is not caused by one factor. Refinery operations, seasonal demand, fuel specifications, taxes, transportation costs, and international trade all contribute.

Understanding these differences helps explain why diesel can remain expensive even when gasoline prices are falling.

How Are Gasoline and Diesel Made?

Both gasoline and diesel are manufactured from crude oil at petroleum refineries.

Crude oil contains a mixture of hydrocarbons with different molecular sizes and properties.

Refineries separate and process these hydrocarbons to produce transportation fuels and other petroleum products.

One important refining process is fractional distillation.

Crude oil is heated, and its components are separated according to their boiling ranges.

Gasoline generally consists of lighter hydrocarbons, while diesel is made from somewhat heavier hydrocarbons that boil at higher temperatures.

However, modern refineries do much more than simply separate crude oil into its natural components.

They use cracking, hydrotreating, blending, and other processes to produce fuels that meet required specifications.

The amount of gasoline and diesel a refinery produces depends on the type of crude oil it processes, its equipment, operating conditions, and market demand.

Why Doesn't Diesel Cost Less if It Is a Heavier Fuel?

A fuel's position in the refining process does not determine its selling price.

The market price depends on how much of the product is available and how strongly customers need it.

Diesel is a valuable transportation and industrial fuel with substantial demand.

It also must meet strict quality and emissions requirements.

Refineries may need additional processing to produce diesel that satisfies those specifications.

Consequently, diesel is not automatically cheaper to manufacture simply because it contains heavier hydrocarbons than gasoline.

A refinery can also earn different margins on gasoline and diesel depending on market conditions.

When diesel demand is strong relative to available supplies, diesel prices can rise above gasoline prices.

What Makes Diesel Demand Different From Gasoline Demand?

Gasoline demand in the United States is closely associated with passenger cars, light trucks, and personal travel.

Diesel demand is more heavily connected to commercial and industrial activity.

Large trucks transport groceries, construction materials, manufactured goods, and other products across the country.

Diesel engines also power agricultural equipment, construction machinery, locomotives, some buses, commercial vessels, and backup generators.

These uses create a different pattern of demand.

A household may reduce discretionary driving when gasoline prices rise.

A trucking company delivering essential goods may have less flexibility to reduce fuel consumption immediately.

Similarly, farmers must often operate equipment during planting and harvesting seasons regardless of short-term changes in diesel prices.

This does not mean diesel demand never responds to prices. Businesses can change routes, improve efficiency, adjust operations, or pass costs along over time.

But some diesel consumption is closely tied to activities that cannot easily be postponed.

That can contribute to strong demand even when prices increase.

Why Does Heating Oil Affect Diesel Prices?

Heating oil and diesel are closely related petroleum products.

Both belong to a group commonly called middle distillates, which are produced from similar portions of crude oil.

In the United States, the heating oil used in many residential oil-burning systems is chemically similar to diesel fuel, although applicable specifications, additives, and legal uses can differ.

Because refineries use related production processes to manufacture these fuels, demand for heating oil can affect the broader market for distillate products.

During colder months, homes and businesses in regions that rely on oil heating may consume more heating oil.

That additional demand can tighten distillate supplies, particularly if inventories are already low.

As a result, diesel prices may increase during periods of strong heating demand.

This relationship is especially relevant in parts of the northeastern United States, where heating oil remains an important residential heating fuel.

However, winter weather does not always cause diesel prices to rise. Inventory levels, refinery production, imports, and overall economic conditions also matter.

Is Home Heating Oil the Same as Diesel Fuel?

They are closely related, but they are not necessarily interchangeable in every application.

No. 2 heating oil and No. 2 diesel are both middle-distillate petroleum products with broadly similar physical characteristics.

However, on-road diesel must meet specific federal fuel-quality and emissions requirements, including limits on sulfur content.

Heating oil specifications can differ depending on state requirements and supplier practices.

Some heating oil sold in certain states is also ultra-low sulfur, making its sulfur content similar to modern highway diesel.

Fuel additives, dye, taxation, and equipment requirements may still differ.

On-road diesel is generally taxed for highway use. Heating oil and certain off-road diesel fuels are not subject to the same highway fuel taxes and may be dyed to identify their tax status.

Using dyed, untaxed fuel in a vehicle operated on public roads can violate fuel tax laws unless a specific exemption applies.

Likewise, using a fuel that does not meet an engine or heating system's requirements can cause operational or warranty problems.

The fact that the fuels are chemically similar does not mean they should be substituted without checking the applicable specifications and legal requirements.

Why Does Diesel Sometimes Rise During Winter?

Winter can affect diesel prices through several channels.

First, heating oil demand may increase when temperatures fall.

Second, winter diesel formulations may require changes to improve cold-weather performance.

Diesel contains hydrocarbons that can form wax crystals at low temperatures. If the fuel becomes too cold, those crystals can interfere with fuel flow and clog filters.

Suppliers may blend No. 1 diesel or other suitable components with No. 2 diesel, or use approved additives, to improve cold-weather operability.

No. 1 diesel generally has better low-temperature characteristics but can be more expensive or have lower energy content per gallon than No. 2 diesel.

The availability and cost of winter blending components can influence retail prices.

Third, severe weather can disrupt transportation and fuel deliveries.

Snowstorms, freezing conditions, and other weather events may interfere with refinery operations, terminals, or distribution systems.

These factors can contribute to higher winter diesel prices, although their effects vary by region and year.

Why Can Gasoline Prices Fall While Diesel Prices Stay High?

Gasoline and diesel are sold in related but distinct markets.

Their prices are influenced by the same crude oil market, but their supply and demand conditions can differ.

Suppose gasoline demand declines after the summer driving season.

Refineries and distributors may have adequate gasoline inventories, allowing gasoline prices to fall.

At the same time, demand for diesel may remain strong because of freight transportation, agriculture, industrial activity, or heating oil consumption.

If diesel inventories are relatively low, diesel prices may remain elevated even while gasoline becomes cheaper.

Refineries cannot always increase diesel production immediately without affecting production of other petroleum products.

The result can be a widening price difference between diesel and gasoline.

This is why comparing crude oil prices alone cannot fully explain what happens to either fuel at the pump.

Can Refineries Simply Make More Diesel?

Refineries can adjust their operations to some extent, but they cannot freely choose any combination of gasoline and diesel output.

The amount of each product depends on refinery equipment, crude oil characteristics, processing capacity, and operating constraints.

For example, a refinery may use cracking units to convert heavier petroleum components into lighter products.

Other equipment may be used to increase production of diesel and related distillates.

However, changing the production mix can require adjustments to operating conditions and may reduce the output of other valuable products.

Some refineries are better equipped than others to maximize diesel production.

Refiners generally consider the relative market prices of gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and other products when deciding how to operate their facilities.

If diesel prices rise substantially, refineries may have an incentive to produce more diesel where technically and economically practical.

But these adjustments have limits, especially when refineries are already operating near capacity.

What Is Ultra-Low Sulfur Diesel?

Ultra-low sulfur diesel, commonly called ULSD, is diesel fuel containing no more than 15 parts per million of sulfur.

Federal requirements introduced ULSD for highway diesel fuel to support modern emissions-control systems.

Reducing sulfur allows technologies such as diesel particulate filters and other exhaust-treatment equipment to operate effectively.

Producing ULSD requires refineries to remove sulfur compounds from petroleum streams.

A common process is hydrotreating, which uses hydrogen and catalysts under controlled conditions to reduce sulfur and other impurities.

This processing requires equipment, energy, hydrogen, and operating expenses.

Those costs contribute to the economics of diesel production.

However, ULSD requirements alone do not explain every price difference between diesel and gasoline.

Market supply, demand, taxes, and refinery margins can be more important at particular times.

Are Diesel Taxes Higher Than Gasoline Taxes?

In the United States, the federal excise tax on highway diesel is higher than the federal tax on gasoline.

The federal gasoline tax is 18.4 cents per gallon, while the federal highway diesel tax is 24.4 cents per gallon.

These figures include the applicable federal underground storage tank financing component.

That creates a six-cent-per-gallon difference at the federal level.

State taxes and fees can add substantially more to retail fuel prices.

Some states tax diesel and gasoline differently, while others apply similar rates or use tax structures that change over time.

Local taxes may also apply.

Taxes therefore help explain part of the difference between diesel and gasoline prices, but they usually do not account for large gaps of several dozen cents or more by themselves.

The larger differences often reflect wholesale fuel markets and refining conditions.

Why Do International Markets Affect American Diesel Prices?

Diesel is traded internationally, just as crude oil and gasoline are.

Demand for diesel and related distillate fuels in Europe, Asia, and other regions can influence the prices American buyers and sellers face.

For example, if a major overseas supplier of diesel becomes unavailable, customers may seek replacement fuel from refineries in other countries.

That can increase international competition for diesel supplies.

American refiners may receive higher prices for exports, while domestic buyers face the effects of a tighter global market.

Shipping costs, trade restrictions, refinery outages, and geopolitical events can all influence international distillate markets.

The United States produces substantial quantities of diesel, but domestic production does not completely isolate American consumers from international price changes.

How Do Wars and Sanctions Affect Diesel Prices?

Wars and international sanctions can disrupt crude oil production, refining, transportation, or trade in finished petroleum products.

Diesel prices may be especially sensitive when a disruption affects a region that supplies large quantities of middle-distillate fuels.

A conflict can also increase shipping insurance costs or force tankers to use longer routes.

These changes can raise the delivered cost of diesel even when the amount of crude oil available worldwide has not fallen substantially.

Trade restrictions can alter where diesel is bought and sold, requiring importers to find alternative suppliers.

Those adjustments may increase transportation costs and create temporary regional shortages.

The effect on American diesel prices depends on the size of the disruption, available replacement supplies, inventories, and international demand.

What Are Diesel Inventories and Why Do They Matter?

Inventories are the quantities of fuel stored at refineries, terminals, pipelines, and other facilities before being consumed.

The United States tracks distillate fuel oil inventories, which include diesel and related products.

Inventories provide a buffer when production or deliveries are interrupted.

When inventories are relatively high, buyers may have more flexibility to respond to unexpected increases in demand.

When inventories are low, a refinery outage, transportation disruption, or sudden demand increase can have a larger effect on prices.

For example, if distillate inventories are already tight before winter, a period of unusually cold weather may place additional pressure on supplies.

Prices may rise as buyers compete for available fuel.

Inventory levels are therefore an important indicator of diesel market conditions, although they must be considered alongside demand and production.

Why Does Diesel Cost More in Some States Than Others?

Diesel prices vary geographically because of taxes, transportation costs, local demand, and fuel availability.

States farther from major refining centers may face higher costs to transport diesel to distribution terminals and retail stations.

Some regions rely more heavily on pipelines, while others depend on marine shipments, rail, or trucks.

Different state tax structures also affect retail prices.

Local competition matters as well.

A truck stop serving a busy freight corridor may have different operating costs and pricing strategies than a small rural fuel station.

In colder regions, suppliers may also need to provide diesel with enhanced winter performance.

These factors explain why the price difference between diesel and gasoline can vary significantly from one location to another.

Why Does Diesel Pricing Matter to People Who Don't Drive Diesel Vehicles?

Diesel is used throughout the economy, so higher diesel prices can affect consumers who never purchase it directly.

Trucks transport food, clothing, building materials, medical supplies, and many other products.

Farm equipment uses diesel to plant, cultivate, and harvest crops.

Construction machinery and industrial equipment also consume substantial quantities.

When diesel prices rise, transportation and production costs may increase.

Businesses may absorb some of those costs, improve efficiency, or pass part of the increase along through higher prices for goods and services.

The effect depends on how much diesel contributes to a product's total cost and how competitive the relevant market is.

Diesel prices are therefore relevant not only to drivers but also to inflation, business expenses, and household budgets.

Is Diesel More Energy-Dense Than Gasoline?

Yes. Diesel generally contains more energy per gallon than gasoline.

A gallon of diesel typically contains roughly 10 to 15 percent more energy than a gallon of gasoline, depending on the fuel formulations being compared.

Diesel engines also often operate more efficiently than comparable gasoline engines because of differences in engine design and combustion characteristics.

These advantages help explain why diesel is widely used in heavy trucks, commercial equipment, and applications requiring substantial torque and fuel efficiency.

However, greater energy content does not guarantee a lower retail price per gallon.

The market price of diesel depends on supply, demand, processing costs, taxes, and transportation.

For some vehicle operators, the more useful comparison is fuel cost per mile or per hour of operation rather than the price of a single gallon.

Has Diesel Always Been More Expensive Than Gasoline?

No.

The relationship has changed over time.

Diesel was often less expensive than regular gasoline in earlier periods of the American fuel market.

Changes in fuel specifications, refining economics, global distillate demand, and taxation contributed to a shift in that relationship.

The introduction of ultra-low sulfur diesel requirements increased the importance of additional refining processes.

International demand for diesel and related fuels has also influenced prices.

However, diesel does not always cost more than gasoline in every location or during every period.

Market conditions can temporarily narrow, eliminate, or reverse the price difference.

The important point is that the two fuels have distinct supply and demand characteristics, so their prices need not move together.

Could Diesel Become Cheaper Than Gasoline Again?

Yes. There is no permanent economic rule requiring diesel to cost more than gasoline.

If diesel supplies become plentiful relative to demand, wholesale diesel prices may decline.

At the same time, strong gasoline demand or refinery disruptions affecting gasoline production could push gasoline prices higher.

The price difference also depends on crude oil costs, fuel taxes, transportation expenses, and seasonal conditions.

A narrowing or reversal of the price gap is possible when those factors change.

However, the timing and magnitude of future price movements cannot be determined from fuel type alone.

The Bottom Line

Diesel sometimes costs more than gasoline because the two fuels serve different markets and face different production and distribution conditions.

Diesel demand is closely connected to trucking, agriculture, construction, and industrial activity. It also shares a market relationship with heating oil, which can increase demand for distillate fuels during winter.

Refineries must produce diesel that meets strict specifications, including ultra-low sulfur requirements for highway use. Their ability to increase diesel output is limited by equipment and operating conditions.

Higher federal diesel taxes, transportation costs, international demand, and regional supply constraints can also contribute to the price difference.

Although diesel and gasoline both originate from crude oil, their prices do not have to move together.

The price at the pump reflects not just the cost of oil, but the balance between how much of each fuel is available and how strongly consumers and businesses need it.
