How Does Wi-Fi Work?

September 28, 202614 min read

Wi-Fi allows computers, phones, televisions and other devices to communicate over a local network using radio waves instead of Ethernet cables.

A Wi-Fi device converts digital data into radio signals and transmits them through an antenna. A nearby Wi-Fi access point or another compatible device receives those signals, converts them back into digital data and sends information in the opposite direction when necessary.

Wi-Fi is therefore essentially a wireless networking technology. It can provide access to the internet, but Wi-Fi and the internet are not the same thing. A Wi-Fi network can continue operating between local devices even when its internet connection is unavailable.

What Is Wi-Fi?

Wi-Fi is a family of wireless networking technologies based on the IEEE 802.11 standards.

These standards define how compatible wireless devices transmit, receive and organize information over radio connections.

Different generations of Wi-Fi have introduced improvements in speed, capacity, efficiency and use of the radio spectrum. Names such as Wi-Fi 4, Wi-Fi 5, Wi-Fi 6, Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 refer to different generations or extensions of this technology.

Despite their differences, they share the same basic purpose: allowing devices to exchange network data wirelessly.

What Does a Wi-Fi Access Point Do?

The central device in most Wi-Fi networks is an access point, commonly abbreviated AP.

The access point creates a wireless connection point for nearby devices. Phones, laptops and other Wi-Fi devices associate with the AP and use it to communicate with the rest of the network.

In many homes, the access point is built into the device people call their Wi-Fi router. That single box may actually perform several separate jobs: routing traffic, providing Ethernet connections, acting as a firewall and operating one or more Wi-Fi access points.

In businesses, schools and other larger networks, access points are commonly separate devices connected to a larger wired network.

The access point itself does not have to be the router.

How Does a Device Find a Wi-Fi Network?

Wi-Fi access points periodically transmit management information that helps nearby devices discover available networks.

One important transmission is called a beacon frame. Beacon frames contain information about the wireless network and its capabilities.

The familiar network name displayed on a phone or computer is called the SSID, or Service Set Identifier.

Devices can listen for these transmissions and build a list of nearby Wi-Fi networks. They can also actively send requests looking for networks.

When you select a network, the device and access point exchange additional management information to establish a connection.

If the network is protected, authentication and security procedures are also performed before normal network traffic is allowed.

How Does Wi-Fi Send Data?

Computers represent information digitally as bits.

A Wi-Fi radio takes digital information and encodes it onto a radio-frequency signal using modulation and other signal-processing techniques defined by the particular Wi-Fi standard.

An antenna transmits the resulting electromagnetic waves through the surrounding environment.

The receiving antenna detects a tiny portion of that radio energy. The receiver processes the signal, determines the encoded information and reconstructs the transmitted data.

This process occurs extremely quickly in both directions, allowing Wi-Fi to carry webpages, video, audio, messages, files and other network traffic.

What Frequencies Does Wi-Fi Use?

Modern Wi-Fi primarily operates in portions of three radio-frequency ranges commonly described as 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz.

Not every Wi-Fi generation or device supports all three bands.

The 2.4 GHz band has been used by Wi-Fi for decades and generally provides good range and penetration through common building materials, but it has limited spectrum and is shared with many other technologies.

The 5 GHz band provides substantially more spectrum for Wi-Fi and supports wider channels, but its signals generally experience greater attenuation through distance and obstacles than lower-frequency 2.4 GHz signals under comparable conditions.

The 6 GHz band provides a large amount of additional spectrum for newer Wi-Fi technologies in countries where regulators have made it available. It can support many wide channels with less interference from older Wi-Fi equipment, although propagation losses generally increase as frequency rises.

The differences among these bands are important enough to be considered separately from the basic explanation of how Wi-Fi works.

What Is a Wi-Fi Channel?

A frequency band contains a range of radio frequencies. Wi-Fi divides the usable spectrum into channels.

An access point operates on one or more selected channels depending on its capabilities and configuration.

Nearby networks using the same or overlapping spectrum must share the available radio airtime. This is one reason Wi-Fi performance can decline in apartment buildings, offices and other areas containing many access points and wireless devices.

Modern Wi-Fi can use channels of different widths. Wider channels provide more spectrum and can support higher data rates, but they also consume a larger portion of the available frequency band.

A wider channel is therefore not automatically the best choice in every environment.

How Do Multiple Devices Share One Wi-Fi Channel?

Wi-Fi is a shared radio medium.

Several devices connected to the same access point do not normally receive their own completely independent radio frequency. They must coordinate access to the available wireless spectrum.

Traditional Wi-Fi systems use a method called carrier-sense multiple access with collision avoidance, or CSMA/CA. Before transmitting, a device generally listens to determine whether the channel appears to be busy. If another transmission is occurring, it waits before trying to transmit.

Modern Wi-Fi generations add more sophisticated techniques that allow available radio resources to be used more efficiently and, under appropriate conditions, allow an access point to communicate with multiple devices in more advanced ways.

This shared-medium behavior helps explain why the performance of one Wi-Fi device can be affected by other devices using the same channel.

What Happens When Two Wi-Fi Networks Use the Same Channel?

Wi-Fi networks operating on the same channel can often detect one another and take turns using the shared radio medium.

This does not necessarily cause the networks to stop working. It means they must compete for available airtime.

The more devices trying to use the same spectrum, the less airtime may be available to each one.

Signals on nearby or overlapping frequencies can also create interference that makes successful reception more difficult.

Good Wi-Fi design therefore involves more than simply maximizing transmitter power. Channel selection, access point placement, channel width and the number of devices all matter.

Why Does Wi-Fi Slow Down With Distance?

Radio signals become weaker as they spread through space.

When a device is close to an access point with a strong, clean signal, Wi-Fi can use more complex modulation and coding methods that carry large amounts of information efficiently.

As the signal becomes weaker or noisier, the connection may switch to more robust but slower transmission methods.

The device may also need to retransmit data that was not received correctly.

This means Wi-Fi does not normally have one fixed speed. The radio connection continually adapts to changing conditions.

A device at the edge of coverage may therefore communicate much more slowly than one located near the access point.

Why Do Walls Affect Wi-Fi?

Radio waves can pass through many building materials, but they lose energy in the process.

Different materials affect Wi-Fi signals differently. Drywall and wood may weaken a signal moderately, while concrete, brick, metal and some types of coated glass can cause much greater attenuation or reflection.

Water also absorbs radio-frequency energy. Because the human body contains a large amount of water, people themselves can affect Wi-Fi propagation in some situations.

Radio waves can also reflect from walls, floors, ceilings, furniture and other surfaces. These reflections create multiple signal paths between transmitter and receiver.

Modern Wi-Fi systems can sometimes take advantage of these multiple paths rather than treating all reflections as harmful.

What Is MIMO?

MIMO stands for Multiple Input, Multiple Output.

Instead of relying on a single transmitting and receiving antenna, MIMO systems can use multiple antennas and radio chains to improve wireless communication.

Under suitable conditions, multiple versions of a radio signal traveling along different paths can carry separate streams of information. This technique is called spatial multiplexing and can substantially increase data rates.

Multiple antennas can also be used to improve reliability and signal quality.

MIMO is one of the important technologies that allowed newer generations of Wi-Fi to achieve much higher performance than early wireless networks.

What Is Beamforming?

Beamforming uses multiple antennas and signal-processing techniques to shape how transmitted radio energy combines in space.

Instead of treating each antenna as an entirely independent transmitter, a system can adjust the timing and phase of signals from multiple antennas so they combine more favorably in particular directions or at a receiving device.

Beamforming does not create a perfectly narrow beam like a visible laser. Radio propagation indoors remains complicated because signals reflect and scatter from surrounding objects.

However, beamforming can improve signal quality and performance under suitable conditions.

Why Is the Speed on the Wi-Fi Box Higher Than the Speed You Actually Get?

Wi-Fi products are often advertised using maximum theoretical physical-layer data rates or combinations of rates supported by multiple radios.

Those figures are not the same as the amount of application data a single device will actually transfer.

Real Wi-Fi communication includes management traffic, acknowledgments, error correction, protocol overhead and time spent waiting for access to the radio channel.

Signal strength, interference, device capabilities, channel width, number of spatial streams and network congestion further affect performance.

If internet traffic is being measured, the internet connection itself can also become the limiting factor.

A router advertised with a multi-gigabit Wi-Fi rating therefore does not guarantee that every connected device will transfer files or access the internet at that speed.

Is Wi-Fi Full Duplex?

Traditional Wi-Fi communication on a particular channel is generally not full duplex in the same sense as modern switched Ethernet.

A Wi-Fi radio typically cannot transmit and receive independent traffic simultaneously on the same channel. Devices instead coordinate access to the shared wireless medium and communicate at different times.

Because this happens extremely quickly, normal applications can appear to send and receive information simultaneously from a user's perspective.

This distinction is one reason comparing a Wi-Fi link directly with a wired Ethernet connection based only on their advertised bit rates can be misleading.

How Does Wi-Fi Know Which Device Should Receive the Data?

Network devices have identifiers used to direct traffic.

At the Wi-Fi link layer, devices use MAC addresses and information contained in wireless frames to identify transmitters, receivers and other relevant network endpoints.

The access point examines these frames and bridges appropriate traffic between wireless devices and the rest of the local network.

Higher-level networking technologies such as IP then handle addressing and routing beyond the local wireless connection.

Wi-Fi therefore provides part of a larger networking system rather than replacing all the other protocols used by computers and the internet.

How Does Wi-Fi Connect to Ethernet?

An access point commonly acts as a bridge between wireless Wi-Fi devices and a wired Ethernet network.

A laptop may transmit a network packet over Wi-Fi to the access point. The access point receives the wireless frame and forwards the appropriate data onto the wired network.

Traffic returning to the laptop can travel through Ethernet to the access point and then be transmitted over Wi-Fi.

From the perspective of many applications, this transition between wired and wireless networking is largely invisible.

This is why a business can have hundreds of Wi-Fi devices connected through access points while most of the underlying network infrastructure remains wired Ethernet.

Is Wi-Fi the Same as the Internet?

No.

Wi-Fi provides a local wireless network connection. The internet is a global network connecting enormous numbers of separate networks around the world.

A device can have a strong Wi-Fi connection while having no internet access at all.

For example, if an internet service provider has an outage, a phone may remain successfully connected to the home's Wi-Fi access point. It may still communicate with local printers, servers, cameras or other devices even though websites on the internet cannot be reached.

Conversely, a phone can access the internet through a cellular network without using Wi-Fi.

The Wi-Fi symbol therefore indicates a wireless network connection, not a guarantee that the internet itself is working.

What Does the Router Do?

In a typical home network, the router connects the local network with another network, usually the internet connection supplied by an internet service provider.

The router decides where network packets should be forwarded based on their destination.

Home products commonly combine the router, Ethernet switch and Wi-Fi access point into a single enclosure, which is why people often use the words router and Wi-Fi interchangeably.

They are nevertheless different functions.

A network can use a router with no built-in Wi-Fi and separate access points connected by Ethernet. Large business networks commonly use this type of architecture.

What Is a Mesh Wi-Fi System?

A mesh Wi-Fi system uses multiple wireless nodes or access points to extend network coverage across a larger area.

Devices can connect to different nodes depending on their location and the system's design.

Some mesh systems use wireless links between nodes. This is called wireless backhaul. Others can use Ethernet connections between access points, providing wired backhaul.

Using multiple access points can improve coverage, but simply adding more transmitters does not automatically improve performance. Poor placement, excessive channel overlap and inefficient backhaul connections can create new problems.

Well-designed Wi-Fi networks coordinate coverage, channels and capacity rather than attempting to make every access point transmit as far as possible.

What Happens When You Walk Between Access Points?

In a network with multiple access points using the same network name and compatible security settings, a device can move from one access point to another. This process is commonly called roaming.

The client device plays an important role in deciding when to leave one access point and connect to another.

Network technologies can provide information and mechanisms that help devices roam more efficiently, but the access point does not simply force every client to switch at a predetermined signal strength.

A device that holds onto a distant access point for too long can experience poor performance even when a stronger access point is nearby.

How Is Wi-Fi Protected From Eavesdropping?

Modern Wi-Fi networks can use encryption and authentication systems such as WPA2 and WPA3.

Encryption transforms transmitted information so that someone receiving the radio signal cannot simply read the protected data without the necessary cryptographic keys.

Authentication helps determine whether a device is authorized to join the network.

Home networks commonly use a shared password or passphrase, while enterprise Wi-Fi can use more sophisticated identity and authentication systems.

Security depends on configuration as well as technology. Strong modern security settings, updated equipment and appropriate passwords are important because Wi-Fi signals can extend beyond the physical boundaries of a home or building.

Can Someone Receive Your Wi-Fi Signal Outside Your House?

Yes.

Radio waves do not stop automatically at property lines or exterior walls. A Wi-Fi signal may be detectable outside the building, sometimes at considerable distances depending on transmitter power, antennas, building materials and the surrounding environment.

That does not mean an outside device automatically has access to the network.

Proper authentication and encryption are intended to prevent unauthorized devices from joining or reading protected communications simply because they can receive the radio signal.

Why Does Restarting Wi-Fi Sometimes Fix a Problem?

Restarting a router or access point can clear temporary software problems, reset radio hardware, terminate stalled network sessions and cause some network functions to initialize again.

A restart may therefore solve certain temporary failures.

It does not fix every Wi-Fi problem. Poor access point placement, weak signals, interference, congested channels, damaged hardware or an internet service outage will not necessarily disappear because equipment was restarted.

Understanding whether the problem is the wireless connection, local network or internet connection can help identify the actual cause.

The Bottom Line

Wi-Fi works by encoding digital information onto radio signals and transmitting those signals between wireless devices and access points. Modern Wi-Fi primarily uses portions of the 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz and 6 GHz radio bands, depending on the equipment and Wi-Fi generation involved.

Access points connect wireless devices to a local network and often bridge their traffic onto wired Ethernet infrastructure. Devices share the available radio spectrum, so distance, obstacles, interference, channel use and competing devices all influence performance.

Technologies such as MIMO, beamforming and increasingly sophisticated methods of sharing radio resources allow modern Wi-Fi networks to carry far more data and support many more devices than early wireless networks.

Most importantly, Wi-Fi is not the internet itself. It is a method for connecting devices wirelessly to a network. That network may provide access to the internet, local devices or both. Understanding that distinction makes many common Wi-Fi problems easier to diagnose: a device can have perfectly good Wi-Fi while the internet connection beyond it is not working at all.
