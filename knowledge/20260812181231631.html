What Is a Lunar Eclipse?

September 2, 202614 min read

What Is a Lunar Eclipse?

A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, causing Earth's shadow to fall on the Moon.

Lunar eclipses occur around the time of a full moon, when the Sun, Earth, and Moon are aligned closely enough for the Moon to pass through part or all of Earth's shadow.

Depending on the alignment, the result can be a total, partial, or penumbral lunar eclipse.

Unlike a solar eclipse, a lunar eclipse is safe to watch directly without special eye protection.

What Causes a Lunar Eclipse?

A lunar eclipse occurs when the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up in this order:

Sun → Earth → Moon

Earth blocks direct sunlight from reaching some or all of the Moon.

Earth's shadow has two main regions: a dark central shadow called the umbra and a lighter outer shadow called the penumbra.

The type of lunar eclipse depends on how deeply the Moon travels through those shadows.

What Are the Types of Lunar Eclipses?

There are three main types of lunar eclipses:

  • Total lunar eclipse.
  • Partial lunar eclipse.
  • Penumbral lunar eclipse.

Each results from a different path through Earth's shadow.

What Is a Total Lunar Eclipse?

A total lunar eclipse occurs when the entire Moon moves into Earth's umbra, the darkest part of Earth's shadow.

During totality, direct sunlight is blocked from reaching the Moon. However, the Moon usually does not become completely black or disappear.

Instead, it often turns shades of red, orange, copper, or brown because some sunlight passes through Earth's atmosphere and is bent into the shadow.

The period when the entire Moon is inside the umbra is called totality.

Totality can last for more than an hour during some lunar eclipses, while the entire eclipse sequence can extend for several hours.

What Is a Partial Lunar Eclipse?

A partial lunar eclipse occurs when only part of the Moon enters Earth's umbra.

From Earth, the dark portion of Earth's shadow can appear to move gradually across the lunar surface, making it look as though a curved section of the Moon is being covered.

The remainder of the Moon stays outside the umbra and remains much brighter.

Every total lunar eclipse includes partial phases as the Moon enters and later leaves Earth's umbra.

What Is a Penumbral Lunar Eclipse?

A penumbral lunar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes only through Earth's lighter outer shadow, called the penumbra, without entering the umbra.

The effect can be subtle.

Rather than appearing to have a dark section removed from it, the Moon may simply look slightly dimmer or shaded on one side.

A weak penumbral eclipse can be difficult to notice without knowing that an eclipse is occurring.

What Are Earth's Umbra and Penumbra?

Earth casts different types of shadow into space.

The umbra is the dark central region where Earth blocks direct sunlight from reaching the Moon.

The penumbra is the lighter outer region where Earth blocks only part of the Sun from the Moon's perspective.

A Moon entirely inside the umbra produces the total phase of a total lunar eclipse. A Moon partly inside the umbra produces a partial lunar eclipse, while a Moon passing only through the penumbra produces a penumbral eclipse.

Why Does the Moon Turn Red During a Lunar Eclipse?

During a total lunar eclipse, Earth blocks direct sunlight from illuminating the Moon, but Earth's atmosphere allows some sunlight to reach it indirectly.

Sunlight contains many colors. As sunlight passes through Earth's atmosphere, shorter wavelengths such as blue light are scattered more readily. Longer red and orange wavelengths pass through the atmosphere more effectively and are refracted, or bent, into Earth's shadow.

That filtered reddish light reaches the Moon and reflects back toward Earth.

The same basic atmospheric effect helps produce red and orange colors during sunrises and sunsets.

In a sense, the reddish Moon during a total lunar eclipse is being illuminated by sunlight that has passed through the atmosphere around the edge of Earth.

What Is a Blood Moon?

Blood moon is a popular name for the reddish appearance of the Moon during a total lunar eclipse.

It is not a separate scientific type of lunar eclipse.

The Moon may appear bright orange, copper, deep red, brownish, or unusually dark during totality depending on atmospheric conditions and the geometry of the eclipse.

The term "blood moon" has become common in popular culture and news coverage, but astronomers classify the event as a total lunar eclipse.

Why Are Some Lunar Eclipses Redder or Darker Than Others?

Not every total lunar eclipse looks the same.

The amount and color of sunlight passing through Earth's atmosphere can vary depending on atmospheric conditions.

Clouds, dust, smoke, and volcanic aerosols can affect how much sunlight reaches the Moon through Earth's atmosphere.

The Moon's path through Earth's umbra also matters. A Moon passing deeply through the central portion of the umbra can appear darker than one passing closer to its edge.

As a result, one total lunar eclipse might produce a bright orange Moon while another appears deep red or unusually dark.

Astronomers sometimes describe the appearance of a total lunar eclipse using the Danjon Scale, which ranges from L=0 to L=4. An L=0 eclipse is extremely dark and can make the Moon difficult to see, while an L=4 eclipse is bright copper-red or orange. Intermediate ratings describe progressively brighter and more colorful eclipses.

The Danjon Scale is based on the Moon's observed brightness and color during totality. Atmospheric conditions, including dust and volcanic aerosols, can influence how dark an eclipse appears, so observations of eclipse brightness can provide information about the transparency of Earth's atmosphere.

Why Isn't There a Lunar Eclipse Every Full Moon?

A lunar eclipse requires a full moon, but one does not occur every month.

The Moon's orbit around Earth is tilted by about 5 degrees relative to the plane of Earth's orbit around the Sun.

During most full moons, the Moon passes slightly above or below Earth's shadow.

A lunar eclipse occurs when a full moon happens sufficiently close to one of the points where the Moon's orbit crosses Earth's orbital plane.

These crossing points are called nodes.

This is the same orbital tilt that explains why solar eclipses do not occur at every new moon.

What Is an Eclipse Season?

An eclipse season is a period when the Sun is close enough to one of the Moon's orbital nodes for eclipses to occur.

Eclipse seasons occur a little less than six months apart and last for several weeks.

During an eclipse season, the alignment can produce a solar eclipse around new moon and a lunar eclipse around full moon, depending on the exact geometry and timing.

This is why solar and lunar eclipses often occur within a few weeks of one another.

Where Can a Lunar Eclipse Be Seen?

A lunar eclipse can generally be seen from any location on Earth's nighttime side where the Moon is above the horizon during the event.

That makes lunar eclipses visible across a much larger area than total solar eclipses.

A total solar eclipse is visible only from a relatively narrow path where the Moon's shadow crosses Earth's surface. A total lunar eclipse, by comparison, can be visible simultaneously to people across large portions of continents and oceans.

Whether someone can see the entire eclipse depends on local moonrise and moonset times. In some locations, the Moon may rise after the eclipse has begun or set before it ends.

Cloud cover can also prevent an otherwise visible eclipse from being observed.

How Long Does a Lunar Eclipse Last?

A complete lunar eclipse event can unfold over several hours as the Moon gradually enters and exits Earth's penumbra and umbra.

During a total lunar eclipse, totality itself can last for more than an hour under favorable eclipse geometry.

The duration varies because the Moon does not always follow the same path through Earth's shadow.

A Moon passing near the center of the umbra generally spends more time in totality than one passing near the edge.

Is It Safe to Look at a Lunar Eclipse?

Yes.

A lunar eclipse is safe to view with the unaided eye.

Eclipse glasses are not required.

The Moon is reflecting sunlight rather than exposing observers to the intense direct light of the Sun.

A lunar eclipse can also be viewed safely through binoculars or a telescope under ordinary nighttime observing conditions.

This is an important difference between lunar and solar eclipses. Special solar filters are required for most direct viewing of a solar eclipse because observers are looking toward the Sun. Those solar-viewing precautions are not necessary for a lunar eclipse.

Can You Photograph a Lunar Eclipse?

Yes.

A lunar eclipse can be photographed without the special solar filters required for photographing the Sun.

During totality, however, the Moon can become much dimmer than an ordinary full moon. Cameras may therefore require longer exposures, higher sensitivity settings, wider apertures, or a combination of those adjustments.

A tripod can help prevent camera movement during longer exposures.

The rapidly changing brightness between the normal full Moon, partial eclipse, and totality can require photographers to adjust exposure settings as the eclipse progresses.

Does the Moon Produce Red Light During an Eclipse?

No.

The Moon does not begin generating red light during a lunar eclipse.

The reddish illumination comes from sunlight that has traveled through Earth's atmosphere before reaching the Moon.

The Moon then reflects some of that light back toward observers on Earth.

Does the Moon Completely Disappear During a Total Lunar Eclipse?

Usually not.

If Earth had no atmosphere, the Moon would become much darker while completely inside Earth's umbra because direct sunlight would be blocked.

Earth's atmosphere bends some sunlight into the shadow, however, allowing the eclipsed Moon to remain visible.

That refracted sunlight often gives the Moon its characteristic red or orange appearance during totality.

Extremely dark eclipses can make the Moon harder to see, but it generally remains visible.

What Is the Difference Between a Lunar and Solar Eclipse?

The two events involve opposite alignments.

During a lunar eclipse:

Sun → Earth → Moon

Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. Lunar eclipses occur around full moon.

During a solar eclipse:

Sun → Moon → Earth

The Moon's shadow falls on Earth. Solar eclipses occur around new moon.

There are also major differences in visibility and eye safety.

A lunar eclipse can be visible from much of Earth's nighttime side and is safe to watch directly.

A total solar eclipse can be seen only from a narrow path of totality, and proper solar viewing protection is required whenever the Sun's bright surface is visible.

Can a Lunar and Solar Eclipse Happen Close Together?

Yes.

Because eclipses occur during eclipse seasons, a solar eclipse and lunar eclipse can occur approximately two weeks apart.

For example, a solar eclipse can occur around new moon, followed roughly two weeks later by a lunar eclipse around full moon, or the sequence can occur in the opposite order.

They are separate events produced by the same general period of favorable orbital alignment.

Can Scientists Predict Lunar Eclipses?

Yes.

The motions of Earth and the Moon are predictable enough for astronomers to calculate lunar eclipses far in advance.

Scientists can determine when an eclipse will begin, when the Moon will enter and leave Earth's shadows, whether the eclipse will be penumbral, partial, or total, how long the phases will last, and which portions of Earth will be able to see the event.

Modern eclipse predictions can be calculated with very high precision hundreds of years into the past or future.

What Is the Saros Cycle?

Eclipses occur in repeating patterns because of the orbital motions of Earth and the Moon.

One important pattern is the Saros cycle, which lasts approximately 18 years, 11 days, and 8 hours.

After one Saros period, the Sun, Earth, and Moon return to approximately the same relative geometry, producing a similar eclipse.

Because the cycle includes an additional eight hours—about one-third of a day—Earth has rotated substantially before the next eclipse, so the similar eclipse is visible from a different region of the planet.

Does a Lunar Eclipse Affect Earth's Tides?

A lunar eclipse itself does not create a special type of tidal force.

Lunar eclipses occur around full moon, when the Sun, Earth, and Moon are already positioned in a configuration associated with spring tides—periods when the gravitational effects of the Sun and Moon combine to produce a greater difference between high and low tides.

The eclipse is a consequence of the alignment, not an additional force causing unusually powerful tides beyond the effects associated with the full-moon alignment itself.

Can a Lunar Eclipse Cause Earthquakes or Natural Disasters?

There is no established scientific evidence that lunar eclipses cause earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, severe weather, or other natural disasters.

A lunar eclipse is primarily an astronomical shadow event resulting from the predictable orbital alignment of the Sun, Earth, and Moon.

Claims connecting eclipses to unrelated disasters should not be confused with the ordinary gravitational effects of the Sun and Moon that occur continuously whether an eclipse is happening or not.

Are Lunar Eclipses Dangerous During Pregnancy?

There is no scientific evidence that lunar eclipses pose a special danger to pregnant people or unborn children.

Some cultures have traditions or beliefs connecting eclipses with pregnancy, but an eclipse does not produce a known biological hazard specific to pregnancy.

Unlike a solar eclipse, there is also no direct-sunlight eye hazard associated with watching a lunar eclipse.

Common Misunderstandings

A Lunar Eclipse Is When the Moon Blocks the Sun

No.

That describes a solar eclipse. During a lunar eclipse, Earth is between the Sun and Moon, and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.

A Lunar Eclipse Happens Every Full Moon

No.

The Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's orbital plane, so the full Moon normally passes above or below Earth's shadow.

A Blood Moon Is a Special Type of Eclipse

No.

"Blood moon" is a popular description of the reddish appearance of the Moon during a total lunar eclipse, not a separate astronomical classification.

You Need Eclipse Glasses to Watch a Lunar Eclipse

No.

Lunar eclipses are safe to observe directly with the eyes, binoculars, or telescopes. Eclipse glasses and solar filters are needed for observing the Sun, not the eclipsed Moon.

The Moon Produces Red Light During Totality

No.

The red or orange illumination comes primarily from sunlight filtered and bent through Earth's atmosphere before reaching the Moon.

The Moon Completely Disappears During Totality

Usually not.

Earth's atmosphere refracts some sunlight into the umbra, often leaving the Moon visible in shades of red, orange, or brown.

Only People Along a Narrow Eclipse Path Can See It

No.

That limitation primarily applies to total solar eclipses. A lunar eclipse can be visible across much of Earth's nighttime hemisphere wherever the Moon is above the horizon.

Lunar Eclipses Cause Natural Disasters

There is no established scientific evidence that lunar eclipses cause earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, or severe weather.

Why Lunar Eclipses Matter

Lunar eclipses provide a visible demonstration of the orbital relationship among the Sun, Earth, and Moon.

They show the shape and location of Earth's shadow and demonstrate how Earth's atmosphere interacts with sunlight.

The reddish color of a totally eclipsed Moon is also a striking example of atmospheric scattering and refraction—the same broad physical processes responsible for many of the colors seen at sunrise and sunset.

Unlike total solar eclipses, lunar eclipses can be observed simultaneously across a large portion of Earth and require no specialized eye protection, making them particularly accessible astronomical events.

Conclusion

A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls across the lunar surface.

Depending on the Moon's path through Earth's shadow, the eclipse can be penumbral, partial, or total. During a total lunar eclipse, the Moon often appears red or orange because sunlight passing through Earth's atmosphere is scattered and bent into Earth's shadow.

Lunar eclipses occur only around full moon, but they do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's orbital plane.

Unlike solar eclipses, lunar eclipses are safe to view directly without eclipse glasses and can be visible from much of Earth's nighttime side at the same time.
