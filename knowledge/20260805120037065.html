What Is the Election Assistance Commission (EAC)?

August 5, 20264 min read

What Is the Election Assistance Commission (EAC)?

The U.S. Election Assistance Commission (EAC) is an independent federal agency that helps improve the administration of elections across the United States. It develops voluntary guidelines for voting systems, distributes certain federal election funds, conducts research, and provides resources to state and local election officials.

The EAC does not run federal elections or determine election results. Instead, it supports states and local jurisdictions, which are primarily responsible for administering elections.

Why Was the EAC Created?

The EAC was established by the Help America Vote Act (HAVA) of 2002, which Congress passed following the disputed 2000 presidential election.

Its mission is to improve election administration, promote accessibility, and help states modernize voting equipment and election procedures.

What Does the EAC Do?

The EAC has several primary responsibilities, including:

  • Developing voluntary voting system guidelines.
  • Testing and certifying voting equipment through accredited laboratories.
  • Distributing certain federal election grants authorized by Congress.
  • Conducting research on election administration.
  • Collecting nationwide election data.
  • Providing training and best practices for election officials.
  • Supporting voting accessibility for people with disabilities and military and overseas voters.

Does the EAC Run Elections?

No.

Federal elections are administered primarily by state and local election officials, not by the federal government.

The EAC does not:

  • Count votes.
  • Certify election winners.
  • Operate polling places.
  • Register voters.
  • Set each state's election laws.

Those responsibilities belong to individual states and, in many cases, counties or municipalities.

What Are Voluntary Voting System Guidelines?

One of the EAC's best-known responsibilities is developing the Voluntary Voting System Guidelines (VVSG).

These guidelines establish technical, security, accessibility, and testing standards that voting equipment manufacturers may use when designing voting systems.

States decide whether to require EAC-certified voting equipment under their own election laws.

How Does Voting System Certification Work?

The EAC oversees a national certification program for voting equipment.

Independent laboratories accredited through the EAC program test voting systems against applicable federal standards. If a system meets those standards, it may receive EAC certification.

However, federal certification does not require every state to use that equipment. Each state determines which systems it will approve under its own laws and procedures.

Why Is the EAC Frequently in the News?

The EAC often appears in news reports involving:

  • Election security.
  • Voting machine certification.
  • Federal election funding.
  • Election administration.
  • Accessibility improvements.
  • Election research and nationwide voting statistics.
  • Disputes over federal and state authority in elections.

Because the agency develops national guidance and certifies voting systems, its actions are often discussed during major federal elections.

Who Leads the EAC?

Under the Help America Vote Act, the EAC is structured to have four commissioners who are nominated by the President and confirmed by the U.S. Senate.

No more than two commissioners may belong to the same political party. Major commission actions generally require approval from at least three commissioners, creating a bipartisan quorum requirement.

Current Leadership Status

As of July 2026, the EAC has no sitting commissioners and therefore lacks the quorum needed to approve new commission-level policies, adopt or revise voting system guidelines, or take other actions that require commissioner votes.

Commissioner Donald Palmer resigned effective April 30, 2026. The remaining three commissioners—Thomas Hicks, Christy McCormick, and Benjamin Hovland—were effectively removed from their positions on July 9, 2026, following changes in the legal understanding of presidential authority over independent agency officials.

The agency's professional staff continues performing day-to-day administrative work under its executive leadership. Existing resources, certifications, testing programs, research, and routine services may continue, but actions requiring approval by the commissioners cannot proceed until enough new members are nominated and confirmed to restore a quorum.

This vacancy is a current institutional condition rather than a change to the EAC's permanent legal structure. Federal law still provides for a four-member bipartisan commission.

Common Misunderstandings

One common misconception is that the EAC controls U.S. elections. In reality, elections are administered primarily by state and local governments.

Another misunderstanding is that the EAC can overturn election results. It has no authority to certify winners or invalidate election outcomes.

EAC certification of voting equipment also does not automatically require states to use those systems. States decide which equipment meets their own legal requirements.

Finally, the absence of sitting commissioners does not mean the entire agency has closed. Staff may continue routine functions, although the commission cannot take actions that legally require commissioner approval and a quorum.

Conclusion

The U.S. Election Assistance Commission is an independent federal agency that supports election administration through research, voting system certification, federal guidance, and grants authorized by Congress. Although it plays an important national role, it does not conduct elections or determine their outcomes. Those responsibilities remain primarily with state and local election officials.

The EAC is legally designed as a four-member bipartisan commission. As of July 2026, however, all four seats are vacant, limiting the agency's ability to approve new commission-level policies until new commissioners are nominated and confirmed.
