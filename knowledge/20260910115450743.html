What Causes the Northern Lights?

September 12, 20267 min read

The northern lights, also called the aurora borealis, are glowing displays that appear in the night sky, most often at high northern latitudes. They can look like pale arcs, rippling curtains, vertical rays or large areas of color stretching across the sky.

The light is produced when electrically charged particles associated with the Sun enter Earth's upper atmosphere and transfer energy to atoms and molecules there. Earth's magnetic field plays a crucial role by guiding many of those particles toward the polar regions.

Where Do the Particles Come From?

The Sun constantly releases a stream of charged particles known as the solar wind. It consists mainly of electrons and protons traveling outward through the solar system.

The amount and speed of this material are not constant. Activity on the Sun can send faster or denser streams of charged particles toward Earth.

Particularly important events include coronal mass ejections, or CMEs, in which the Sun releases enormous clouds of magnetized plasma into space. If a CME travels toward Earth and interacts strongly with Earth's magnetic environment, it can cause a geomagnetic storm and produce unusually widespread auroras.

High-speed solar wind streams can also trigger increased auroral activity without a major CME.

Earth's Magnetic Field Provides Protection

Earth is surrounded by a magnetic field that extends far into space. The region dominated by this field is called the magnetosphere.

The magnetosphere deflects much of the charged material arriving from the Sun, helping protect Earth's atmosphere and surface from direct exposure to the solar wind.

But the interaction is not a perfect barrier. Under suitable conditions, energy and particles from the solar wind can enter Earth's magnetic environment. Magnetic processes then accelerate and guide charged particles along Earth's magnetic field lines toward the upper atmosphere, particularly in regions surrounding the magnetic poles.

That is why auroras occur much more frequently in far northern and far southern regions than near the equator.

How Do Charged Particles Create Light?

When energetic particles reach Earth's upper atmosphere, they collide with atoms and molecules, especially oxygen and nitrogen.

These collisions transfer energy to the atmospheric particles, placing them temporarily into higher-energy states. They cannot remain in those states indefinitely. As they return to lower-energy states, they release the excess energy as light.

The same basic idea occurs in many types of glowing gases: energy is supplied to atoms or molecules, and light is emitted when that energy is released.

An aurora is therefore not sunlight reflecting from the atmosphere. The atmosphere itself is emitting visible light after being energized by incoming particles.

Why Are the Northern Lights Different Colors?

The color of an aurora depends mainly on which atmospheric gases are emitting light, the altitude where the interaction occurs and the energy involved.

Green is the most commonly observed auroral color. It is produced primarily by atomic oxygen at altitudes of roughly 100 to 200 kilometers, although the exact altitude varies.

Red auroras can also be produced by oxygen, generally at higher altitudes where the atmosphere is thinner. During strong geomagnetic activity, red auroras can sometimes cover large portions of the sky.

Blue and violet colors are associated mainly with nitrogen and tend to occur at lower altitudes or in energetic auroral displays.

Different emissions can overlap, producing mixtures that appear pink, purple or other shades.

Human vision also affects how auroral colors appear. A faint aurora may look grayish or nearly colorless to the naked eye even when a camera records noticeable green or red. Cameras can collect light for longer periods than the human eye effectively does, making weak colors much easier to see in photographs.

Why Do Auroras Look Like Curtains and Ribbons?

Charged particles tend to travel along Earth's magnetic field lines as they approach the atmosphere.

Auroral light is therefore organized by the structure of Earth's magnetic field rather than appearing randomly across the sky. Large sheets of glowing atmosphere can form along these magnetic structures.

Viewed from the ground, those sheets can resemble curtains, ribbons or arcs. Changes in the flow of particles and in Earth's magnetic environment can cause the structures to move, ripple, brighten or develop vertical-looking rays.

Although an aurora can appear relatively close, the glowing material is actually high above Earth's surface, usually around 100 kilometers or more above the ground, with some emissions occurring several hundred kilometers up.

Why Are Auroras Usually Seen Near the Poles?

Earth's magnetic field directs energetic particles toward regions surrounding the magnetic poles.

Instead of occurring directly over the poles alone, auroras are commonly concentrated in broad ring-shaped regions called auroral ovals. One surrounds the northern magnetic region and another surrounds the southern one.

Places such as Alaska, northern Canada, Iceland, Scandinavia and other high-latitude regions frequently lie within or near the northern auroral oval, making auroras relatively common there.

There is an equivalent phenomenon in the Southern Hemisphere called the aurora australis, or southern lights. The northern and southern lights are produced by the same fundamental process.

Why Can the Northern Lights Sometimes Be Seen Farther South?

During strong geomagnetic storms, the auroral oval can expand toward lower latitudes.

This can make auroras visible hundreds or even thousands of kilometers farther south than usual. During particularly powerful events, people in areas that rarely experience auroras may see red, green or purple light across the sky.

Seeing an aurora unusually far south does not mean Earth's magnetic poles have suddenly moved there. It generally means solar activity has caused a significant disturbance in Earth's magnetosphere and expanded the region where auroral activity is occurring.

What Is a Geomagnetic Storm?

A geomagnetic storm is a significant disturbance of Earth's magnetosphere caused by its interaction with conditions in the solar wind.

The strength of a storm depends on more than simply how much material the Sun sends toward Earth. The magnetic field carried by the solar wind is also important. Certain orientations allow energy to transfer much more efficiently into Earth's magnetic environment.

Strong geomagnetic storms can produce spectacular auroras, but their effects are not limited to colorful skies. They can also affect satellites, radio communications, navigation systems and, in severe cases, electrical power systems.

This is one reason scientists closely monitor activity on the Sun and conditions in space around Earth.

Can Scientists Predict the Northern Lights?

Auroral activity can be forecast, but predicting exactly what an observer will see at a particular location is difficult.

Scientists monitor the Sun for eruptions and measure the solar wind as it approaches Earth. This can provide warning that conditions are favorable for increased geomagnetic activity.

Once disturbances reach monitoring spacecraft closer to Earth, scientists can better estimate their likely effects. Even then, cloud cover, darkness, local light pollution and rapidly changing magnetic conditions determine whether an aurora will actually be visible to someone on the ground.

Are the Northern Lights Dangerous to People?

The visible aurora itself is not dangerous to people watching from Earth's surface. The glowing interactions occur high in the atmosphere, far above normal weather and commercial aircraft cruising altitudes.

The space weather that produces powerful auroras can, however, affect technology. Energetic particles and disturbances in Earth's magnetic field can interfere with satellites and radio signals, increase radiation exposure for spacecraft and some high-altitude operations, and induce electrical currents in long conductors such as power transmission lines.

For most people on the ground, the aurora is simply the visible part of a much larger interaction taking place between the Sun and Earth's magnetic environment.

The Bottom Line

The northern lights occur when energy and charged particles associated with the Sun interact with Earth's magnetosphere and upper atmosphere. Earth's magnetic field guides energetic particles toward high-latitude regions, where collisions with oxygen and nitrogen cause the atmosphere to emit visible light.

The gases involved, the altitude and the energy of the particles help determine whether the sky glows green, red, blue, violet or a mixture of colors. Changes in Earth's magnetic environment create the moving curtains, arcs and rays associated with auroral displays.

What appears from the ground as colored light dancing across the night sky is ultimately a visible sign of the continuing interaction between Earth and the Sun.
