🧪 MIT Reopens Chemistry Building After Hazmat ScareTests Find No Mercury Exposure Following Lab Investigation 👇

IllustrationIllustration of hazmat crews responding to a refinery chemical leak.Credit: AI-generated illustration (No Spin Media)
EducationAugust 31, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 31, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) reopened Building 18, home to the university's chemistry department, on August 31 after completing a multi-day hazardous materials investigation prompted by a graduate student's claim that they had synthesized dimethylmercury, an extremely toxic organomercury compound. The building had been closed since August 26 while MIT Environment, Health and Safety (EHS) personnel and hazardous materials specialists conducted extensive testing, decontamination, and safety assessments.

University officials said the investigation began after the student sought medical attention and reported the possible synthesis of the prohibited chemical. Initial blood tests showed no evidence of mercury exposure, and investigators later said available evidence cast significant doubt on whether dimethylmercury had actually been produced. As a precaution, MIT closed the laboratory, cleaned affected work areas and common spaces, and conducted environmental monitoring before allowing the building to reopen.

MIT said Building 18 resumed normal operations at 6:00 a.m. Monday after environmental testing indicated the facility was safe for students, faculty, and staff. The university also confirmed that the student's residence at Ashdown House was evaluated and cleaned as a precaution, with no evidence of secondary contamination found. Department leaders reiterated that the purchase or synthesis of dimethylmercury is prohibited under MIT's laboratory safety policies and that the incident will be reviewed to determine whether additional safeguards are needed.

Dimethylmercury is considered one of the world's most hazardous laboratory chemicals because it can be absorbed through the skin and may cause severe neurological injury after delayed exposure. The compound is widely known because of the 1997 death of Dr. Karen Wetterhahn, a Dartmouth College chemistry professor whose accidental exposure led to significant changes in laboratory safety practices nationwide. MIT officials said they will continue reviewing laboratory procedures while emphasizing that the building has been cleared for normal use.
