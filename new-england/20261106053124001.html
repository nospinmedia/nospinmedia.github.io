🗳️ Dunlap concedes to LePage, but Maine race isn't legally overState must complete ranked-choice count despite concession 👇

EconomyNovember 6, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published November 6, 2026 at 8:45 AM ET

Democrat Matt Dunlap has conceded Maine's 2nd Congressional District race to Republican Paul LePage, but the concession does not end the state's election process. Maine officials say a ranked-choice tabulation must still be completed because no candidate received more than 50% of the first-choice vote.

Unofficial results showed LePage, the former Maine governor, with 170,263 votes, or 49.89%, compared with 164,845 votes, or 48.31%, for Dunlap, a former Maine secretary of state. Another 123 votes went to write-in candidates, while 6,017 ballots did not contain a first-choice vote in the congressional race.

Dunlap conceded Thursday, saying the remaining process was not expected to change the outcome. His concession, however, is a political acknowledgment of the apparent result rather than the official mechanism for determining the winner.

Under Maine's ranked-choice voting system, the state must conduct additional tabulation when no candidate receives the required first-round majority. Secretary of State Shenna Bellows said that process will move forward despite Dunlap's concession.

The official ranked-choice tabulation is scheduled to begin Monday, November 9, at 10 a.m. in Augusta. Election data from municipalities will be brought together for the calculation required under state law before a winner can be formally certified.

That creates an unusual situation: Dunlap has acknowledged LePage as the apparent winner, but the election is not yet legally finished. A concession does not erase votes already cast or substitute for the state's certification process. If the ranked-choice calculation unexpectedly changed the result, the official tabulation — rather than the concession — would determine the election outcome.

The development updates an election that remained unresolved after Tuesday's voting because LePage finished just short of an outright majority. Dunlap's concession makes a LePage victory appear considerably more likely, but Maine must still complete the process required by law.
