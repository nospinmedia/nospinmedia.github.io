🌲 Conservationists Push Congress to Protect New England's Roadless ForestsAdvocates Seek Permanent Safeguards as Federal Roadless Policy Faces Uncertainty 👇

File photoU.S. Capitol building in Washington, D.C.Credit: Architect of the Capitol
EnvironmentOctober 13, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published October 13, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Conservation groups are urging Congress to provide stronger, permanent protections for roadless areas of New England's national forests, arguing that existing administrative safeguards could be changed or eliminated by future federal policy decisions.

The effort centers on roadless lands within the White Mountain National Forest in New Hampshire and Maine and the Green Mountain National Forest in Vermont. These areas include large stretches of relatively undeveloped federal forest where road construction and associated development have been restricted under federal policy.

At the center of the debate is the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule, which established protections for millions of acres of inventoried roadless areas within the National Forest System. Conservationists argue that because those protections are based on an administrative rule rather than an act of Congress, they remain vulnerable to changes by presidential administrations.

Environmental advocates want lawmakers to establish protections through federal legislation, making them more difficult to reverse through administrative action. Supporters say preserving roadless areas helps maintain wildlife habitat, protect watersheds and keep large forest ecosystems from becoming fragmented by new roads and development.

The issue has particular significance in northern New England, where national forests are important to both conservation and recreation. The White Mountain National Forest draws visitors for hiking, camping, skiing and other outdoor activities, while also supporting timber harvesting and other uses under federal land-management plans.

The debate does not mean that all roadless areas are legally designated wilderness. Congressionally designated wilderness generally receives stronger statutory protections, while inventoried roadless areas can remain subject to different federal management rules. Conservationists say that distinction leaves some lands more vulnerable to future policy changes.

Supporters of permanent protections also point to the role large, intact forests play in storing carbon, maintaining water quality and providing connected habitat for wildlife. Decisions about additional protections, however, involve balancing conservation goals with timber management, recreation, local economic interests and federal land-management priorities.

The campaign comes as the future of federal roadless protections receives renewed attention nationally, putting New England's relatively limited national forest acreage into a broader debate over how public lands should be managed.

Should Congress make existing protections for New England's roadless national forest areas permanent, or should future administrations retain flexibility over how those lands are managed?
