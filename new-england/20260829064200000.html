📸 New England Cities Reconsider Flock Camera NetworksPrivacy concerns prompt removals, pauses and policy reviews 👇

PoliticsAugust 29, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 29, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

New Bedford, Massachusetts, has become the latest New England community to halt use of Flock Safety automated license plate reader cameras after the City Council unanimously voted to direct Mayor Jon Mitchell and the police department to cover or remove the city's 20 cameras while an investigation into alleged misuse continues. The action follows reports that a police officer improperly accessed the system to monitor a former romantic partner, prompting an expanded internal review by Police Chief Jason Thody.

The New Bedford vote reflects a broader regional reassessment of automated license plate reader technology. Other Massachusetts communities, including Hopkinton, Stow, and East Bridgewater, have either ended or reconsidered Flock Safety agreements following concerns about procurement practices, privacy protections and oversight. Civil liberties advocates have argued that widespread camera networks collecting vehicle location data require stronger safeguards and greater public accountability.

Across Connecticut, legislative leaders have urged municipalities to pause new Flock Safety installations while lawmakers consider statewide regulations expected during the 2027 legislative session. In Milford, officials halted additional deployments after public opposition, while residents in New London raised concerns about regional data-sharing agreements. In Vermont, the Manchester Selectboard postponed action on a proposed network of five automated readers to examine questions involving data retention, artificial intelligence accuracy and privacy protections.

Supporters of automated license plate readers say the technology helps investigators locate stolen vehicles, identify suspects and solve serious crimes more quickly. Critics argue that expanding interconnected camera systems raises concerns about government surveillance, data security and potential misuse of personal travel information. As more New England communities review their policies, lawmakers and local officials are weighing how to balance public safety with privacy protections.
