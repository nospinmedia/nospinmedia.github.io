📷 Massachusetts Flock Backlash WidensHopkinton Ends Camera Contract as Police Misuse Probes Expand 👇

New EnglandAugust 21, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 21, 2026 at 5:21 PM ET

Massachusetts communities are intensifying scrutiny of Flock Safety automated license plate reader systems after multiple investigations into alleged police misuse and growing public opposition. On August 20, the Town of Hopkinton announced it had terminated its contract with Flock Safety following a legal review that found the agreement did not comply with state procurement requirements. Town officials ordered the company's equipment removed by September 30 while launching a broader review of surveillance technology, procurement policies, and privacy safeguards.

The controversy intensified in New Bedford, where a planned City Council vote on the future of the city's 20-camera Flock network was derailed when protesters repeatedly interrupted the meeting, forcing officials to adjourn before taking action. The cameras had already been suspended earlier in August by Police Chief Jason Thody after allegations that Officer Emily Pacheco improperly used the system to monitor an ex-partner. An internal affairs investigation remains ongoing, and a judge recently extended an abuse prevention order against Pacheco for an additional six months while the case proceeds.

Questions about Flock use have also spread to Lynn, where Police Chief Ashley Affonco placed two officers on paid administrative leave after an internal audit uncovered what the department described as a concerning pattern of unauthorized database searches. Access to the Flock system has since been restricted to lieutenants and above while investigators determine whether department policies or state law were violated. The developments follow other recent disciplinary actions in Massachusetts involving alleged misuse of law enforcement databases, reinforcing concerns that the issue extends beyond a single department.

The incidents have fueled calls for statewide oversight of automated license plate reader technology. The ACLU of Massachusetts has argued that the systems require stronger statutory safeguards, audit requirements, and public transparency to prevent abuse. In response to growing criticism, Flock Safety has expanded its Audit Assist monitoring tools to automatically identify unusual search activity, but local officials across Massachusetts continue debating whether enhanced oversight is sufficient or whether communities should discontinue the technology altogether.
