💰 Kosta Diamantis sues Connecticut after state stops his pensionFormer official challenges cutoff after fleeing to Greece before sentencing 👇

IllustrationIllustration representing court rulings and legal proceedings.Credit: AI-generated illustration
EducationSeptember 19, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published September 19, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Former Connecticut official Kosta Diamantis has filed a federal lawsuit against state officials after the state stopped paying his pension following his flight to Greece before a scheduled corruption sentencing. Diamantis is suing Connecticut Comptroller Sean Scanlon and Attorney General William Tong, arguing that the state improperly interrupted more than $70,000 a year in pension benefits without first following the legal process required to reduce or revoke them.

The lawsuit is a new development following Diamantis' failure to appear for sentencing on September 17. A federal jury convicted the former deputy state budget director and head of Connecticut's school construction office last year on 21 counts, including bribery, extortion, conspiracy and lying to federal investigators. Prosecutors said he solicited bribes from construction contractors whose work he oversaw. Diamantis has maintained that his prosecution was politically motivated.

After officials confirmed that Diamantis had left the country, Scanlon announced that his office was discontinuing direct deposits of Diamantis' monthly pension. Scanlon said someone convicted of defrauding taxpayers and fleeing justice should not continue receiving a state pension. His office says it is confident it has authority over how pension payments are issued and will defend that position in court.

Diamantis' lawsuit argues that Connecticut law establishes a separate judicial process for taking away or reducing the pension of a public official convicted of corruption-related crimes. Under that process, the Attorney General's Office can ask a state Superior Court judge to reduce or revoke a pension, and the judge considers factors including the seriousness of the crime, financial losses and violation of the public trust. The Attorney General's Office has used the law more than 30 times since 2008.

Diamantis is not asking the federal court to overturn his criminal verdict or decide that he is entitled to keep his pension permanently. His attorney, Norm Pattis, argues instead that the state cannot stop an existing pension benefit without adequate notice and an opportunity to be heard. As of Friday, the Attorney General's Office had not yet filed the separate state-court action seeking to permanently revoke or reduce the pension.
