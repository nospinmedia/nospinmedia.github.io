🧠 Boston native Karl Deisseroth wins Nobel Prize in medicineLight-based breakthrough transformed how scientists study the brain 👇

EducationOctober 6, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published October 6, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Boston native Karl Deisseroth is one of three scientists awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries that made it possible to control nerve cells using light, work that helped create the field known as optogenetics.

Deisseroth, a neuroscientist and psychiatrist at Stanford University, shares the prize with German scientists Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel. The Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet announced the award on October 5, recognizing their discoveries involving light-sensitive proteins called channelrhodopsins and their use in manipulating the activity of specific cells.

The award has a strong New England connection. Deisseroth was born in Boston and studied at Harvard University, earning his bachelor's degree in biochemical sciences in 1992 before completing medical and doctoral training at Stanford. His later research helped demonstrate that genes encoding microbial light-sensitive proteins could be introduced into neurons, allowing researchers to switch selected brain cells on or off with precisely timed pulses of light.

Hegemann and Nagel's earlier work helped establish how channelrhodopsins respond to light. Deisseroth and his collaborators then demonstrated their usefulness for controlling neurons, helping turn those discoveries into a powerful neuroscience research method. Optogenetics now allows scientists to investigate specific neural circuits rather than stimulating broad areas of the brain.

Researchers around the world have used the technique to study movement, memory, behavior and disorders including Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, depression and schizophrenia. Scientists are also investigating possible medical applications, although many potential treatments involving optogenetics remain experimental.

Deisseroth, Hegemann and Nagel will share the Nobel award of 12 million Swedish kronor, worth roughly $1.2 million. The Nobel committee credited their work with giving researchers an unprecedented ability to investigate how individual cells contribute to the activity of complex biological systems.
