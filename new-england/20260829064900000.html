🚁 Boston Police Launch Drone First-Responder PilotAircraft will respond to 911 calls before officers arrive 👇

New EnglandAugust 29, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 29, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

The Boston Police Department (BPD) has launched a Drone as First Responder (DFR) pilot program that will dispatch unmanned aircraft to selected 911 calls and ShotSpotter gunshot detection alerts before officers reach the scene. Announced on August 28, the program begins field operations during the August 29-30 weekend and is designed to provide dispatchers and responding officers with live aerial video to improve situational awareness and speed emergency response in heavily traveled areas of the city.

Police Commissioner Michael Cox said the drones can often reach emergency scenes in about one minute, allowing officers to assess conditions before arriving. The department is partnering with Motorola Solutions and BRINC, whose weather-resistant drone systems operate from automated docking stations placed at strategic locations around Boston. Officials said the aircraft may also be used to help locate missing children, endangered adults and other individuals when rapid aerial searches could improve response efforts.

To address privacy concerns, the department said every flight will be controlled by FAA-certified Boston police pilots operating under established departmental policies. During transit, drone cameras will remain fixed toward the horizon and may only be directed downward after reaching the reported emergency location. Officials said the pilot program will monitor response times, operational effectiveness and community feedback before any decision is made on expanding the system.

The initiative follows similar first-responder drone deployments in other Massachusetts communities, including Revere, and complements Boston's broader investments in public safety technology. Supporters argue that drones can provide critical information during fast-moving emergencies while reducing risks to officers and the public. Privacy advocates, however, continue to urge strong oversight, clear data-retention policies and limits on surveillance to ensure the technology is used only for legitimate public safety purposes.
