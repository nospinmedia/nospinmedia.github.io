🧠 Boston researchers find blood marker that may detect CTE during lifeSmall study offers potential path toward diagnosing disease now confirmed only after death 👇

EducationNovember 20, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published November 20, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

Researchers led by Boston University and Banner Health have identified a blood marker that may eventually make it possible to detect chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, while a person is still alive. The findings, published Thursday, November 19 in the peer-reviewed Journal of the American Medical Association, represent an early but potentially important step toward a test for a disease that currently can be definitively diagnosed only after death.

The researchers studied 21 former football players who had provided blood samples while alive and later donated their brains to the BU CTE Center Brain Bank. Autopsies found CTE in 18 of the 21, including 16 with high-stage disease and two with low-stage CTE. Three did not have CTE.

Researchers examined the blood for endogenously cleaved microtubule-binding region tau, or eMTBR-tau, a protein associated with the tau tangles found in CTE and Alzheimer's disease. Concentrations were significantly higher among participants with advanced CTE than among those with early-stage or no CTE.

Five participants with CTE also had Alzheimer's disease. Researchers found another marker, p-tau217, was elevated only in participants who also had Alzheimer's, raising the possibility that a combination of biomarkers could eventually help doctors distinguish between the two diseases.

The findings remain preliminary. The study included only 21 people, with very few participants who had early-stage CTE or no disease, and researchers said larger and more diverse studies are needed. The proposed blood test is not FDA-approved, is not available to patients and should not currently be used to make diagnostic decisions.

Adding confidence to the finding, a separate research team published another study in the same JAMA issue that detected elevated levels of a related eMTBR-tau marker in different people with autopsy-confirmed CTE using a different testing method.

BU researcher Michael Alosco, co-director of clinical research at the university's CTE Center, said validation could eventually allow researchers to diagnose CTE during life, enroll affected patients in clinical trials and begin testing potential treatments.
