⚖️ Five New England states sue over new immigration ruleCoalition seeks to block expanded public-charge policy before Friday 👇

HealthSeptember 15, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published September 15, 2026 at 8:45 AM ET

Five New England states have joined a multistate lawsuit seeking to block the Trump administration's new public charge rule before it takes effect on September 18. Attorneys general representing Massachusetts, Connecticut, Maine, Rhode Island and Vermont are among officials from 22 states and the District of Columbia challenging the policy in federal court in New York. New Hampshire is the only New England state not participating.

The new Department of Homeland Security rule gives immigration officers broader discretion when deciding whether immigrants seeking green cards are likely to become a public charge. Under the policy, officers can consider nearly any means-tested public benefit used for any length of time and may also consider some benefits legally received by an applicant's family members, including U.S. citizens.

That represents a significant expansion from the 2022 rule, which generally limited public-charge determinations to cash assistance for income maintenance and long-term institutional care paid for by the government. The Trump administration says the broader policy is intended to reinforce requirements that immigrants seeking permanent residency demonstrate self-sufficiency and reduce reliance on taxpayer-funded benefits.

The states argue the new rule exceeds DHS's authority and violates the Administrative Procedure Act because it is arbitrary and capricious and departs from the longstanding interpretation of public charge. They also contend that uncertainty over which benefits could count against an applicant may discourage eligible immigrant families from using programs including Medicaid, SNAP and housing assistance.

DHS itself estimates that people avoiding or leaving benefits because of the rule could reduce annual federal transfers to states by approximately $4.05 billion for Medicaid and CHIP and another $1.02 billion for SNAP, according to the lawsuit. Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell and the other plaintiffs argue those changes could shift costs to states while affecting health care providers and other public services.

The coalition is asking the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York to declare the rule unlawful and prevent it from taking effect.
