🔌 New England Has Avoided the AI Data Center Boom — So FarISO New England Prepares Grid as Developers Eye Region for Power-Hungry Facilities 👇

EducationOctober 24, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published October 24, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

New England has largely avoided the rapid expansion of massive AI data centers seen in other parts of the United States, but regional grid planners are preparing for the possibility that the industry's enormous electricity demand could soon arrive here.

One major reason is cost. New England has some of the country's highest electricity prices, making the region less attractive than areas where large technology companies can secure cheaper power. The region also has limited available transmission capacity and a lengthy process for connecting very large new electricity users to the grid.

Modern AI data centers can require hundreds of megawatts of electricity, with some proposed facilities elsewhere in the country demanding as much power as a small city. Unlike many traditional businesses, data centers typically consume electricity continuously, creating substantial new demand around the clock.

ISO New England, which operates the six-state regional power grid, is preparing for potential growth by changing how it evaluates large new electricity customers. The grid operator wants better information about proposed projects before they connect so planners can determine whether additional generation or transmission infrastructure will be necessary.

The region's relative lack of giant data centers could give policymakers an opportunity to plan before demand accelerates. Other parts of the country have faced questions about whether rapid data-center construction could strain electrical grids, require new power plants or transmission lines, and contribute to higher costs for other customers.

At the same time, attracting data centers can bring construction spending, tax revenue and technology investment. New England already has major universities, technology companies and a highly educated workforce, making the region potentially attractive if developers can secure sufficient electricity.

The challenge is balancing that economic opportunity with New England's existing concerns about electricity prices, grid reliability and clean-energy goals. Decisions made before large projects arrive could determine how much of their infrastructure cost is borne by developers and how much could ultimately affect other electricity customers.
