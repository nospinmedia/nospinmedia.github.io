🤖 Which Maine jobs are most exposed to AI?State research points to office and tech work while hands-on jobs face less disruption 👇

HealthNovember 25, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published November 25, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Artificial intelligence could reshape parts of Maine's workforce, but state researchers caution that a job being highly exposed to AI does not necessarily mean the worker will be replaced.

Research from the Maine Department of Labor's Center for Workforce Research and Information examines how much of the work performed in different occupations could potentially be completed by generative AI in at least half the time while producing comparable quality.

Some of the greatest exposure is in office and administrative support jobs, including bookkeeping, billing, payroll, insurance claims processing and legal or court clerk work. Maine has already lost roughly 21,000 office and administrative support jobs over the longer-term period examined, although those losses cannot simply be attributed to today's generative AI systems.

Perhaps less intuitively, highly skilled technology jobs also rank near the top. Software development, computer systems and data science occupations were estimated to have roughly 73% to 87% AI task exposure. That measures the share of work AI could potentially assist with or perform—not the percentage of workers expected to lose their jobs.

At the opposite end are jobs requiring physical work, direct personal interaction or hands-on skills. Cooks, dishwashers, food-service workers, landscapers, construction laborers, equipment operators and many hands-on healthcare and trade workers generally showed little AI task exposure, in some cases around 0% to 5%.

So far, Maine's labor market does not show widespread job losses tied to occupations with high AI exposure. The Maine Center for Economic Policy reported that state employment and wage data through August had not shown a measurable spike in unemployment or falling wages among those occupations.

Workers are nevertheless concerned about what comes next. A May 2026 survey cited by the organization found 70% of Mainers expected AI to reduce the total number of jobs over the next decade, while about one-quarter of workers already using AI were concerned about losing their own jobs.

The distinction is important: the research measures what AI can potentially do within a job, not which jobs it will eliminate.
