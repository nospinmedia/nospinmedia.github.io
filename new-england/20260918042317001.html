🌲 Penobscot Nation regains 11,297 acres near KatahdinFirst phase could eventually help create a southern entrance to national monument 👇

EnvironmentSeptember 18, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published September 18, 2026 at 6:58 PM ET

The Penobscot Nation has regained 11,297 acres of ancestral land near Mount Katahdin, completing the first phase of a much larger effort that is expected to return more than 30,000 acres to tribal control. The transfer was completed September 16 with help from the Trust for Public Land, which acquired the larger property after it became available and has been working with the Nation to finance its return.

The land surrounds portions of Wáhsehtəkʷ, the East Branch of the Penobscot River, and lies near Katahdin Woods and Waters National Monument. The first-phase property includes about 15 miles of cold-water streams and 865 acres of ponds and wetlands, providing habitat for species including Atlantic salmon, brook trout and moose. The transfer raises the Penobscot Nation's total land holdings to approximately 140,000 acres, according to the Portland Press Herald.

The Trust for Public Land and Penobscot Nation began their partnership in 2023 to raise $32.5 million for the broader return and improved access to the national monument. They have raised about $20.7 million so far, with fundraising continuing for the remaining roughly 20,000 acres. Penobscot officials say the newly returned land will support hunting, fishing, cultural activities, economic opportunities and conservation work, including habitat restoration and wildlife monitoring.

The project could eventually also improve public access to Katahdin Woods and Waters National Monument. Officials are considering a southern route from the Millinocket area through the property; the monument is currently primarily accessed from the east. The Press Herald reports that congressional approval would be needed for the National Park Service to acquire the necessary road rights-of-way, and Maine Public reports that plans for the southern access remain long-term and are still being developed.

The Wáhsehtəkʷ project is part of a broader effort involving Wabanaki tribal governments and conservation organizations to return ancestral lands across Maine. Penobscot Nation Chief Kirk Francis said the transfer gives the Nation an opportunity to care for the land for its people and future generations.
