💰 New Haven Reaches $30 Million Wrongful-Conviction SettlementCity Could Spend Decades Paying Debt From Two More Cases 👇

IllustrationIllustration representing court rulings and legal proceedings.Credit: AI-generated illustration
EducationNovember 21, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published November 21, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

New Haven, Connecticut, has reached proposed settlements totaling $30 million with a man wrongfully convicted of murder and the estate of another man convicted in the same case, adding a major financial obligation that the city plans to pay through long-term borrowing.

Under the agreements, George Gould would receive $18 million, while the estate of Ronald Taylor, who has since died, would receive $12 million. The settlements still require approval from the New Haven Board of Alders before the city can issue bonds to fund the payments.

Gould and Taylor were convicted in 1995 of the 1993 robbery and murder of New Haven bodega owner Eugenio DeLeon Vega. Their convictions relied on testimony from a single witness and were later overturned, leading to civil litigation seeking compensation for their wrongful imprisonment.

Instead of paying the settlements entirely from its current operating budget, New Haven plans to borrow the money. Mayor Justin Elicker said the city typically limits annual borrowing to approximately $30 million, normally using that capacity for infrastructure including roads and schools. Using bonds for wrongful-conviction settlements therefore competes with borrowing that otherwise could support city projects.

Elicker also said New Haven is paying more than twice as much in wrongful-conviction compensation as the State of Connecticut, highlighting the scale of the financial burden falling on the municipality.

Borrowing spreads the immediate cost across future budgets but also means taxpayers can continue paying principal and interest long after the underlying criminal cases and civil lawsuits have ended. The eventual expense can therefore exceed the settlements' original $30 million value.

The agreements add to previous wrongful-conviction settlements facing New Haven and illustrate how failures in criminal cases can produce financial consequences decades later, affecting both those who were imprisoned and residents responsible for financing compensation.
