🏠 Massachusetts Expands Support for Backyard HomesNew tools and loans target barriers to accessory dwelling units 👇

EnvironmentAugust 7, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 7, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Massachusetts is expanding financial and technical support for homeowners seeking to build accessory dwelling units as the state tries to increase housing supply and promote smaller, energy-efficient homes. More than 1,200 ADUs received permits statewide during 2025, but that remains below the state's goal of adding between 1,600 and 2,000 units annually through 2030.

A new Massachusetts Housing Partnership program connects homeowners with approved providers who can study whether an ADU is practical on a particular property. The assessments examine site conditions, zoning and permitting requirements, utility connections, preliminary designs and estimated construction costs. MHP pays participating providers $500 for completed studies, while homeowners may be charged no more than an additional $500.

The Mass Clean Energy Center has also launched a statewide online ADU Resource Center containing planning guidance, typical construction costs and a property lookup tool. Its separate Pioneer Valley ADU Accelerator provides one-on-one assistance to homeowners in Franklin, Hampden and Hampshire counties who are considering detached, modular and all-electric units. Participants can receive help selecting builders, evaluating repeatable designs and navigating the development process.

Income-eligible homeowners may separately apply through MassHousing for fixed-rate second mortgages of up to $250,000 for detached ADUs and up to $150,000 for attached units. The financing program is intended to offer lower rates and higher combined loan-to-value limits than many conventional home-equity products.

The programs follow the Affordable Homes Act, which allows ADUs measuring up to 900 square feet to be built by right in single-family zoning districts across 350 of Massachusetts' 351 municipalities. Boston is governed by separate rules, and local governments may still enforce generally applicable building, septic, setback and safety requirements.
