⚖️ Maine High Court Upholds Custody Order on Church AttendanceFather Retains Final Religious Decision Authority 👇

LegalSeptember 2, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published September 2, 2026 at 1:03 PM ET

Maine's highest court ruled that a father may prevent his daughter from attending a specific church under the terms of a shared custody agreement, affirming a lower court order that gave him final authority over the child's religious upbringing. In a decision issued Sept. 1, the Maine Supreme Judicial Court upheld the custody ruling in Bickford v. Bradeen, concluding that family courts may assign one parent decision-making authority over disputed issues when doing so serves the child's best interests.

The dispute involved Matthew Bradeen and Emily Bickford, who share custody of their 12-year-old daughter. Bradeen sought court intervention after arguing that the child's attendance at Calvary Chapel Greater Portland contributed to significant anxiety and panic attacks. A trial court previously found sufficient evidence that the child's well-being had been negatively affected and granted Bradeen final authority over decisions involving her attendance at that church. Bickford appealed, arguing the order violated her constitutional rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

The Supreme Judicial Court rejected that argument, ruling that Maine law permits trial courts to allocate specific parental responsibilities when parents sharing custody cannot agree. The justices concluded that assigning one parent final decision-making authority in a narrowly defined area does not, by itself, violate the other parent's constitutional rights when the order is based on the child's welfare. The ruling leaves the underlying custody arrangement in place while clarifying the authority family courts have to resolve similar parental disputes.

Following the decision, Liberty Counsel, which represents Bickford, announced plans to seek review by the U.S. Supreme Court, arguing the case raises broader questions about parental rights and religious freedom. Unless a higher court agrees to hear the appeal, the Maine ruling will remain the controlling precedent for similar custody disputes within the state.
