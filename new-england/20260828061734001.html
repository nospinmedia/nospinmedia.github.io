⚖️ Jury begins deliberations in Lindsay Clancy caseMassachusetts panel weighs criminal responsibility after 21-day trial 👇

IllustrationIllustration representing court rulings and legal proceedings.Credit: AI-generated illustration
HealthAugust 28, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 28, 2026 at 8:45 AM ET

A Plymouth Superior Court jury has begun deliberating the fate of Lindsay Clancy, the former Massachusetts labor and delivery nurse charged with murdering her three young children in January 2023. The 12-member jury, made up of nine women and three men, concluded its first afternoon of deliberations without reaching a verdict and is scheduled to resume Friday. The case now turns on whether jurors find Clancy criminally responsible for the deaths or conclude that a severe mental illness prevented her from understanding the wrongfulness of her actions.

The jury received the case after 21 days of testimony and closing arguments that presented sharply different accounts of Clancy's mental state. Defense attorney Kevin Reddington argued that Clancy was suffering from severe postpartum psychosis, compounded by numerous psychiatric medications and medical treatment failures, leaving her unable to appreciate the nature of her conduct. Prosecutors argued the killings of 5-year-old Cora, 3-year-old Dawson, and 8-month-old Callan Clancy were intentional and carefully planned while Clancy's husband was away from the family home.

Jurors have several possible verdict options under Massachusetts law. They may convict Clancy of first-degree murder, second-degree murder, or a lesser offense such as manslaughter. They may also find her not criminally responsible by reason of mental illness if they determine she lacked criminal responsibility at the time of the killings. Judge William Sullivan instructed jurors on the applicable legal standards before deliberations began following weeks of testimony from psychiatrists, medical experts and other witnesses.

A conviction for first-degree murder would carry a mandatory sentence of life in prison without the possibility of parole. A verdict of not criminally responsible would result in Clancy's commitment to a secure psychiatric facility, where her confinement would be subject to ongoing judicial review rather than a prison sentence. The closely watched case has drawn national attention because of its focus on postpartum mental illness and the legal standards governing criminal responsibility.
