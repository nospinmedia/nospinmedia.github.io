🚰 Rhode Island launches statewide manganese studyURI project will map risks and help small water systems improve treatment 👇

EducationAugust 4, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 4, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

The University of Rhode Island and Rhode Island Department of Health have launched a statewide project to study manganese in drinking water and help public water systems and private well owners manage elevated levels. The initiative, called Statewide Support for Manganese Management in Rhode Island, began fieldwork this summer and will continue through September 30, 2030.

The project is led by URI engineering professor Joseph Goodwill and funded through the federal Emerging Contaminants in Small and Disadvantaged Communities program, administered through RIDOH and the Rhode Island Infrastructure Bank. The Infrastructure Bank awarded URI $199,856 for the work, which includes analyzing data from 169 public water systems and collecting approximately 1,400 water samples statewide.

Researchers will use the results to create a heat map showing manganese concentrations in raw and treated water. They will also produce a publicly accessible treatment manual containing cost information for different removal methods, conduct case studies with selected water suppliers and test new treatment techniques designed for smaller systems with limited resources. A training seminar for water system operators, engineers and private well owners is planned for 2027.

Manganese occurs naturally in soil and rock, and New England has comparatively elevated concentrations because of the region's glacial geology. Groundwater provides drinking water to about 26% of Rhode Island residents, including customers of public systems and households with private wells.

RIDOH says small amounts of manganese are necessary for health, but prolonged exposure to elevated concentrations can cause neurological effects, particularly in infants and young children. The department uses a 0.3 milligram-per-liter do-not-drink threshold for children younger than one and 1.0 milligram per liter for older residents. Private well owners can have their water tested and install certified treatment equipment when elevated manganese is detected.

Goodwill said the project is intended to help Rhode Island move from reacting to manganese problems toward identifying vulnerable systems and selecting appropriate responses before conditions worsen.
