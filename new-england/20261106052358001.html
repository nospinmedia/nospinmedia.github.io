💰 Massachusetts voters reject changes to tax rebate formulaQuestion 5 defeat leaves existing revenue limit in place 👇

EducationNovember 6, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published November 6, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

Massachusetts voters have rejected Question 5, defeating a proposal that would have changed how the state calculates its tax revenue limit and potentially triggered taxpayer refunds more frequently.

The result leaves the state's existing Chapter 62F system in place. Enacted by voters in 1986, the law establishes a limit on how quickly state tax collections can grow relative to wages and salaries. When collections exceed the calculated limit, the excess must be returned to taxpayers. The provision was triggered in 2022, resulting in nearly $3 billion in refunds.

Question 5 proposed changing the formula used to determine that limit. It would have based allowable revenue growth on the previous year's net tax collections and the average growth in Massachusetts wages and salaries over three years. The proposal also would have changed how revenue from the state's 4% surtax on annual income above $1 million is treated when determining whether collections exceed the limit.

Supporters argued that the existing formula allows state revenue to grow too quickly and that the proposed changes would make refunds more likely when tax collections outpace income growth. They presented the measure as a way to restrain government spending and return more excess revenue to taxpayers facing high living costs.

Opponents argued that tightening the revenue limit could reduce money available for education, health care, transportation and other public services, particularly during periods when state finances fluctuate. Labor organizations and other groups opposing the measure warned that the change could create significant pressure on future state budgets.

The race remained too close to call immediately after Tuesday's election. The campaign supporting Question 5 subsequently conceded, confirming that voters had rejected the proposal. Massachusetts had a record-tying nine statewide ballot questions before voters this year.

With Question 5 defeated, no new tax is created or eliminated, and the existing system for determining when excess state revenue must be returned to taxpayers remains unchanged.
