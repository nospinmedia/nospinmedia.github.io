🦆 Vermont Begins Removing Some Artificial Loon Nesting RaftsWildlife officials say decades of conservation have helped the species recover 👇

EnvironmentAugust 16, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 16, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Wildlife biologists in Vermont have begun selectively removing some artificial loon nesting rafts after decades of conservation efforts helped restore the state's Common Loon population. The Vermont Center for Ecostudies (VCE) and the Vermont Department of Fish and Wildlife say the floating platforms were critical to the species' recovery, but improving habitat conditions now allow more loons to nest naturally on many lakes and ponds.

When Vermont launched its loon recovery program in the early 1980s, only seven breeding pairs remained statewide, placing the species on the state's endangered list. Today, more than 117 nesting pairs occupy lakes across Vermont, making the loon one of the state's most successful wildlife recovery stories. Artificial nesting rafts protected eggs from fluctuating water levels, shoreline development and predators, helping many pairs successfully raise chicks.

Conservation groups say the rafts remain highly effective, with nesting success rates of about 86%, compared with roughly 50% to 75% for natural shoreline nests. However, as the loon population has expanded, some lakes now support enough breeding pairs that artificial nesting sites can contribute to territorial conflicts. Instead of maintaining every raft indefinitely, wildlife managers are beginning a gradual, site-by-site evaluation to determine where natural nesting can once again succeed.

Officials emphasized that the strategy is not to eliminate all artificial nesting platforms. Rafts will continue to be used on lakes where they remain important for nesting success, while conservation efforts increasingly focus on protecting natural shoreline habitat, reducing disturbance during nesting season and limiting preventable threats such as lead fishing tackle. Biologists say the transition reflects the long-term success of Vermont's loon recovery program and the next phase of ensuring the species remains healthy for future generations.
