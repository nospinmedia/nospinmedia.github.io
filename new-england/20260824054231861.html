⚖️ Harvard agrees to $53M settlement over stolen donor remainsMedical school resolves lawsuits tied to morgue trafficking scandal 👇

IllustrationIllustration representing court rulings and legal proceedings.Credit: AI-generated illustration
EducationAugust 24, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 24, 2026 at 12:30 PM ET

Harvard Medical School has agreed to establish two class-action settlement funds totaling $53 million to resolve lawsuits filed by families whose loved ones' donated remains were stolen and sold by the school's former morgue manager. A Suffolk Superior Court judge in Boston granted preliminary approval of the agreement, which covers relatives and designated beneficiaries of anatomical donors whose remains were handled through the school's Anatomical Gift Program between January 1, 2018, and March 31, 2023.

The litigation stemmed from the actions of former morgue manager Cedric Lodge, who pleaded guilty to stealing and selling body parts, including heads, brains, skin and hands, from donated cadavers intended for medical education and research. Federal prosecutors said Lodge transported the remains from Harvard's morgue in Boston to his home in Goffstown, New Hampshire, where he and others sold them on the black market. He was sentenced to eight years in federal prison, while his wife, Denise Lodge, and several other participants also received prison sentences.

In announcing the settlement, Harvard Medical School Deans George Q. Daley and Bernard S. Chang called Lodge's actions a flagrant betrayal of the institution's values. In addition to the financial settlement, Harvard agreed to provide a formal statement to affected families, appoint a third-party administrator to oversee claims, continue reforms to its Anatomical Gift Program, and establish an annual scholarship honoring anatomical donors. A final approval hearing is scheduled for December 2026.

The settlement follows a Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court ruling that revived the families' negligence claims after a lower court dismissed them, concluding Harvard was not automatically shielded from liability under the state's anatomical gift law. The agreement resolves one of the most significant legal cases involving mishandled donated human remains at a U.S. medical institution.
