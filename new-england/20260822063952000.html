⚖️ Massachusetts High Court Blocks Quincy Saint StatuesSJC Keeps Religious Display Off Public Safety Building 👇

LegalAugust 22, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 22, 2026 at 2:38 PM ET

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court unanimously upheld a preliminary injunction preventing the City of Quincy from installing two 10-foot-tall bronze statues of Saint Michael the Archangel and Saint Florian outside the city's new $175 million public safety headquarters. In a decision written by Justice Scott Kafker, the court concluded that the plaintiffs are likely to succeed in arguing the display violates the religious neutrality protections of the Massachusetts Constitution, allowing the injunction to remain in place while the lawsuit continues.

The statues, commissioned by Mayor Thomas Koch, were intended to honor police officers and firefighters, as Saint Michael and Saint Florian are traditionally recognized as their patron saints. The sculptures, valued at approximately $850,000, have not been installed and will remain in storage during the litigation. The city argued that the display served a secular purpose by recognizing the service and sacrifice of first responders, but the court found that prominently displaying standalone religious figures on government property could reasonably be viewed as government endorsement of a particular faith.

The lawsuit was filed by a coalition of Quincy residents led by Claire Fitzmaurice and is supported by the ACLU of Massachusetts, Americans United for Separation of Church and State, and the Freedom From Religion Foundation. The Supreme Judicial Court affirmed an earlier ruling by Norfolk Superior Court Judge William Sullivan, who issued the original injunction while the constitutional claims are considered in greater detail.

The justices emphasized that the decision does not resolve the full lawsuit but instead determines that the plaintiffs have demonstrated a sufficient likelihood of success to justify keeping the statues off public property until the case is decided. The ruling leaves the underlying constitutional challenge for further proceedings in the trial court and continues a broader debate over the role of religious symbolism on government-owned property.
