⚛️ New England States Join Forces to Explore Nuclear Energy ExpansionSix-State Summit Focuses on New Reactors, Grid Reliability and Energy Costs 👇

EconomyOctober 10, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published October 10, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

Officials from all six New England states are working together on a regional strategy for nuclear energy, setting aside some of their differing energy policies to examine how new nuclear technologies could help meet the region's future electricity needs.

Representatives from Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Vermont gathered for a regional nuclear policy summit at UMass Lowell, organized with the National Governors Association. The meeting brought together governors' staff, state energy officials, regulators, utilities and nuclear industry representatives.

The discussions focused heavily on advanced nuclear technologies, including small modular reactors, or SMRs, that are designed to be smaller and potentially easier to deploy than traditional large nuclear plants. Officials examined regulatory requirements, potential sites, workforce needs, financing and the federal licensing process.

The collaboration comes as New England faces several interconnected energy challenges. Electricity demand is expected to grow as transportation and heating become increasingly electrified and as data centers and other large power users expand. At the same time, the region continues to experience winter constraints on natural gas supplies that can contribute to higher electricity prices during periods of extreme cold.

New England already receives a significant portion of its electricity from nuclear power, primarily through existing plants including Seabrook Station in New Hampshire and Millstone Power Station in Connecticut. Those facilities provide electricity around the clock without producing carbon emissions during generation.

The six states do not have identical energy policies. Some have aggressive statutory targets for reducing greenhouse gas emissions, while others have emphasized affordability, reliability and increasing the amount of electricity generated within the region. Despite those differences, officials at the summit identified nuclear power as an area where greater regional coordination could be useful.

The summit does not represent a commitment to build new nuclear reactors. Instead, states are examining what regulatory, financial and technical changes would be required if advanced nuclear projects become economically viable.

Officials also discussed the possibility of coordinating state policies rather than having each state independently develop rules for emerging reactor technologies. Supporters say that approach could reduce duplication and make New England more attractive for future nuclear investment.

Significant obstacles remain, including the cost of new reactors, federal licensing requirements, radioactive waste management and public acceptance. Several advanced reactor designs are still under development and have not yet been widely deployed commercially in the United States.
