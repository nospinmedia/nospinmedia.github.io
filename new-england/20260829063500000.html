🦞 Canada Spares Maine Lobster From New TariffsForest products and other exports still face trade pressure 👇

File photoShipping containers are lifted by a crane at a commercial cargo port.Credit: PickPik
EnvironmentAugust 29, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 29, 2026 at 8:45 AM ET

Canada has removed seafood, including Maine lobster, from its list of planned retaliatory tariffs, preventing a 25% duty that industry leaders warned would severely disrupt the region's cross-border lobster trade during the busy fall harvesting season. The exemption provides immediate relief for fishermen, dealers and processors who rely on Canadian facilities to handle a significant share of Maine's annual catch before it is sold to markets around the world.

The decision followed warnings from the Maine Lobstermen's Association and other industry groups that imposing tariffs on lobster would harm businesses and workers on both sides of the border because the supply chain is deeply integrated. Large volumes of Maine lobster are transported to Canada for processing before being exported, making the industry especially vulnerable to new trade barriers.

Despite the seafood exemption, many other Maine industries remain exposed to Canada's planned retaliatory tariffs scheduled to take effect on September 8. Sen. Susan Collins warned that roughly $170 million in annual Maine exports remain subject to the new duties, with the state's forest products industry representing the largest share of that trade. Collins urged Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer to resume negotiations with Canadian officials to prevent further economic damage to businesses that depend on cross-border commerce.

The tariffs are part of a broader trade dispute that escalated after negotiations between the United States and Canada broke down, prompting both countries to announce additional trade measures. While the seafood exemption eases immediate concerns for Maine's signature lobster industry, manufacturers, paper producers, agricultural businesses and energy-related sectors across northern New England could still face higher costs if the broader dispute is not resolved before the tariffs take effect.
