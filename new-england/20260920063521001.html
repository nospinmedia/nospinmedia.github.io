🌊 Massive ‘chocolate tide’ stretches across New England coastAlgae bloom threatens marine life from Maine to Massachusetts 👇

New EnglandSeptember 20, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published September 20, 2026 at 8:45 AM ET

Scientists have identified the organism behind a massive algae bloom stretching along the New England coast from Maine through New Hampshire and into Massachusetts. The bloom is caused by Karenia mikimotoi, a microscopic phytoplankton that can turn seawater brown and produce a strong odor, leading one Maine scientist to describe the phenomenon as a "chocolate tide."

Satellite observations analyzed by NOAA's National Centers for Coastal Ocean Science detected unusually high concentrations of algae along the entire Maine and New Hampshire coasts and into Massachusetts as far south as Plymouth. In Portland Harbor, water samples reached approximately 2 million Karenia mikimotoi cells per liter. Maine officials say the bloom has been particularly concentrated in Casco Bay.

Unlike some harmful algal blooms, Karenia mikimotoi is not considered a threat to human health or a shellfish-poisoning risk. It can, however, seriously harm marine ecosystems. At high concentrations, the organism can interfere with fish and shellfish feeding and respiration, while dense blooms can reduce dissolved oxygen as they break down. Maine officials have documented stress and mortality among soft-shell clams and other marine organisms in areas including Maquoit Bay, Middle Bay and Smith Cove.

The species has become more noticeable in northern New England relatively recently. Bigelow Laboratory for Ocean Sciences senior scientist Nick Record said Karenia mikimotoi was not found in Maine waters a decade ago and historically occurred farther south. Scientists say warming waters could be one factor allowing marine species to expand northward, while ship traffic can also transport organisms into new areas. Researchers have not attributed this particular bloom to a single cause.

There are signs the event may be easing. Maine officials say cell counts in Casco Bay have generally been declining, and recent satellite imagery suggests the bloom may be subsiding. Monitoring of phytoplankton and dissolved oxygen will continue until it ends.
