⚖️ Lawsuit Challenges New Hampshire Ban On Student IDs For VotingFederal case seeks relief before Sept. 8 state primary 👇

IllustrationIllustration representing court rulings and legal proceedings.Credit: AI-generated illustration
EducationAugust 16, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published August 16, 2026 at 8:45 AM ET

A voting-rights organization is asking a federal court to block New Hampshire's new ban on using student identification cards to vote, weeks before the state's September 8 primary. The Coalition for Open Democracy filed suit against Secretary of State David Scanlan and Attorney General John Formella, challenging House Bill 323, which removed qualifying high school and college IDs from the forms of identification automatically accepted when voters obtain ballots.

Gov. Kelly Ayotte signed HB 323 in April, and the law took effect June 2, 2026. Students who previously could use qualifying school-issued photo IDs must now present another accepted government-issued identification, such as a driver's license from any state, passport, military identification or state-issued non-driver ID. College students may still vote in New Hampshire if they otherwise qualify and establish the state as their voting domicile.

The lawsuit also challenges guidance issued by Scanlan that bars student IDs from being used to establish identity when registering to vote or requesting an absentee ballot. The plaintiffs argue HB 323 changed the identification rules for obtaining a ballot but did not authorize the secretary of state to extend the prohibition to voter registration. They contend the combined restrictions improperly burden younger voters and violate the First, Fourteenth and Twenty-Sixth Amendments, while the challenge to Scanlan's directive also raises state separation-of-powers claims.

Republican lawmakers who supported HB 323 argue school-issued IDs lack the security and uniformity of government identification and that the law subjects students to the same identification standard as other voters. House Election Law Committee Chair Ross Berry has defended the measure as an equal requirement rather than a restriction targeted at students.

The case is separate from the earlier challenge to HB 1569, New Hampshire's proof-of-citizenship law, which a federal judge blocked in May and the state is appealing. It remains unclear whether the new student-ID case will receive a ruling before the September 8 primary.
