📷 New Hampshire becomes model for limiting license plate camerasState's 3-minute deletion rule blocks creation of long-term vehicle tracking databases 👇

EconomySeptember 25, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published September 25, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

As states reconsider the rapid spread of automated license plate readers, New Hampshire's decade-old restrictions are drawing national attention as a possible model for balancing police use of the technology with privacy protections. The state has had some of the country's strictest rules governing automated license plate readers since 2016, sharply limiting when law enforcement can use them and how long most information can be retained.

Under New Hampshire law, automated plate readers may be used by law enforcement for specific purposes, including locating stolen vehicles, people with outstanding warrants, missing or endangered people, and vehicles connected to serious crimes. The devices must be limited to recording license plates and cannot be designed to capture images of vehicle occupants. Agencies using the technology must also receive authorization from their leadership, register the devices with the state and provide training for officers.

One of New Hampshire's strongest privacy protections is its three-minute retention limit. If a license plate scan does not lead to an arrest, citation, protective-custody action or the recovery of a missing person, the plate image generally must be deleted within three minutes. Privacy specialists say that requirement effectively prevents the creation of the large historical databases that make it possible to search where a vehicle has traveled over days or weeks.

The restrictions have limited the foothold of companies such as Flock Safety, whose systems have expanded rapidly elsewhere. However, the law does not eliminate every form of plate-reader use in New Hampshire. The state allows specific exceptions, including certain tolling operations, and The Boston Globe reported that some New Hampshire police agencies can contract for access to plate-reader databases containing information collected outside the state.

New Hampshire's approach is now attracting attention elsewhere. Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis pointed to New Hampshire earlier this month while calling for restrictions on license plate cameras in Florida, saying the state had demonstrated one way such limits could be implemented. Similar concerns about automated vehicle surveillance have emerged in Florida, Arizona, Connecticut and South Dakota.

The debate is not finished in New Hampshire either. State Rep. Michael Granger of Milton has filed a legislative request for the 2027 session dealing with surveillance on public roads and property, signaling that lawmakers could consider additional restrictions next year.

The issue centers on a basic tradeoff: license plate readers can help police locate wanted people and vehicles quickly, but retaining millions of scans can also create a detailed record of where ordinary drivers have traveled. New Hampshire's approach allows targeted law-enforcement uses while sharply limiting how much information can become part of a searchable historical database.
