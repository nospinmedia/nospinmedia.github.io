💥 MIT lab backed Army plan for Iowa explosives blastEPA finds no airborne lead as testing continues after detonation 👇

EnvironmentNovember 26, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published November 26, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

An MIT laboratory reviewed the Army's plan to destroy nearly five tons of unstable lead azide in Iowa and concluded that a controlled detonation was preferable to the danger of an accidental explosion, providing a New England connection to an operation that raised widespread environmental concerns.

MIT Lincoln Laboratory said it reviewed the plan at the request of the federal government. Based on the Army's documentation, the laboratory concluded that carrying out the explosion under controlled conditions was preferable to accepting the risks associated with a potential unplanned detonation.

The U.S. Army destroyed 68 barrels containing about 9,700 pounds of degraded lead azide in two controlled blasts at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, at around 9 a.m. Central Time on Wednesday, November 25. The material had deteriorated and crystallized in storage, making it highly sensitive and increasingly dangerous to handle.

The Army used extensive mitigation measures intended to contain lead dust, including concrete and sand barriers, gels, foam and more than 100,000 gallons of water. Residents near the facility had been advised to evacuate or shelter in place because of concerns that the detonation could create a plume containing toxic lead particles.

Initial monitoring found no airborne lead. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency reported that real-time monitors showed zero detectable airborne lead before, during and after the operation, with readings continuing to show no detection more than five hours after the blasts.

Maj. Gen. Eric P. Shirley said all 68 barrels were destroyed, with no secondary explosions and no infrastructure damage. The Army said the operation eliminated the immediate danger posed by the deteriorating explosives.

The initial air readings do not complete the environmental assessment. EPA collected more than 1,200 air, land, dust and water samples to establish baseline conditions and is conducting post-detonation sampling to determine whether lead or other material settled on surrounding soil, surfaces or water. Independent experts said laboratory analysis of those samples could take days to weeks.
