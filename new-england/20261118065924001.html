⚡ Maine electric bills are going up again in JanuaryCMP customers will pay about $60 more a year as supply costs rise 👇

EconomyNovember 18, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published November 18, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

Electric bills for many Maine households will increase beginning January 1, 2027, after state regulators approved new electricity supply rates for customers of Central Maine Power (CMP) and part of Versant Power's service territory.

The Maine Public Utilities Commission approved the new standard-offer supply rates Tuesday. An average residential CMP customer using about 550 kilowatt-hours per month will pay approximately $4.99 more each month, or about $60 more per year.

CMP's residential and small-business standard-offer rate will increase from 12.72 cents to 13.63 cents per kilowatt-hour. CMP's weighted average rate for medium business customers will decrease slightly, from 12.37 cents to 12.03 cents per kilowatt-hour. Large non-residential customers will have prices indexed to wholesale market prices and set monthly.

Customers in Versant's Maine Public District will see a smaller residential increase. An average household using about 500 kilowatt-hours per month will pay roughly $2.30 more each month, with the residential rate increasing from 14.88 cents to 15.34 cents per kilowatt-hour. Its medium-business rate will rise from 14.68 cents to 16.09 cents, while the weighted average large-business rate will edge up from 17.16 cents to 17.30 cents per kilowatt-hour.

Rates for Versant's Bangor Hydro District, which serves eastern and coastal Maine including Bangor and parts of Hancock and Washington counties, were scheduled for a separate commission deliberative session on Wednesday, November 18.

The increases involve the electricity supply portion of customers' bills, which accounts for roughly half of a typical Maine residential electric bill. The remainder includes regulated transmission and distribution charges. CMP also has a separate multi-year distribution rate request pending before regulators.

Regulators said natural-gas prices remain a major driver of New England electricity costs and cited global conflicts, including those involving Iran and Ukraine, as contributing to market volatility.
