🌲 Roadless protections at risk across 241,000 New England acresFederal proposal could reshape White Mountain forest protections 👇

EnvironmentSeptember 20, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published September 20, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

About 241,000 acres of roadless forest in New Hampshire and Maine could lose nationwide protections under a federal proposal to repeal the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule. The affected land includes roughly 235,000 acres in New Hampshire's White Mountain National Forest and another 6,000 acres extending into western Maine around the Caribou and Speckled Mountain area.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture proposed rescinding the rule in August, arguing that nationwide restrictions have limited forest managers' ability to reduce wildfire risks, address disease and insect outbreaks and carry out other forest-management work. The administration says decisions about road construction, timber harvesting and other activities should instead be made through individual national forest management plans and by officials familiar with local conditions.

Repealing the rule would not automatically authorize logging, mining or new roads in the White Mountains. Existing forest plans and other federal requirements would continue to govern the land, and individual projects could require additional environmental review. But the repeal would remove the nationwide restrictions that currently prohibit most road construction, road reconstruction and timber harvesting within designated roadless areas.

Conservation organizations oppose the proposal, arguing that road construction and expanded development could fragment one of the relatively few large, mostly unbroken forest areas remaining in New England. The protected areas cross a range of elevations and watersheds and provide habitat for wildlife including peregrine falcons, golden eagles and other species. Portions of the forest also drain toward the Saco River watershed, an important water source for communities in Maine and New Hampshire.

The Roadless Rule currently applies to more than 44 million acres of National Forest System land nationwide. USDA says removing the national restrictions would give local managers more flexibility, while conservation groups argue that the existing rule provides protections that individual management plans may not preserve.

The proposal has not been finalized. USDA recently extended the public-comment deadline from September 21 to October 6, after which the department will consider comments as it develops any final rule and supporting environmental analysis.
