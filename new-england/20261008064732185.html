🚀 Apollo software pioneer Margaret Hamilton dies at 90MIT engineer helped make the first moon landing possible with fault-tolerant code 👇

EducationOctober 8, 2026By No Spin Media Newsroom · Published October 8, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Margaret Hamilton, the pioneering computer scientist who led development of crucial onboard software for NASA's Apollo missions at MIT, died September 30 at age 90. MIT announced her death Wednesday. A cause of death was not disclosed.

Hamilton's connection to Massachusetts began in 1959, when she moved to Boston and joined MIT, initially writing weather-prediction software for meteorologist Edward Lorenz. She later worked at MIT Lincoln Laboratory before joining the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory's Apollo project in 1965 as its first programmer and first female programmer. She eventually led the teams responsible for the Lunar Module and Command and Service Module onboard flight software, overseeing work involving more than 400 people.

Her team's software played a critical role during Apollo 11's July 1969 moon landing. As the Eagle lunar module descended, its guidance computer became overloaded and generated 1202 alarms. The priority-driven software Hamilton's team had developed was designed to shed less-important work while continuing mission-critical tasks. Mission Control determined the landing could proceed, and Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin reached the lunar surface safely.

Hamilton also became a major advocate for treating software development as an engineering discipline. She described using the term “software engineering” at a time when colleagues initially treated the phrase as a joke. She later founded Higher Order Software in 1976 and Hamilton Technologies in 1986, continuing her work on reliable and error-resistant computer systems.

In 2016, President Barack Obama awarded Hamilton the Presidential Medal of Freedom, recognizing her contributions to Apollo and modern software engineering. Her Massachusetts legacy continued well beyond the moon program: MIT says Hamilton worked at the university from 1959 until the mid-1970s, and a memorial service is planned for Cambridge in the spring.
