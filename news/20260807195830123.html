Ukrainian Drone Campaign Disrupts Russia's Largest Online Retailer

Reuters analysis finds over 20% of Wildberries warehouse capacity damaged

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 7, 2026 at 10:16 AM ET

A sustained Ukrainian drone campaign has damaged or destroyed at least 1.18 million square meters of warehouse space belonging to Wildberries, Russia's largest online retailer, according to a Reuters review of satellite imagery. The analysis found that more than 20% of the company's nationwide warehouse capacity has been damaged or destroyed since attacks began in mid-July, disrupting one of Russia's largest civilian logistics networks. Ukrainian officials argue the facilities support Russia's war effort by handling dual-use goods, while Wildberries and the Kremlin deny the company supplies military equipment.

The strikes have hit approximately 20 Wildberries distribution centers stretching from western Russia to the Ural Mountains, including recent attacks near Yekaterinburg. Reuters reported that repeated drone strikes have destroyed inventory, forced warehouse closures, and disrupted deliveries across the retailer's network of roughly 98,000 pickup locations. The company says it has redirected shipments where possible, but many merchants continue reporting delayed deliveries and significant business losses.

The disruption is spreading beyond Wildberries itself. Reuters reported that more than 3,100 pickup locations have been offered for sale as parcel volumes declined, while Sberbank warned that financial pressure on merchants could increase credit losses after more than 300 affected businesses sought debt restructuring. Russian officials have discussed possible financial assistance for impacted businesses, although no nationwide support package has been announced.

The campaign reflects Ukraine's broader strategy of targeting logistics infrastructure that officials say helps sustain Russia's wartime economy. Analysts say prolonged disruptions to warehouse capacity could continue affecting supply chains, retailers, and consumers even after individual facilities reopen, making economic infrastructure an increasingly important front in the conflict.
