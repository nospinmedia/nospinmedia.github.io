U.S. Refunds $100B In Tariffs After Supreme Court Defeat

$128.68B in claims accepted as Treasury payouts continue

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 6, 2026 at 1:16 PM ET

The U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has transferred roughly $100 billion in tariff refunds to the U.S. Department of the Treasury for payment to businesses, according to a new filing with the U.S. Court of International Trade. The refunds stem from the U.S. Supreme Court's 6–3 decision in February 2026 that struck down President Trump's emergency tariff program imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA).

In a declaration dated July 31, 2026, CBP official Brandon Lord said the agency had accepted $128.68 billion in potential and certified refund claims. By that date, approximately $100 billion had already been forwarded to Treasury for disbursement. To manage the unprecedented workload, CBP launched the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) platform on April 20, 2026, allowing refunds and interest payments to be processed through a consolidated electronic system rather than individual entry-by-entry claims.

Court filings state that CAPE has processed 252,496 refund declarations covering approximately 25.1 million import entries. CBP says Treasury is continuing to issue payments as claims are verified, while some importers remain in review because of documentation requirements or additional compliance checks. Trade attorneys say the process has moved faster than many expected, although some smaller businesses continue to argue that administrative hurdles make obtaining refunds more difficult than for larger importers.

The Supreme Court's ruling limited the president's authority to impose broad country-by-country tariffs under IEEPA, while leaving tariffs imposed under other trade laws, including Section 232, unaffected. Separate legal challenges remain active. On August 3, 2026, a coalition of 25 states filed suit seeking to block newly announced 10%–12.5% tariffs imposed under a different legal authority, arguing the administration is attempting to restore duties that the courts previously rejected.
