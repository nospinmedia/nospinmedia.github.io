Federal plan would deepen Colorado River cuts for three states

Arizona, California and Nevada could lose up to 3 million acre-feet annually

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 1, 2026 at 6:31 PM ET

The U.S. Bureau of Reclamation has proposed requiring Arizona, California, and Nevada to reduce their combined use of the Colorado River by as much as 3 million acre-feet annually during the driest conditions through 2036. The reductions would be determined every two years based on river and reservoir conditions as federal officials try to prevent further declines at Lake Mead and Lake Powell.

The maximum reduction equals roughly 130 billion cubic feet of water, about the combined annual allocation of Arizona and Nevada and enough to serve more than 25 million people for a year. The proposal would divide an initial portion of the reductions using an approach previously developed by the three Lower Basin states. Additional cuts would be distributed under the existing priority system, which generally gives many California users stronger legal claims than users in Arizona and Nevada.

The four Upper Basin states—Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming—would not face mandatory reductions under the proposal for now. They could instead participate in voluntary conservation programs, primarily involving agricultural users. Lower Basin officials have argued that all seven states should accept measurable reductions, while Upper Basin leaders maintain that compulsory cuts to their current use would conflict with existing river agreements.

The federal government stepped in after the seven states repeatedly failed to reach a long-term agreement governing the river after current operating rules expire at the end of 2026. A record-dry winter left Lake Mead and Lake Powell at a combined record low, increasing concerns about water deliveries and hydroelectric generation at Hoover Dam and Glen Canyon Dam.

Officials in Arizona called the proposal economically damaging, while Nevada representatives described the potential reductions as unrealistic and severe. California officials said the proposal represents progress but emphasized that negotiations are not finished. The framework remains a federal proposal rather than a final allocation decision, and the states are expected to continue negotiating before new operating guidelines take effect.
