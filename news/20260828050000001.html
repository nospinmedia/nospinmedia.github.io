Judge freezes new USPS mail-ballot requirements

14-day order shields states as voting deadlines approach

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 28, 2026 at 11:46 AM ET

U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani temporarily blocked several key provisions of new U.S. Postal Service requirements for mail voting, putting the regulations on hold for 14 days as states prepare for the November 3, 2026 midterm elections. The ruling came after 24 states, the District of Columbia, and voting-rights organizations challenged the Postal Service rule, arguing that the agency lacks authority to regulate how states administer federal elections.

The Postal Service's final rule establishes design requirements for mail-ballot envelopes, including trackable barcodes, and requires election officials to submit lists of intended mail-ballot recipients through an online federal portal. USPS would use that information to create state-specific mail-in and absentee participation lists. The agency has said it would not determine voter eligibility, maintain voter rolls or count ballots. Talwani found that the challenging states are likely to succeed in arguing that Congress did not give USPS authority to impose the disputed election-mail requirements.

Timing was central to the judge's decision. Many states have already ordered their ballots, and North Carolina is scheduled to begin sending ballots to military and overseas voters on September 4. Talwani said states lacked sufficient time and money to redesign ballots and envelopes, obtain approvals, modify election-management systems, train officials on the USPS portal and upload required voter information before the midterms. The Justice Department argued that the rule imposes only modest information requirements and does not displace state election laws.

The case follows a 6-3 Supreme Court decision earlier this week allowing the Trump administration to proceed with parts of President Trump's March executive order after finding that an earlier challenge had been brought prematurely. The Supreme Court did not decide whether the mail-voting restrictions themselves are legal. Talwani's latest order addresses the subsequently finalized USPS rule, creating a new legal dispute that could again reach the high court. A preliminary-injunction hearing is scheduled for September 3.
