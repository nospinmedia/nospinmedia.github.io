Supreme Court blocks new mail-ballot restrictions

States can keep existing systems for 2026 midterms

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 15, 2026 at 2:31 PM ET

The U.S. Supreme Court rejected the Trump administration's emergency request to enforce new restrictions on mail-in voting ahead of the November 2026 midterm elections, leaving a lower-court injunction in place. In its brief unsigned order issued September 14, the court said the government was "unlikely to succeed on the merits" of its challenge and that the equities did not favor granting emergency relief so close to the election.

The dispute stems from an executive order issued by President Trump in March 2026 and a subsequent U.S. Postal Service rule issued in August. The USPS requirements called for states to submit information identifying voters receiving mail ballots through a federal portal and obtain approval for ballot-envelope designs using identifying barcodes. Under the rule, the Postal Service could refuse to deliver ballots that did not comply with the new requirements.

States and voting-rights organizations challenged the policy, arguing that the executive branch and Postal Service lacked authority to impose the requirements. U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Massachusetts blocked enforcement, and the 1st U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals declined to suspend her injunction while litigation continued. A second federal judge, Carl Nichols, separately blocked the rule shortly before the Supreme Court acted.

Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote separately that the Postal Service may ultimately have statutory authority to regulate the handling of election mail, but said implementing the changes for the 2026 election would violate the Administrative Procedure Act because election officials had insufficient time to comply. Justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas would have allowed the restrictions to take effect.

Election officials also pointed to the approaching September 19 federal deadline for transmitting requested ballots to military and overseas voters as evidence that election preparations were already well underway, making major last-minute changes increasingly difficult. The challenged USPS rule did not itself cover those military and overseas ballots.
