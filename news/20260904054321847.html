Judge rejects bid to remove juror in Lindsay Clancy trial

Deadlocked panel enters seventh day after dispute over reasonable doubt

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 4, 2026 at 6:15 AM ET

A Massachusetts judge refused to remove a juror from the high-profile Lindsay Clancy murder trial after the jury foreperson reported that one member was refusing to follow the court's instructions on reasonable doubt. The confrontation came during the sixth day of deliberations in Plymouth Superior Court, where jurors are deciding whether Clancy was criminally responsible when she killed her three young children in 2023. The panel returns Friday for a seventh day of deliberations.

Defense attorney Kevin Reddington asked Judge William Sullivan to dismiss the juror after receiving the foreperson's note. Sullivan instead questioned each juror individually about whether they could follow the law and then reminded the entire panel that they were required to follow his legal instructions. He reread the definition of reasonable doubt before sending them back to deliberate. Reddington renewed his objection, arguing that a juror who refused to follow the court's instructions should not be allowed to determine the outcome. Prosecutors opposed removing anyone, saying the court could not determine which side of the jury's disagreement was correct without improperly intruding into deliberations. Sullivan agreed and declined to remove the juror.

Clancy, 36, a former labor and delivery nurse, does not dispute that she strangled 5-year-old Cora, 3-year-old Dawson and 8-month-old Callan at the family's Duxbury home on January 24, 2023. The central question is her mental state. Her attorneys argue that severe postpartum psychosis left her unable to appreciate the wrongfulness of her actions and therefore not criminally responsible. Prosecutors contend that Clancy understood what she was doing and deliberately planned the killings.

The jury has repeatedly struggled to reach unanimity. After jurors reported being deadlocked for a second time, Sullivan gave them the Massachusetts Tuey-Rodriguez instruction, which directs jurors in an impasse to reconsider their positions while emphasizing that no one should surrender an honestly held judgment merely to reach a verdict. Thursday's dispute over the individual juror came after that instruction had already been given.

No mistrial has been declared, and the judge has ordered deliberations to continue. If the jury ultimately remains unable to reach a unanimous verdict, Sullivan could eventually declare a mistrial, leaving prosecutors to decide whether to try the case again.
