Pilots warned Amazon cargo jet was too fast before deadly overrun

Recorder data shows key braking systems never deployed

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 11, 2026 at 3:46 PM ET

New recorder data shows an Amazon Air cargo jet was repeatedly described as traveling too fast during its final approach to Miami International Airport before overrunning Runway 30 in a September 6 crash that killed five people. The National Transportation Safety Board says the preliminary evidence also shows the Boeing 767's speed brakes and thrust reversers never deployed.

21 Air Flight 7598, arriving from San Juan, Puerto Rico, was operated by 21 Air for Amazon. According to the cockpit voice recorder, one pilot cautioned that the aircraft was too fast about 1 minute and 42 seconds before the recording ended and made additional comments about excessive speed through the remainder of the flight. The NTSB said there was not a consistent verbal response to those comments. Automated warnings during the approach included repeated "sink rate" alerts, a "minimums" callout and multiple "too low terrain" warnings.

Flight-data recorder information shows the nose and right main landing gear touched down at 158 knots. Braking began at 146 knots, while the left main landing gear did not touch down until about 11 seconds after the initial touchdown, when the aircraft had slowed to 134 knots. Investigators have not said that the unusual landing-gear sequence caused the failure of the speed brakes or thrust reversers to deploy.

At 120 knots, the brakes were released and the throttles increased to a setting consistent with go-around thrust. Four seconds later, the throttles returned to idle and braking resumed. The recorder ended with the aircraft still moving at 65 knots, with no recorded indication that the speed brakes or thrust reversers had deployed.

The jet traveled roughly 1,300 feet beyond the paved runway, striking a van carrying workers from Professional Ocean Service Corporation and a Toyota SUV. The five people killed were in the vehicles; the two pilots survived. Runway 30 does not have an Engineered Materials Arresting System, which can help stop aircraft that overrun runways, although the airport meets federal runway-safety requirements.

The NTSB stressed that the recorder findings are preliminary and has not determined why the aircraft approached at excessive speed, why the deceleration systems were not deployed or what ultimately caused the crash.
