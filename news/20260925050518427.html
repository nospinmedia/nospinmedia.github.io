Mortgage rates climb above 7% for first time since early 2025

Average 30-year loan reaches 7.03% as borrowing costs rise

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 25, 2026 at 2:30 PM ET

The average U.S. 30-year fixed mortgage rate climbed to 7.03%, crossing the 7% threshold for the first time since January 2025 and adding another affordability challenge for Americans trying to buy a home.

Freddie Mac's weekly mortgage survey, released September 24, showed the 30-year rate rising from 6.95% a week earlier and 6.76% two weeks earlier. A year ago, the same mortgage averaged 6.30%. The average 15-year fixed mortgage also increased sharply, reaching 6.42%, up from 6.26% the previous week and 5.49% a year earlier.

The increase comes as Treasury yields have moved sharply higher. The 10-year Treasury yield, which strongly influences mortgage borrowing costs, briefly approached 5.15% Thursday, a level last reached in 2001. Treasury yields had already been rising amid concerns about inflation and federal debt, and moved higher after stronger-than-expected economic data increased expectations that the Federal Reserve could raise interest rates further.

The latest increase compounds an already difficult affordability environment. Homebuyers are confronting borrowing costs substantially higher than earlier this year while home prices remain elevated. Freddie Mac's weekly data show the 30-year mortgage averaged 5.98% in late February and 6.43% in early July before climbing steadily through August and September.

Another industry measure had already moved above 7%. The Mortgage Bankers Association reported that its average 30-year fixed mortgage rate reached 7.12% for the week ending September 18, its highest level in more than two years. The Freddie Mac and MBA figures use different methodologies, which can produce different weekly averages.

For borrowers, even relatively small rate increases can translate into substantially higher monthly payments over the life of a mortgage, particularly when combined with today's elevated home prices. Where rates move next will depend partly on inflation, economic data and changes in Treasury yields.
