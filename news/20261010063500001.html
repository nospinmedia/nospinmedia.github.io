DeSantis Withdraws Death Warrant, Halting Florida Execution

First Such Cancellation of His Governorship Comes Days Before Scheduled Execution

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 10, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

Florida Governor Ron DeSantis has withdrawn the death warrant for William Lee Thompson, halting the execution of the 74-year-old death row inmate just days before he was scheduled to die by lethal injection. The decision marks the first time DeSantis has withdrawn a death warrant since becoming governor in 2019.

Thompson had been scheduled for execution on Tuesday, October 13, for the 1976 murder of Sally Ivester, a 23-year-old woman who was kidnapped from a restaurant and taken to a Miami-Dade County motel. Thompson and another man were convicted in connection with her kidnapping, sexual assault and murder.

The unusual reversal followed questions about whether Thompson remained mentally competent to be executed. His attorneys argued that he suffers from serious cognitive decline and other health problems that prevent him from understanding why the state intends to put him to death.

Under constitutional standards established by the U.S. Supreme Court, a prisoner must have a rational understanding of the reason for an execution. After Thompson's lawyers raised concerns about his condition, DeSantis ordered a panel of three psychiatrists to evaluate his competency.

The governor subsequently withdrew the death warrant. The findings of the psychiatric panel were not publicly released, and DeSantis did not announce that Thompson's death sentence had been commuted. The action therefore stops the scheduled execution but does not remove Thompson from death row or necessarily prevent Florida from seeking another execution date in the future.

The decision stands out because Florida has carried out executions at a rapid pace during DeSantis' administration. Until Thompson's case, the governor had not withdrawn one of the death warrants he signed.

Thompson has spent nearly five decades on death row, making his case one of Florida's longest-running capital punishment proceedings. His attorneys have pursued numerous appeals over the years, including challenges involving his mental condition and the circumstances surrounding his original guilty plea and sentence.

The cancellation adds another dimension to the continuing national debate over capital punishment, particularly the execution of elderly prisoners and inmates suffering from dementia or other severe cognitive impairments.
