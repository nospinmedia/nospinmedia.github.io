U.S. growth estimate revised sharply higher to 4.3%

New data show strong third quarter as inflation remains elevated

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 26, 2026 at 11:46 AM ET

The latest estimate of U.S. economic growth was revised substantially higher for the third quarter, while a separate inflation report showed prices continuing to rise faster than the Federal Reserve’s 2% target.

The Bureau of Economic Analysis reported Wednesday that its second estimate of real gross domestic product showed the economy expanding at an annualized rate of 4.3% during the third quarter of 2026, up from the government’s initial 3.4% estimate. The new figure represents the fastest quarterly growth pace in about three years and compares with 2.2% growth in the second quarter.

The 4.3% figure is not final. GDP estimates are revised as the BEA receives more complete economic data, and another third-quarter estimate is still scheduled. Later annual updates can also revise previously reported growth.

The upward revision was driven in part by stronger nonresidential fixed investment, including increased business spending on software and AI-related infrastructure, along with continued consumer spending. Consumer spending increased at an estimated 4.2% annualized rate during the quarter.

The report also provided the government’s first estimate of third-quarter corporate profits. Profits increased by $206 billion, or 4.4% from the previous quarter, and were 21.3% higher than a year earlier.

Separately, the BEA reported that the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index increased 0.3% in October and was 3.8% higher than a year earlier. Core PCE, which excludes food and energy prices, also rose 0.3% and was 3.3% higher from a year earlier.

Personal income increased 0.7%, or $201.8 billion, partly because of one-time Medicare Improvement Fund premium rebate payments and higher dividend income. The personal saving rate was 4.1%. Consumer spending increased 0.3%, but was essentially unchanged after accounting for inflation.

The combination leaves the Federal Reserve weighing rapid estimated economic growth against inflation that remains above its target ahead of its next interest-rate decision.
