Senate Leaders Unveil Stopgap To Avert Shutdown

Funding runs through Dec. 11; House bill ends Dec. 4

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 3, 2026 at 8:46 AM ET

Key Senate negotiators announced on August 2, 2026 a bipartisan stopgap that would keep the federal government operating at current levels through December 11, 2026, aiming to head off a shutdown during the heart of campaign season. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said moving the measure before the August recess was a top priority, noting Americans have already endured two lengthy shutdowns over the last 10 months.

The Senate framework mirrors much of the House’s short-term plan but differs on timing and several policy riders. The Senate bill: - Funds agencies at current levels through December 11, 2026 - Omits a White House request for $1 billion to begin procuring parts for proposed “Trump‑class” battleships - Includes language to prevent executive transfers that would move money from other programs to the U.S. Border Patrol during the stopgap period - Delays implementation of a proposed grants rule requiring political appointee review for awards

The Senate’s approach arrives less than two months before the end of the fiscal year on September 30, 2026, and just weeks ahead of the November 2026 midterms—timing leaders say is intended to reduce brinkmanship. In the House, lawmakers passed a separate continuing resolution on July 21, 2026, by a 220–205 vote to extend funding only until December 4, 2026, setting up a reconciliation of differences if the Senate measure advances. Speaker Mike Johnson steered that bill through largely along party lines.

Negotiations were led on the Senate side by Appropriations Chair Susan Collins with Sen. Patty Murray as the lead Democrat. Their deal is designed to push full‑year appropriations into a lame‑duck session while keeping agencies—including those central to elections, security, and disaster response—on stable footing through early December. While supporters argue the package averts immediate disruptions and reins in controversial add‑ons, final passage still requires a Senate vote this week, House acceptance of any changes, and President Trump’s signature.
