Appeals Court Backs States in Prediction Market Dispute

Ninth Circuit ruling deepens split over Kalshi regulation

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 29, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

A unanimous three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled that Nevada may enforce its gambling laws against KalshiEX LLC, rejecting the company's argument that federal commodities law prevents states from regulating its sports-related prediction markets. The decision affirms a lower court order allowing the Nevada Gaming Control Board to pursue enforcement against Kalshi's unlicensed sports event contracts while litigation continues.

Writing for the panel, U.S. Circuit Judge Ryan D. Nelson concluded that sports event contracts offered through Kalshi function as sports wagers subject to state gambling laws rather than financial products shielded exclusively by the Commodity Exchange Act. The panel held that Kalshi failed to demonstrate Congress intended federal commodities regulation to preempt traditional state authority over gambling. However, the court sent the narrower question involving election-related event contracts back to U.S. District Judge Andrew Gordon for additional proceedings.

The ruling creates a direct conflict with an April 2026 decision by the Third U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which concluded that the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has exclusive authority over Kalshi's event contracts and blocked New Jersey from enforcing similar state restrictions. Because two federal appellate courts have now reached different conclusions on the same legal issue, the dispute is more likely to receive review by the U.S. Supreme Court. The Ninth Circuit panel also included Judges Bridget S. Bade and Kenneth K. Lee.

Kalshi has argued that its event contracts are federally regulated derivatives rather than gambling products, while Nevada officials maintain the contracts are sports betting subject to state licensing requirements. Similar legal disputes involving prediction markets are pending in multiple states as regulators and exchanges continue to debate whether these products fall under federal commodities law or state gaming statutes.
