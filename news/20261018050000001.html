Trump’s Russia Sanctions Waiver Draws Bipartisan Backlash

Zelenskyy Says Decision Rewards Putin

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 18, 2026 at 10:15 AM ET

President Trump’s decision to waive sweeping new sanctions against Russia has drawn criticism from Republican and Democratic lawmakers who overwhelmingly approved the measures just weeks earlier. The sanctions were scheduled to take effect October 18, targeting Russia and countries helping finance its war in Ukraine through purchases of Russian energy.

Trump signed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 into law on September 18. The law authorized sanctions against Russian officials, financial institutions and other entities, along with tariffs intended to pressure countries purchasing Russian oil and gas. An executive order issued October 16 invoked a provision of the law allowing the president to waive many of those measures when determined to be in the national interests of the United States.

The decision prompted objections from lawmakers in both parties. Republican Sen. Thom Tillis of North Carolina called the move "beyond irresponsible," while other Republicans and Democrats who supported the legislation questioned why sanctions approved with broad bipartisan backing were being set aside. Congress has limited ability to respond immediately because lawmakers are in recess until after the November 3 midterm elections.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy also criticized the decision, describing the suspension of sanctions as a reward for Russian President Vladimir Putin. Zelenskyy urged the United States to maintain economic pressure on Moscow as Russia continues attacks on Ukraine. The dispute comes amid broader tensions between Washington and Kyiv over energy policy and the direction of U.S. support for Ukraine.

The waiver does not eliminate all existing U.S. sanctions against Russia. Trump’s order specifically states that sanctions imposed under earlier executive orders addressing Russian government activities remain in effect. The new Graham Act measures can also be reconsidered later if the administration determines further action is appropriate.
