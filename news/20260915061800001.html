U.S. confirms it has weapons deployed in orbit

Air Force secretary reveals 'space control weapons' for first time

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 15, 2026 at 1:15 PM ET

Air Force Secretary Troy Meink has publicly confirmed that the United States has weapons deployed in Earth's orbit, marking the first direct acknowledgment by a senior U.S. official of a capability that had long been assumed but not openly confirmed. Speaking September 14 at the Air, Space & Cyber Conference in National Harbor, Maryland, Meink said the U.S. Space Force now has "on-orbit space control weapons" capable of defending American and allied forces against hostile actions.

Meink did not identify the weapons, explain how they operate or disclose when they became operational. That distinction is important because the term "space control weapons" can encompass multiple technologies and missions. The public disclosure does not establish whether the systems are kinetic weapons capable of physically destroying another spacecraft or non-kinetic systems that could disrupt, disable or interfere with an adversary's satellites.

The announcement comes as the Pentagon increasingly warns about military competition in space. China and Russia have developed counterspace capabilities that U.S. officials say could threaten satellites used for communications, navigation, missile warning, intelligence and other military operations. Meink said the United States must be prepared to respond to evolving threats wherever they occur, including in orbit.

The disclosure represents a notable change in how openly the Pentagon discusses American military capabilities in space. The United States has previously acknowledged ground-based counterspace systems and demonstrated the ability to destroy satellites using weapons launched from Earth, but details about operational weapons already positioned in orbit have remained highly classified.

Independent space-security experts said the disclosure appears intended partly as a deterrent but could also contribute to further military competition in orbit. Victoria Samson of the Secure World Foundation warned that China and Russia could point to the U.S. announcement to justify expanding their own offensive space programs. Analysts also cautioned against assuming what the newly acknowledged American systems can do because their specific capabilities remain classified.

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty does not categorically prohibit conventional military capabilities in orbit, although it prohibits placing nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction there and requires space activities to comply with international law.
