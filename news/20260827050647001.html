Fed's preferred inflation gauge holds at 3.7%

July data shows prices remain high as real consumer spending stalls

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 27, 2026 at 5:16 PM ET

A key measure of U.S. inflation remained stubbornly elevated in July 2026, reinforcing concerns that price pressures are proving difficult to bring back toward the Federal Reserve's 2% target. The personal consumption expenditures price index, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, increased 0.2% from June and 3.7% from a year earlier, unchanged from June's annual rate and slightly above economists' expectations. Core PCE inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy prices, also rose 0.2% for the month and remained at 3.3% year over year.

The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis reported that personal income increased $115.1 billion, or 0.4%, while consumer spending rose $36.3 billion, or 0.2%. After adjusting for inflation, however, real consumer spending increased by just $1.3 billion, or less than 0.1%, following a 0.4% increase in June. The weakness was concentrated in goods: current-dollar spending on goods fell $49.9 billion, while spending on services increased $86.2 billion.

The figures show an economy in which households continue spending more dollars even as inflation absorbs much of that increase. Persistent price pressures have been fueled in part by elevated energy costs and trade disruptions, while service-sector expenses remain an important source of inflation. The 3.7% headline PCE reading remains well above the Federal Reserve's longer-term inflation goal and complicates decisions over whether policymakers can reduce interest rates or need to keep borrowing costs elevated.

The report arrives just before Federal Reserve officials gather for the annual Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium in Wyoming. Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh is scheduled to deliver keynote remarks on August 28, giving investors and households another indication of how policymakers view persistent inflation, consumer demand and the path of interest rates heading into the fall.
