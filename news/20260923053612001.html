Powerful El Niño threatens Asian food production

Drought could push rice and palm oil prices higher worldwide

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 23, 2026 at 8:46 PM ET

A rapidly strengthening El Niño is raising concerns about drought, extreme heat and agricultural losses across Southeast Asia, with potential consequences for food supplies and prices far beyond the region. The climate pattern is expected to continue strengthening through the end of 2026, increasing the risk of weather disruptions in countries that are major producers and exporters of rice, palm oil and other commodities.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration said this month that El Niño has strengthened substantially, with sea-surface temperature anomalies exceeding 3 degrees Celsius in parts of the eastern equatorial Pacific. NOAA says there is a greater than 90% chance the event will become very strong during the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter of 2026-27, although the agency cautions that a stronger El Niño does not guarantee particular regional impacts.

Across Southeast Asia, unusually dry conditions could threaten agriculture, fisheries and water supplies. Indonesia faces heightened wildfire concerns, while reduced rainfall and falling water levels along the Mekong River could affect farming and fishing communities. Major food-producing countries including Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia and Malaysia are particularly important to international markets because the region supplies large amounts of rice and palm oil consumed worldwide.

The economic consequences remain uncertain and depend on how severely El Niño affects harvests. Goldman Sachs estimates that significant production losses could contribute to a global food-price increase exceeding 15%. Higher energy and fertilizer costs have already placed additional pressure on farmers and food systems, potentially making severe weather disruptions harder to absorb. El Niño conditions are expected to peak around December, leaving the ultimate effects on harvests and prices dependent on rainfall, temperatures and crop damage over the coming months.
