Border Construction Begins In Big Bend National Park

Bulldozers enter protected area as lawsuits and bipartisan opposition grow

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 15, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

Heavy equipment has begun clearing land near Santa Elena Canyon inside Big Bend National Park in West Texas, marking the start of on-the-ground work for the Trump administration's expanded border security project. U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) confirmed crews are conducting site preparation as part of a broader effort to strengthen infrastructure along the U.S.-Mexico border. The activity has prompted new lawsuits and criticism from conservation groups, local residents, tribal advocates, and elected officials from both political parties.

The work follows Department of Homeland Security (DHS) waivers issued earlier this year that allow border construction to proceed without complying with numerous federal environmental, cultural, and historic preservation laws that would normally apply on federal lands. According to CBP, the project includes surveys, access roads, vehicle barriers, and surveillance infrastructure rather than a continuous border wall through the national park. The administration has also awarded approximately $1.7 billion in contracts for border infrastructure across the broader Big Bend Sector.

Opponents argue the construction threatens fragile desert ecosystems, archaeological resources, culturally significant sites, and one of America's most recognizable national parks. A coalition that includes People of La Junta for Preservation, the Center for Biological Diversity, and other organizations has asked federal courts to halt the work, arguing DHS exceeded its legal authority by waiving environmental protections. At the same time, Sen. John Cornyn and dozens of members of the Texas House of Representatives have urged the administration to pause construction and consult with local communities before additional work proceeds.

The administration maintains the project is necessary to improve border security and reduce illegal crossings, while emphasizing that the current phase focuses on transportation access, detection technology, and vehicle barriers. The legal challenges are expected to continue as construction advances and federal courts consider whether DHS lawfully exercised its waiver authority inside a national park.
