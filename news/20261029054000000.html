Judge rejects DOJ bid to segregate 20,000 California ballots

Ruling follows earlier court questions over federal election authority

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 29, 2026 at 2:31 PM ET

A federal judge has rejected the Justice Department's emergency request to require California election officials to segregate mail ballots from roughly 20,000 people the federal government identified as possible noncitizens, providing a definitive ruling in a case NSM previously reported while it was still before the courts.

U.S. District Judge David O. Carter denied the request on October 28, saying the federal government had not established grounds to force California to change its election procedures while voting was already underway. The Justice Department had sought to have the ballots separated and not counted unless the voters' citizenship could be verified.

The federal government provided California officials with a list of approximately 20,000 people it said were likely noncitizens. California officials disputed the reliability of the list. According to Reuters, an initial review of 200 registrations found nearly 40% appeared to belong to people born in the United States. Justice Department officials have acknowledged that the broader list could contain U.S. citizens.

Carter also raised concerns that separating ballots could effectively disenfranchise eligible voters. The ruling followed an unsuccessful attempt by the Justice Department to obtain relief from the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals while Carter was considering the case.

The California dispute is part of a much broader federal effort targeting suspected noncitizen voting ahead of the midterms. A Reuters investigation found the Trump administration has issued more than 100 subpoenas in at least 13 states, seeking records ranging from individual voter files to entire voter rolls. The administration says the effort is intended to identify and prosecute illegal voting, which is prohibited in federal elections.

Federal prosecutors have brought individual cases involving alleged noncitizen voting, while state election officials and voting-rights groups have challenged broader federal claims about the scale of the problem. Many of the administration's voter-data cases remain in litigation.
