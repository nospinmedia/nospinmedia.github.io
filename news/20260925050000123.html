California court orders 650,000 seized ballots returned

Justices rule sheriff violated state election law

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 25, 2026 at 5:16 PM ET

The California Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Riverside County Sheriff Chad Bianco violated state election law when his department seized more than 650,000 ballots from county election officials, ordering him to return the ballots and stop handling them.

The ballots were cast in the November 4, 2025 special election involving Proposition 50, which redrew California's congressional districts. Bianco's department obtained search warrants in February 2026 after a local group, the Riverside Election Integrity Team, raised questions about differences between preliminary hand-tally estimates and the final vote count. Riverside County's registrar of voters said those preliminary figures were estimates and were not intended to match the final certified count.

In Cervantes v. Bianco, Chief Justice Patricia Guerrero wrote that California election law requires voted ballots to remain in the custody of election officials, including when law enforcement has obtained a search warrant. The court said existing election procedures allow allegations of misconduct to be investigated without removing ballots from election officials. The justices ordered Bianco to return the seized ballots to the registrar and refrain from further handling them except as needed to comply with the order.

The court also addressed errors in filings submitted by Bianco's legal team. During August 2026 oral arguments, the justices raised problems with material that had been produced with AI assistance, including a misquoted passage and an incorrect description of an earlier court ruling. The court later rebuked the errors in a footnote in its decision.

In a separate unanimous decision, Bonta v. Bianco, the court concluded that the California Constitution gives Attorney General Rob Bonta authority to issue binding instructions to county sheriffs concerning criminal investigations. The court did not decide the full extent of that authority because Bonta had directed Bianco to pause the investigation rather than end it entirely.

Bianco said he disagrees with the rulings but will comply with the court's writs. He also said he is exploring whether to seek assistance from the federal courts, leaving open the possibility of additional litigation over the dispute.
