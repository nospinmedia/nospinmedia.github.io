Southwest floods push Lake Powell up more than 20 feet

Rare rainfall boosts reservoirs but long-term water concerns remain

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 9, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

Weeks of heavy rain and flooding across the U.S. Southwest have delivered an unexpected boost to reservoirs and underground water supplies depleted by years of drought, with Lake Powell rising more than 20 feet from its mid-September low.

Storms in late October and early November sent water through normally small or dry waterways, causing destructive flooding and evacuations in some communities. Much of that runoff is now flowing into reservoirs and aquifers. Lake Powell, which straddles the Arizona-Utah border and is the nation's second-largest reservoir, gained substantially more water from the recent storms than it did from this year's spring snowmelt.

Despite the increase, Lake Powell remains just over one-quarter full, and 15 of its 17 boat launches were still unusable as of Friday. Hydrologist and climate scientist Martin Hoerling of the University of Colorado Boulder estimated that floodwaters and additional rain could ultimately add more than 1.2 million acre-feet to the reservoir, enough water to supply roughly 4 million average families for a year.

Other communities are also seeing significant gains. Around Pagosa Springs, Colorado, roughly seven inches of October rain allowed officials to lift water restrictions affecting more than 10,000 people. In northern Arizona, Upper Lake Mary, which can provide as much as 40% of Flagstaff's water, has received more than 3 billion gallons since early October.

The improvement is not expected to end the Southwest's long-term water problems. The region remains in drought, and federal officials say substantial uncertainty remains about future supplies. The Bureau of Reclamation is unlikely to change planned releases from Lake Powell until officials know how the winter snowpack develops, typically around April.

Under an existing federal plan, California, Nevada and Arizona are scheduled to collectively reduce Colorado River use by 1.25 million acre-feet annually for the next two years, with larger reductions possible depending on conditions. Arizona and Nevada are challenging that plan in court.
