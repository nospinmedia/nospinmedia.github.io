Federal court says Constitution guarantees neither clean water nor truthful contamination warnings

Ruling rejects claims from Jackson residents allegedly exposed to lead

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 9, 2026 at 6:31 PM ET

A federal appeals court has ruled that the U.S. Constitution does not guarantee people a right to clean municipal water or truthful information from public officials about contaminants in that water, rejecting constitutional claims brought by residents of Jackson, Mississippi who alleged they were exposed to lead-contaminated drinking water.

The full U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit issued the ruling September 4 in Sterling v. City of Jackson. Residents alleged that city actions and failures caused lead to leach into the water supply and that officials then told the public the water was safe despite allegedly knowing it likely was not. The plaintiffs said those assurances induced residents to continue drinking contaminated water. Several children of one plaintiff were diagnosed with lead poisoning, while other plaintiffs alleged symptoms associated with lead poisoning or other contamination.

The court held that neither protection from government-caused contaminated water nor a right to truthful information about contaminants is sufficiently rooted in American history and tradition to qualify as a substantive right protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. The majority said the Constitution does not provide a remedy for every government wrongdoing and declined to allow the residents' claims under 42 U.S.C. §1983.

The decision does not eliminate federal or state drinking-water laws or allow municipalities to ignore existing environmental regulations. The majority said residents may still pursue remedies through tort claims, legislation and existing regulatory systems. State and federal agencies have also intervened in Jackson's troubled water system through investigations, regulations and compliance efforts.

The ruling reversed the direction taken by an earlier Fifth Circuit panel and rejected reasoning used by the Sixth Circuit in similar contaminated-water litigation. Because the Fifth Circuit covers Mississippi, Louisiana and Texas, the decision directly governs federal constitutional claims within those states unless further court action changes it.
