Ukraine says combat laser downed Russian jet-powered drone

Blysk system records reported world-first intercept during operational trials

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 26, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

Ukraine says a domestically developed combat laser has shot down a Russian jet-powered attack drone, an interception Ukrainian officials and defense outlets describe as the first known battlefield use of a laser to destroy a jet-powered drone.

Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov, a defense technology adviser to Ukraine’s president, announced the intercept on Wednesday, November 25, and released video footage that he said showed the engagement. According to Beskrestnov, Ukraine’s Blysk laser air-defense system destroyed a Russian Geran one-way attack drone while protecting critical infrastructure.

The exact date and location of the interception have not been disclosed, and the reported world-first designation comes from Ukrainian officials and reporting based on their announcement. The claim has not been independently verified under battlefield conditions.

Blysk was developed by Ukrainian company Carmine Sky with support from the country’s Brave1 defense-technology ecosystem. The system was placed on combat duty in Kyiv in early November to help protect critical infrastructure, but persistent fog and low cloud cover initially prevented it from engaging targets. Those conditions illustrate an important limitation of laser weapons because moisture and other atmospheric conditions can weaken or scatter the beam.

Technical reporting describes Blysk as using a roughly 4-kilowatt laser with an engagement range of up to approximately 1 kilometer. The system focuses energy onto a target long enough to damage critical components rather than firing a missile or projectile.

If proven reliable, lasers could significantly change the economics of defending against mass drone attacks. Individual engagements primarily require electrical power rather than costly air-defense missiles or expendable interceptor drones, potentially allowing many targets to be engaged at comparatively low cost.

However, Beskrestnov cautioned that the technology remains complex and expensive to develop, and it is too early to discuss mass production. Weather, range, maintaining the beam on fast-moving targets and the initial cost of the equipment remain significant challenges.
