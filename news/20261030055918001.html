Judge blocks part of Medicaid work requirement rule

Ruling protects broader exemptions for medically frail recipients

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 30, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

A federal judge in Boston has blocked part of the Trump administration's rules for determining which Medicaid recipients qualify as medically frail, rejecting a requirement that people with certain serious health conditions also prove those conditions significantly impair their ability to meet new work requirements.

U.S. District Judge Richard Stearns ruled Thursday in a lawsuit brought by 25 states and the District of Columbia against guidance issued by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. The dispute stems from Medicaid changes enacted in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which President Trump signed into law in 2025.

Under the law, the medically frail category includes people who are blind or disabled, have substance use disorders or disabling mental disorders, have certain disabilities affecting daily activities, or have serious or complex medical conditions. CMS added a requirement that people in those categories demonstrate that their condition also significantly impairs their ability to comply with the work rules. Stearns ruled that additional restriction went beyond what the law authorized.

The decision does not eliminate Medicaid's new work requirements. Beginning January 1, 2027, most Medicaid expansion enrollees ages 19 to 64 must generally work or perform community service for at least 80 hours per month, or attend school at least half-time, unless they qualify for an exemption.

The ruling was also only a partial victory for the states. Stearns upheld a federal requirement preventing states from relying on medical-frailty evidence that is more than 12 months old. He also upheld a rule generally limiting recipients to one self-attestation of medical frailty during a continuous enrollment period.

The court found that states could face substantial costs changing their eligibility systems and that applying the narrower definition could cause otherwise eligible people to lose Medicaid coverage. CMS declined to comment following the ruling.
