FAA contractor took hours to locate failure behind ground stop

New details raise questions about monitoring and oversight during network upgrade

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 19, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

The contractor operating the telecommunications network behind the nation's air traffic control system took several hours to pinpoint the failure that triggered last Friday's nationwide ground stop, as new reporting raises additional questions about oversight of the network upgrade.

L3Harris Technologies operates the network operations center responsible for monitoring the FAA's telecommunications infrastructure. Sources told CBS News that L3Harris could not immediately identify the IP address where the problem originated despite monitoring systems intended to identify network failures in real time.

Investigators ultimately traced the disruption to El Dorado, Arkansas, where a new Juniper Networks router had been installed as part of a project replacing aging copper connections with fiber. The router generated roughly 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, destabilizing communications and radar feeds and contributing to a nationwide departure halt lasting more than four hours.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the ground stop could have ended sooner if L3Harris had located and isolated the problem faster. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford has said the agency ultimately bears responsibility for the system.

Additional reporting from CNN found that FAA technicians exercised minimal direct oversight during the contractor-led upgrade. Sources familiar with the work described the process as largely hands-off compared with previous upgrade projects, when FAA technicians more closely supervised maintenance.

The FAA has now disabled the same functionality on more than 60 other Juniper routers already installed across the system. Officials said 68 previous copper-to-fiber upgrades had been completed without a similar incident. Juniper Networks said it believes the device functioned as configured by the installer, while investigators continue examining whether configuration, hardware or interaction with older equipment caused the failure.

The outage also drew scrutiny at a House hearing Wednesday, where Rep. Bennie Thompson questioned Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin about taking a government aircraft to Baton Rouge the following day while travelers were still dealing with disruptions. Mullin defended the trip and said he reimbursed personal costs.

The new findings shift the investigation beyond the failed router itself to how the upgrade was supervised and why a contractor monitoring critical national aviation infrastructure needed hours to locate the problem.
