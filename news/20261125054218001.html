Canada says it 'doesn't report to the United States'

Defense minister pushes back after Pentagon criticizes military readiness

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 25, 2026 at 1:15 PM ET

Canadian Defense Minister David McGuinty pushed back against U.S. criticism of Canada's military readiness and spending plans, declaring that decisions about the country's defense policy belong to Ottawa.

Speaking to reporters on Parliament Hill on Tuesday, November 24, McGuinty responded to a Pentagon fact sheet titled "Canadian Defense Shortfalls." "Canada is a sovereign nation. I'll be very direct. We don't report to the United States," McGuinty said.

The Pentagon cited serviceability rates of 41.9% for Royal Canadian Air Force fleets, 53.9% for the navy and 48% for the army as evidence of readiness problems. It also criticized Canada's continuing review of its planned purchase of 88 F-35 fighter jets, warning that operating a mixed fighter fleet could create additional personnel, training and logistical demands.

Canada has committed to purchasing the first 16 F-35s, while Prime Minister Mark Carney's government continues evaluating whether to complete the full order. Sweden's Saab Gripen is among the alternatives being considered for the remaining aircraft.

The disagreement also involves defense spending. Canada has reached NATO's previous benchmark of 2% of GDP and joined other alliance members in pledging to reach a broader 5% defense and security target by 2035. Canada's current budget projects combined defense and security spending reaching 4.1% of GDP by 2027–28, including about 2.6% of GDP, or roughly $99 billion, for core defense.

Despite the unusually public dispute, McGuinty said operational cooperation with the United States remains unchanged. The two countries jointly operate NORAD, are both members of NATO, and maintain extensive military and intelligence ties. McGuinty said cooperation involving NORAD, military-to-military relationships and integrated air and missile defense continues.

The exchange comes as Canada reviews military procurement and seeks to diversify some defense relationships, while Washington presses allies for greater defense spending and readiness.
