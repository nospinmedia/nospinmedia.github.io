Dormant terrorism court used for first U.S. deportation

Afghan permanent resident removed after conceding 'alien terrorist' status

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 13, 2026 at 2:30 PM ET

The United States has carried out the first deportation through the Alien Terrorist Removal Court, activating a specialized federal tribunal that had never handled a case since Congress created it in 1996. Nazira Haji Zada, 47, an Afghan national and lawful permanent resident who lived in Fort Worth, Texas, was deported to Afghanistan after conceding that she met the statutory definition of an "alien terrorist" and waiving her right to appeal the removal order.

The Justice Department alleged that Zada supported family members who planned an ISIS-inspired mass shooting on Election Day in 2024. Her son, Abdullah Haji Zada, and son-in-law, Nasir Ahmad Tawhedi, were arrested after purchasing two AK-47-style rifles and ammunition from an undercover FBI employee. Abdullah was later sentenced to 15 years in federal prison, while Tawhedi pleaded guilty to terrorism-related offenses and is awaiting sentencing. Nazira Haji Zada herself was never criminally charged.

The government filed its removal application on July 15, 2026, and Alien Terrorist Removal Court Chief Judge Joan N. Ericksen held the court's first public hearing on July 30. The government relied in part on classified information and also provided Zada's attorneys with approximately half a terabyte of documents supporting its case. The court issued the removal order on August 20, and the records were unsealed on September 11 after she had been returned to Afghanistan. She is now permanently barred from entering the United States.

The case has also raised an unresolved constitutional issue because the tribunal operates differently from a criminal court. The government needs to establish removability by a preponderance of the evidence, rather than the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and classified evidence can be used under special procedures. Zada's federal public defenders argued that denying her access to classified evidence used against her violated due process, and said her agreement to removal should not be interpreted as accepting the court's legitimacy.
