FAA expects U.S. air travel to normalize by late Sunday

Router failure during network upgrade caused nationwide disruption

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 15, 2026 at 8:46 AM ET

The Federal Aviation Administration expects U.S. air travel to return to normal by late Sunday, ending several days of widespread cancellations and delays that began when a communications failure forced the agency to halt departures nationwide.

The ground stop began Friday after problems developed during work on the telecommunications network used by air traffic controllers. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said contractor L3Harris Technologies was replacing copper wiring with fiber-optic connections when a newly installed router began repeatedly attempting to connect with older equipment across the network.

Federal officials said the router generated more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, overwhelming legacy switches and communications equipment supporting radar and other air traffic control functions. The resulting network instability led the FAA to stop departing flights nationwide while technicians worked to restore reliable communications. Aircraft already airborne were generally allowed to continue to their destinations and land.

Officials have said there is no evidence of a cyberattack or other malicious activity. Investigators instead are examining the equipment failure, the network upgrade and the work performed by L3Harris to determine precisely how the installation produced such a widespread disruption.

Although the nationwide ground stop lasted roughly four hours, its effects continued long after departures resumed. Airlines were left with aircraft and crews out of position, passengers requiring rebooking and airports facing large backlogs. Thousands of flights were canceled or delayed as carriers worked through those problems into the weekend.

FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the aviation system was making steady progress and should largely normalize by late Sunday. Travelers were still being advised to check their flight status before heading to airports because individual cancellations and delays could continue even after normal air traffic control operations were restored.

The failure has also intensified scrutiny of the country's aging air traffic control infrastructure. Duffy is now calling for $50 billion to modernize the system, arguing that decades-old equipment—including communications technology still dependent on copper wiring—needs to be replaced on a much larger scale.

The incident demonstrated how problems involving equipment at a single telecommunications facility can cascade through the interconnected national aviation network. Tracking data showed U.S. airspace gradually emptying during the ground stop as aircraft already flying reached their destinations without new departures replacing them.

For travelers, the immediate problem has shifted from the communications failure itself to clearing the remaining backlog of canceled flights, displaced aircraft, crew scheduling problems and passenger rebookings.
