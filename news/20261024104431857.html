President Trump Removes 50% Tariffs From Some Canadian Goods

Changes Take Effect Days Before Election as Susan Collins Faces Tight Maine Senate Race

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 24, 2026 at 10:15 AM ET

President Donald Trump has removed 50% tariffs from selected Canadian agricultural and forestry products, providing relief to some U.S. businesses that rely on Canadian supplies just days before the November 3 midterm elections.

President Trump signed the order Friday, with the exemptions scheduled to take effect October 30. Products receiving relief include plywood sheets, some paper products, certain machinery and refrigeration equipment, packing crates and boxes, and fresh and dried seaweed.

The change is particularly significant in Maine, where Republican Sen. Susan Collins is locked in a close reelection race against Democrat Troy Jackson. Collins had urged the White House to provide tariff relief for products used by Maine businesses and farmers.

Collins said the exemptions would help Maine's blueberry, potato, apple and seaweed producers, some of which rely on Canadian equipment and materials that are difficult or expensive to replace with U.S.-made alternatives.

The order does not represent a broad end to President Trump's Canadian tariffs. The administration simultaneously added other Canadian products to the 50% tariff list, including carpets, paints, shampoos, cocoa powder, wooden coffins, air-conditioning equipment, chest freezers, archery equipment and canoes.

President Trump imposed 50% tariffs on about $20 billion of Canadian imports in August after trade negotiations between the two countries broke down. Canada responded in September with tariffs ranging from 15% to 50% on approximately $20 billion in U.S. goods.

The timing has added a political dimension to the policy change. Collins welcomed the relief, while Jackson argued that the administration's broader trade policies had already increased costs for Maine residents and businesses. Republicans are also defending closely contested Senate seats in other northern states where businesses have extensive economic ties with Canada.

Canada's government said it was reviewing the latest changes and remained willing to negotiate with Washington.
