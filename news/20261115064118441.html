UPS cargo plane and Copa passenger jet collide at Miami airport

Pilot reports missing wingtip as FAA investigates taxiway crash

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 15, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

A UPS cargo plane and a Copa Airlines passenger jet collided at low speed on a taxiway at Miami International Airport late Saturday, leaving two people with minor injuries and prompting a federal investigation into how the two aircraft crossed paths.

The collision involved UPS Flight 358, a Boeing 767 cargo aircraft preparing to depart for Bogotá, Colombia, and Copa Airlines Flight 427, a Boeing 737 that had arrived from Panama City, Panama. The Copa flight was carrying 166 passengers and six crew members.

According to preliminary information and air traffic control communications, the aircraft came into contact near the intersection of two taxiways. The Copa jet's left wingtip struck the nose cone of the UPS aircraft, leaving visible damage that included a hole and crumpled panels on the cargo plane's nose.

Air traffic control audio released after the collision captured the Copa pilot reporting the damage to controllers and saying the aircraft appeared to be missing its left wingtip. The collision occurred while both aircraft were on the ground; neither was taking off or landing at the time.

Miami-Dade Fire Rescue responded and treated two people for minor injuries. Neither required hospitalization. All 172 passengers and crew members aboard the Copa aircraft were able to leave the plane safely, and no serious injuries were reported.

Miami International Airport spokesperson Greg Chin said the collision did not significantly disrupt airport operations. Other aircraft were redirected around the affected area using alternative taxiways while emergency crews responded and the damaged planes remained in place.

The Federal Aviation Administration has opened an investigation. Preliminary information indicates air traffic controllers had instructed the UPS crew to yield while the Copa aircraft was taxiing, but investigators will determine the exact sequence of events and whether the instructions were correctly received and followed. No final determination of responsibility has been made.

Both aircraft will require inspections to determine the full extent of the damage before returning to service. The visible damage to the Copa wingtip and UPS nose provides investigators with physical evidence to help establish the aircraft's positions and movements at the moment of impact.

The collision was separate from the nationwide FAA communications failure that disrupted U.S. air travel beginning Friday. There is no indication the Miami incident was connected to that outage.
