CDC warns of surge in animal rabies and human exposures

Vaccine use jumps as officials report mass exposure events

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 11, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has issued a national health advisory after detecting increased animal rabies activity and human exposures this summer, including mass exposure events involving hundreds of people. The agency is also warning health care providers about errors in administering the treatment used to prevent rabies after exposure.

The CDC reported a 17% increase in rabies-related inquiries during July and August 2026 compared with the same period in 2025. Pharmacy data through September 2 showed rabies vaccine utilization increased 33%, while use of human rabies immune globulin, or HRIG, jumped 76% from the same period last year. At least eight state health departments reported notable increases in post-exposure prophylaxis use, problems administering the treatment correctly, or both.

One of the largest recent incidents involved three baby goats from a mobile petting zoo in North Carolina that tested positive for rabies after exposure to a rabid skunk. Public health officials recommended rabies post-exposure prophylaxis, known as PEP, for more than 260 people who potentially had contact with the animals. The CDC cited that event along with other recent animal exposures as evidence of unusually high activity requiring increased awareness from clinicians and the public.

The agency also highlighted mistakes involving PEP, including injections given in the gluteal area, failure to administer HRIG when required and failure to place HRIG appropriately around wounds. Correct treatment is critical because rabies is nearly always fatal after symptoms appear but is preventable when appropriate care is provided promptly after an exposure.

People who are bitten or scratched by a potentially rabid animal should immediately wash the wound thoroughly with soap and water and contact a health care provider or public health department. The CDC also recommends avoiding direct contact with wild animals and keeping pets current on rabies vaccinations.

The increase does not mean everyone who encounters a wild animal needs rabies treatment. Instead, the CDC is asking clinicians and health departments to carefully assess potential exposures and ensure that people who need PEP receive it correctly.
