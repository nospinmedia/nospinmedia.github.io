U.S. inflation rises 0.4% as gasoline prices jump

August report raises odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 12, 2026 at 1:16 PM ET

U.S. consumer prices rose 0.4% in August, accelerating from a 0.1% increase in July as higher gasoline costs drove much of the monthly increase, according to the latest Consumer Price Index report. Inflation was 3.4% over the past 12 months, unchanged from July and still above the Federal Reserve's 2% inflation target.

Gasoline prices climbed 3.9% in August and accounted for more than one-third of the overall monthly CPI increase. The broader energy index rose 2.1% for the month and was 16.3% higher than a year earlier. Gasoline prices were 27.4% higher than a year ago. Food prices increased a comparatively modest 0.1% in August, while shelter costs rose 0.3%.

Inflation excluding food and energy also showed renewed monthly pressure. Core CPI increased 0.3% in August after rising 0.2% in July. Over the past year, however, core inflation eased to 2.4% from 2.5%, its lowest annual rate in several years. Airline fares rose 2.7% during August and were 23.4% higher than a year earlier, while transportation services increased 0.5% for the month.

The report arrives just before the Federal Reserve's September 15-16 policy meeting. Expectations for another interest-rate increase strengthened following the CPI release, with financial markets assigning roughly an 86% probability to a quarter-percentage-point increase. The Fed's current target range is 3.5% to 3.75%. Chair Kevin Warsh has emphasized the need for inflation to continue moving toward the central bank's target, while economists remain concerned that elevated energy costs could spread into prices for other goods and services.

The August figures also underscore how quickly fuel prices can affect household expenses even when underlying annual inflation is gradually moderating. Energy prices have risen sharply amid disruptions associated with the conflict in the Middle East, adding another source of uncertainty for consumers and Federal Reserve policymakers.
