Judge accuses DOJ of 'weaponization'

Case against two former FBI officials dismissed

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 3, 2026 at 8:46 PM ET

A federal magistrate judge dismissed criminal charges against two former FBI officials accused of lying to Congress about their knowledge of investigations involving Hunter Biden, sharply criticizing the Justice Department's handling and timing of the case.

U.S. Magistrate Judge Zia M. Faruqui dismissed the case against former FBI officials Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides after a preliminary hearing examining whether prosecutors had established probable cause. The two were charged about two weeks earlier with making false statements during congressional testimony in 2023.

Faruqui described the government's investigation as rushed and containing significant errors. In announcing his ruling from the bench Monday, he said he was deeply troubled by what had occurred and said the government's actions could not be described as anything other than “weaponization.”

The judge also questioned the timing. He noted that the statements at issue were made three years earlier, while prosecutors filed charges approximately two weeks before the 2026 midterm elections. Faruqui cited Justice Department policy against timing investigative or prosecutorial actions for the purpose of affecting an election and said no apparent emergency required prosecutors to proceed through a criminal complaint immediately before Election Day.

The dismissal does not necessarily end the investigation. Faruqui said prosecutors remain free to seek an indictment from a grand jury, and the government can challenge his ruling. The Justice Department said it disagreed with the decision and was evaluating its options, while maintaining that its actions were based on facts, evidence and law rather than politics.

The ruling is notably different from some initial descriptions of the case: the defendants were Floris and Benavides, not former FBI leaders Andrew McCabe and William Priestap, and Faruqui's ruling did not permanently prohibit prosecutors from pursuing charges.
