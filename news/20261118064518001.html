Measles surge puts Trump administration's vaccine response under scrutiny

U.S. loses elimination status after 26 years as cases reach highest level since 1991

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 18, 2026 at 10:16 AM ET

The United States has officially lost its measles-elimination status after 26 years, as the country's worst year for measles since 1991 intensifies scrutiny of vaccination rates and the Trump administration's response to the outbreaks.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) announced the change on Tuesday, November 17, after determining that measles had again spread continuously in the United States for at least 12 months. Mexico also lost elimination status, following Canada's loss in November 2025, meaning measles elimination has now been lost across North America.

More than 5,400 U.S. cases and 10 deaths have been reported in 2026, with at least five fatalities involving children. Major outbreaks have occurred in several states, including Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin. The 2026 total is more than double the roughly 2,200 cases reported last year.

CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz said the agency has invested $100 million in state and local health departments to combat transmission, adding, "The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does."

The federal response has drawn political and public-health scrutiny. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has supported vaccination against measles while also making statements about vaccines that have conflicted with established public-health recommendations. President Donald Trump also directed federal health officials in August to pursue separating the combined MMR vaccine into individual measles, mumps and rubella shots.

The CDC continues to recommend MMR vaccination and says losing elimination status does not mean everyone needs another dose. People uncertain about their protection are advised to verify their vaccination records or other evidence of immunity, with additional recommendations possible during outbreaks or for higher-risk groups.
