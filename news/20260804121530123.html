CBP Halts New Well Drilling for New Mexico Border Wall

Permit concerns and rancher warnings spur pause as water plan is drafted

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 5, 2026 at 8:46 PM ET

U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) ordered border wall contractors to stop drilling new groundwater wells in drought‑stricken New Mexico on August 3, 2026, responding to ranchers’ warnings that industrial pumping could deplete local aquifers. The pause covers wells serving segments of President Trump’s barrier in Luna County, where crews had tapped groundwater to mix concrete and control dust.

The directive comes as the multistate barrier effort—backed by more than $46 billion in federal funding—advances across four states. Contractors working the New Mexico stretch include Caddell Construction Co., Fisher Industries, Barnard Construction, and Spencer Construction Co. CBP said it will coordinate with the U.S. Department of Agriculture and New Mexico officials on a sustainable groundwater strategy. The agency did not say whether already‑drilled wells might still be pumped. Many of the worksites sit on a federally controlled strip along the border known as the Roosevelt Reservation.

Local ranchers say the scale of extraction threatened scarce supplies. Russell Johnson, president of the Luna County Farm and Livestock Bureau, said drilling crews told him a nearby industrial well could draw up to 300 gallons per minute, while his roughly dozen ranch wells typically produce 3 to 7 gallons per minute. The New Mexico Office of the State Engineer said staff have identified at least eight wells drilled for wall work without state permission and observed two pumping about 200 gallons per minute. The office reiterated that all non‑tribal groundwater wells in the state require permits, regardless of whether they are located on federal or state land.

Ranch groups, including the New Mexico Cattle Growers’ Association, argue that large‑scale pumping during severe drought could permanently damage local basins. Some ranchers noted that during earlier construction phases, builders sometimes purchased water from farms or municipalities rather than developing new wells—an approach they say could reduce stress on fragile aquifers.
