New laws revive fights over Holocaust-era artwork

Families seek works held in Pasadena and Auschwitz

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 20, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

Two lawsuits filed in Los Angeles are testing new state and federal protections intended to help Holocaust survivors and their heirs pursue claims involving artwork taken or withheld during the Nazi era. The cases seek works held by the Norton Simon Museum in Pasadena and the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum in Poland and are the first such lawsuits filed after recent changes in California and federal law strengthened avenues for restitution claims.

The Jewish Federation of Greater Los Angeles, acting on rights assigned by Marei von Saher, the sole heir of Dutch Jewish art dealer Jacques Goudstikker, is seeking the return of Lucas Cranach the Elder's 1530 diptych Adam and Eve. Goudstikker fled the Netherlands after the German invasion in 1940, and much of his collection was seized by Nazi leader Hermann Göring. The Norton Simon Museum maintains that it legally owns the paintings, pointing to a 2018 federal appeals court ruling that upheld its title. The Supreme Court declined to hear the earlier dispute in 2019.

A separate lawsuit was filed by Michele Babbitt Kane and Karin Wendy Babbitt, daughters of Holocaust survivor Dinah Gottliebova Babbitt. While imprisoned at Auschwitz, Babbitt was forced by Nazi physician Josef Mengele to paint watercolor portraits of Roma prisoners. Her daughters are seeking the works from the Auschwitz museum. Museum spokesperson Pawel Sawicki disputes characterizing those watercolors as Nazi-looted art, arguing that they are irreplaceable documentation of Mengele's crimes and should remain at the memorial.

The cases follow California Assembly Bill 2867, signed in 2024, which strengthened California residents' ability to pursue Holocaust-era art claims under state law. Congress also updated the federal Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act in 2025, permanently removing its expiration date. Supporters say the changes are intended to prevent procedural barriers from ending restitution cases before courts consider the underlying ownership claims.
