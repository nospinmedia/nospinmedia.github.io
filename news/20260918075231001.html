Bank of Japan raises interest rate to 31-year high

Policy rate reaches 1.25% as officials confront persistent inflation

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 18, 2026 at 10:15 AM ET

The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 1.25% on Friday, its highest level in 31 years, continuing Japan's move away from decades of exceptionally low borrowing costs as policymakers confront persistent inflation. The quarter-point increase from 1.0% was approved by a 7-2 vote at the central bank's September policy meeting.

The decision represents another step in a historic shift for Japan, which maintained near-zero or negative interest rates for years while struggling to generate sustained inflation. The Bank of Japan ended its negative-rate policy in 2024 and has gradually increased rates as wages and prices have risen.

Inflation remains complicated. Japan's core consumer prices increased 1.7% in August from a year earlier, below the central bank's 2% target, but another measure excluding both fresh food and energy rose 1.9%. Policymakers are also watching higher energy costs, wage growth and inflation expectations for signs that underlying price pressures could move above the target.

Governor Kazuo Ueda said future rate increases will depend on how inflation develops and indicated that larger or consecutive increases remain possible if conditions warrant them. He also said monetary conditions remain accommodative despite Friday's increase, while acknowledging uncertainty over how high rates ultimately need to rise.

Financial markets reacted unexpectedly. Rather than strengthening after the rate increase, the yen weakened to a two-week low against the U.S. dollar, as investors focused on the two dissenting policymakers and the absence of a strong signal that another increase would come quickly. The dollar rose more than 1% against the yen following the decision.

Japan's monetary policy can have effects beyond its borders because years of extremely low Japanese rates encouraged investors to borrow cheaply in yen and invest in higher-yielding assets elsewhere. As Japanese rates rise, changes in those investment flows can influence currencies, bonds and other financial markets around the world.
