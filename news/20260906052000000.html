Bipartisan backlash grows against Flock license-plate cameras

States and local governments move to restrict surveillance network

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 6, 2026 at 5:16 PM ET

Opposition to automated license-plate readers operated by Flock Safety is spreading across party lines as governors, lawmakers and local governments move to restrict the surveillance technology. The cameras photograph license plates and collect other vehicle information, allowing law enforcement agencies to search data across jurisdictions and potentially track a vehicle's movements over time. Police agencies say the systems help locate stolen vehicles, missing people and criminal suspects, while critics cite privacy risks and documented misuse by officers.

Florida Republican Gov. Ron DeSantis recently backed the removal of license-plate readers from state highway rights-of-way, saying he does not want Florida to become a "surveillance state." The Florida Department of Transportation revoked existing permits on August 31 and gave agencies 30 days to remove affected cameras. Pennsylvania Democratic Gov. Josh Shapiro, meanwhile, said he would welcome bipartisan legislation banning Flock cameras statewide. Texas Republican Gov. Greg Abbott has also frozen state spending for the cameras, while Republican senators have opened scrutiny of police misuse.

The backlash is also reaching local governments. Knox County, Tennessee, commissioners voted unanimously to prohibit agreements involving stationary automated license-plate readers and require existing cameras to be deactivated and removed within 30 days. Associated data must also be removed unless it is connected to an active criminal investigation. In Florida, Palm Beach County commissioners separately paused new permits for automated cameras on county roads while officials examine privacy and data-retention concerns.

Flock cameras capture billions of license plates every month, according to NPR's reporting. Documented abuses have included officers using license-plate databases to track former partners, prompting Flock to change some system features intended to prevent misuse. The debate is increasingly entering 2026 midterm election campaigns in states including Florida, Ohio and Wisconsin. Supporters continue to point to criminal investigations in which the technology has helped police identify or locate suspects, leaving governments to weigh those public-safety benefits against privacy and oversight concerns.
