Supreme Court takes up major climate liability fight

Boulder case could reshape lawsuits against oil companies

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 4, 2026 at 6:31 PM ET

The U.S. Supreme Court will hear arguments Monday, October 5, in a major climate liability case that could affect lawsuits brought by state and local governments against oil and gas companies across the country. The dispute pits the city and county of Boulder, Colorado, against ExxonMobil and Suncor Energy over whether Colorado-based claims seeking compensation for climate-related costs can proceed under state law.

Boulder first sued the companies in 2018, accusing them of misleading the public about the climate effects of fossil fuels and seeking money to help cover local adaptation and recovery costs. The lawsuit predates the 2021 Marshall Fire, which destroyed more than 1,000 homes and other structures, killed two people and caused more than $2 billion in damage, making it Colorado's costliest wildfire. Climate change was considered a factor in conditions surrounding the disaster.

The central legal fight extends far beyond Colorado. ExxonMobil and Suncor argue that claims involving global greenhouse gas emissions are governed by federal law rather than state law. Boulder argues it is not attempting to regulate emissions nationally, but instead seeking compensation under Colorado law for costs being experienced locally. The Trump administration is supporting the oil companies' position.

The outcome could influence dozens of similar lawsuits in which governments are seeking to hold fossil fuel companies financially responsible for climate-related damages. The justices could also avoid deciding the broader question at this stage by concluding that the Supreme Court does not currently have jurisdiction to resolve it.

Only eight justices will participate. Justice Samuel Alito recused himself shortly before arguments after facing calls to step aside because of his financial holdings in oil companies. His absence also creates the possibility of a 4-4 split, which would leave the lower-court result in place without establishing a nationwide Supreme Court precedent.
