DOJ expands state immigration reporting requirement

Future TANF and SSI funding could be at risk

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 3, 2026 at 8:46 AM ET

The U.S. Department of Justice has adopted a new legal interpretation requiring all agencies within states participating in two major federal assistance programs to report people they know are not lawfully present in the United States to the Department of Homeland Security. States that do not comply could jeopardize future federal funding tied to Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) and Supplemental Security Income (SSI).

The Office of Legal Counsel issued the opinion on September 1, 2026, reversing a 1998 OLC interpretation that had limited the reporting requirement to state agencies directly administering the programs. The new opinion says the word "State" in Section 404 of the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 applies to the entire state government. That means agencies such as motor vehicle departments, public universities and other state offices could fall within the reporting obligation when they possess information showing that an individual is unlawfully present.

The change does not require states to repay money received while operating under the previous interpretation. DOJ says the opinion applies prospectively, allowing federal agencies to revise future grant agreements and compliance procedures. According to Reuters, more than $16.5 billion in annual TANF grants and roughly $60 billion in SSI benefits are connected to programs covered by the opinion. Undocumented immigrants are generally not eligible to receive TANF or SSI benefits themselves, making the dispute primarily about state information-sharing obligations rather than eligibility for those programs.

The opinion was written by Deputy Assistant Attorney General Joshua Craddock, while Assistant Attorney General T. Elliot Gaiser said participation in TANF carries a statutory reporting obligation. The interpretation could bring the federal government into conflict with states that restrict cooperation with federal immigration enforcement, and legal challenges are possible as agencies determine how the new standard will be implemented.
