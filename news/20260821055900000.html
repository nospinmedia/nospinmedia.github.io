Hong Kong Court Convicts Tiananmen Vigil Leaders

National Security Verdict Carries Potential 10-Year Sentences

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 21, 2026 at 11:46 AM ET

A Hong Kong High Court convicted veteran pro-democracy activists Lee Cheuk-yan, 69, and Chow Hang-tung, 41, on August 21 of inciting subversion under Beijing's National Security Law, concluding a closely watched case centered on the city's long-running annual commemorations of the 1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown. Both pleaded not guilty, while former alliance chairman Albert Ho, 74, had previously entered a guilty plea. The defendants face prison sentences of up to 10 years, with sentencing to be scheduled.

The three activists were leaders of the now-disbanded Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements of China, which organized candlelight vigils in Victoria Park for more than three decades. In a 216-page judgment, a panel of three national security judges ruled that the group's continued call to "end one-party dictatorship" after the National Security Law took effect in 2020 amounted to inciting the public to undermine the constitutional order established by the Chinese Communist Party. The court rejected defense arguments that the slogan represented peaceful political expression rather than an attempt to subvert state power.

The case has become one of the most prominent national security prosecutions involving Hong Kong's former pro-democracy movement. Public Tiananmen vigils were halted in 2020, initially under pandemic restrictions, and never resumed after Beijing imposed the sweeping security law following the 2019 anti-government protests. Since then, many opposition politicians, activists, journalists, and civil society organizations have been prosecuted, jailed, or dissolved under the new legal framework.

The convictions drew criticism from international human rights organizations and several Western governments, which argued the ruling further narrows political freedoms and freedom of expression in Hong Kong. Hong Kong and Chinese authorities have consistently defended the National Security Law, saying it restored stability after the 2019 unrest and targets only conduct that threatens national security rather than legitimate political views.
