Appeals court blocks pregnant-worker law against Texas

9–8 ruling finds House proxy quorum used in 2022 unconstitutional

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 23, 2026 at 8:46 PM ET

A federal appeals court has blocked enforcement of the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act against the State of Texas, ruling 9–8 that the U.S. House violated the Constitution's Quorum Clause when it passed the legislation using pandemic-era proxy voting procedures in 2022.

The New Orleans-based U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit issued its en banc decision on November 20. Judge Cory Wilson, writing for the majority, concluded that lawmakers participating remotely by proxy could not constitutionally be counted as present to establish the quorum required for the House to conduct business. The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act was included in the roughly $1.7 trillion Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023, approved in December 2022.

The immediate effect is narrower than invalidating the pregnancy-accommodation law nationwide. The Fifth Circuit affirmed an injunction preventing the federal government from enforcing it against Texas state employees. The law generally requires employers with 15 or more employees to provide reasonable accommodations for known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions unless doing so would create an undue hardship.

Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton filed the lawsuit in 2023, challenging how Congress enacted the law rather than its workplace protections. A district judge sided with Texas in 2024, a three-judge Fifth Circuit panel reversed in 2025, and the full appeals court subsequently reheard the case. Judge James Graves, writing the principal dissent, argued that the law should remain enforceable and warned about implications for other legislation passed under the same proxy-voting system.

The ruling establishes precedent for federal courts within the Fifth Circuit, covering Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, but does not automatically invalidate other pandemic-era laws. Legal observers have identified measures from the same spending package, including the Electoral Count Reform Act and the federal prohibition on TikTok on government devices, as potential subjects of future challenges.

The Justice Department, which continued defending the law under President Trump, can seek review by the U.S. Supreme Court.
