FCC clears SpaceX plan for up to 1 million orbital data centers

Starmind approval moves forward despite concerns from astronomers and rivals

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 26, 2026 at 5:16 PM ET

The Federal Communications Commission has granted SpaceX conditional authorization for a proposed constellation of up to 1 million satellites designed to operate as data centers in space, clearing a major regulatory hurdle for a project the company calls Starmind.

The FCC issued its order on Wednesday, November 25, authorizing radio communications used for telemetry, tracking and command. The proposed constellation would operate in orbital shells ranging from about 500 to 1,002 kilometers above Earth and use optical laser links to communicate between satellites and with SpaceX’s Starlink network.

The authorization does not mean SpaceX will immediately launch 1 million satellites. The company must place at least one authorized satellite into operation by November 2033, reach 100,000 by November 2035, deploy 500,000 by November 25, 2038, and complete the authorized constellation by November 2040.

The scale would be unprecedented. The full authorization is nearly 90 times larger than SpaceX’s current operational Starlink fleet of roughly 11,200 satellites.

SpaceX envisions using solar-powered orbital computing infrastructure for workloads including artificial intelligence, potentially reducing dependence on terrestrial electricity and data-center infrastructure. Elon Musk has said the company hopes to begin launching Starmind spacecraft as early as 2027, years before the FCC’s first required deployment deadline.

The approval contains limitations. The FCC accepted orbital-debris plans for satellites operating below 600 kilometers, but deferred SpaceX’s proposal for disposing of some higher-altitude spacecraft in heliocentric or other graveyard orbits rather than through atmospheric reentry. Additional regulatory approval would be required for that approach.

The American Astronomical Society, Viasat and Amazon’s Kuiper Systems opposed aspects of the application. Astronomers warned that a constellation potentially numbering hundreds of thousands of satellites could interfere with ground-based observations, while other objections involved orbital congestion and environmental effects.

The FCC rejected petitions seeking to block the authorization. Chairman Brendan Carr described the decision as the first U.S. authorization for data centers in space.
