Wave Of Tech Failures And Cyber Incidents Hits Critical U.S. Systems

FAA, NSA, FBI And USDA Problems Fuel Questions About Infrastructure Resilience

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 22, 2026 at 2:31 PM ET

A striking series of technology failures and cyber incidents has hit major U.S. systems within days, disrupting air travel, classified intelligence communications, agriculture programs and an FBI criminal-justice database. Officials say there is no evidence the incidents are part of a coordinated attack, but their concentration is raising questions about the resilience of critical infrastructure.

On November 13, a telecommunications failure forced the Federal Aviation Administration to halt departures nationwide for more than four hours. The FAA said newly installed technology overloaded aging network equipment, triggering instability. Officials found no evidence of a cyberattack and subsequently disabled the affected router functionality across more than 60 other routers.

The National Security Agency then experienced a nearly four-day disruption of NSANet, its classified internal communications network. The outage was resolved by Friday, November 20. Officials attributed it to accelerated software patching involving vulnerabilities in the PHP programming language, rather than an outside attack. Mission-critical intelligence capabilities remained operational.

At a November 18 House Homeland Security Committee hearing, senior administration officials said investigations had found no evidence that the recent outages were connected or malicious. President Trump similarly told reporters the problems were "individual events" and urged people to "relax."

Two genuine federal cyber incidents have since added to the concern. The USDA confirmed a cyberattack disrupted systems used for farmer loans and payments, forcing password resets and temporary outages. Officials said no personally identifiable information or data was lost.

Separately, the FBI is investigating an apparent intrusion into its National Crime Information Center, which law-enforcement agencies nationwide use for warrants, fugitives, missing persons and other criminal-justice records. Investigators have not publicly determined whether records were stolen or altered. Trump called the apparent breach "not good" during a November 21 interview while suggesting investigators might ultimately learn something useful from the incident.

There is no confirmed connection between the FBI and USDA incidents, or between either cyber incident and the earlier FAA or NSA failures. The broader issue is whether unrelated equipment failures, software problems and actual cyberattacks occurring so close together expose weaknesses in the resilience of essential U.S. technology systems.
