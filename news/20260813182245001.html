Army Apache Crashes Near Fort Hood, Killing Two Soldiers

Salado wildfire prompts evacuations as Army opens investigation

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 13, 2026 at 2:49 PM ET

Two U.S. Army soldiers were killed on August 12, 2026, when an AH-64 Apache attack helicopter crashed in a field near Salado, Texas, igniting a fast-moving grass fire that prompted evacuations of nearby residents. The aircraft was assigned to Fort Hood, and Army officials confirmed both crew members died in the crash.

The Bell County Sheriff's Office said deputies responded shortly after 2:30 p.m. local time and found the helicopter in open terrain, where the impact sparked a rapidly spreading fire. Officials said the aircraft did not strike homes or other structures. Firefighters, assisted by aircraft dropping fire retardant, worked to contain the blaze while emergency crews secured the crash site. The Army said the soldiers' identities would be released after their families had been notified.

Army leaders said their immediate priorities are supporting the victims' families, assisting local emergency responders, and preserving evidence for investigators. Brig. Gen. Ethan Diven, acting commander of the 1st Cavalry Division, said the Army's focus remains on determining what happened while ensuring a thorough investigation. Army officials said an investigation into the crash has been opened but released no preliminary findings, and there was no immediate indication of damage to civilian property beyond the wildfire caused by the impact.

Fort Hood, one of the Army's largest military installations, routinely conducts helicopter training and operational flights in Central Texas. The AH-64 Apache, introduced into U.S. Army service during the 1980s, is a twin-engine attack helicopter flown by a two-person crew and is used for reconnaissance, close air support, and anti-armor missions. Authorities urged residents to continue following local emergency instructions until crews fully contained the fire and completed recovery operations.
