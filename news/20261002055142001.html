Judge restores Seattle U.S. attorney fired less than an hour after taking office

Court says Trump cannot unilaterally remove prosecutor appointed by federal judges

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 2, 2026 at 5:16 PM ET

A federal judge has restored Roger Rogoff as the U.S. attorney for the Western District of Washington, ruling that President Trump could not unilaterally remove a federal prosecutor who had been appointed to the position by the district court. Trump fired Rogoff less than one hour after he was sworn into office in July.

U.S. District Judge Stanley Bastian issued the ruling Thursday, finding that Rogoff has been the lawful U.S. attorney since July 15, 2026, when federal judges in the district appointed him and he took the oath of office. Bastian prohibited the administration from enforcing Rogoff's firing or treating another person as U.S. attorney until a replacement is confirmed by the Senate. The Justice Department said it intends to appeal and seek an immediate stay of the decision.

The dispute centers on the federal law governing vacancies in U.S. attorney offices. Under 28 U.S.C. § 546, the attorney general can appoint an interim prosecutor for 120 days. If that period expires without a presidential nominee being confirmed, the district court can appoint a U.S. attorney who serves until the vacancy is filled. Judges in Washington used that authority to appoint Rogoff after the previous interim arrangement expired.

Trump later directed Charles Neil Floyd, the office's first assistant U.S. attorney, to perform the duties of the top prosecutor. Bastian ruled that Floyd is not the U.S. attorney and that authority to perform the office's responsibilities had been improperly granted to him.

The administration has nominated Simon Peter Serrano to permanently lead the Seattle office, but he has not been confirmed by the Senate. Similar disputes over Trump administration efforts to install preferred federal prosecutors have arisen elsewhere, although Rogoff's case directly tests whether a president can remove a U.S. attorney appointed by a federal district court.
