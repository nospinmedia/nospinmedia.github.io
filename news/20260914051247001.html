Redistricting gives Republicans a House buffer

Trump’s 33% approval creates competing midterm pressure

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 14, 2026 at 2:31 PM ET

Republicans enter the final weeks before the November 3, 2026, midterm elections with significant structural advantages from newly redrawn congressional districts and party-level campaign spending, even as President Trump faces historically weak approval ratings. The competing forces have made control of the narrowly divided U.S. House increasingly dependent on a relatively small number of competitive districts.

Mid-cycle redistricting pushed the median congressional district approximately two percentage points toward Republicans, according to an analysis by the Brookings Institution. District-by-district assessments leave Republicans with an effective cushion of roughly seven or eight seats before the most competitive races are considered. The changes followed an unusual wave of redistricting outside the normal post-census cycle, including Republican-led efforts in states such as Texas and North Carolina, along with Democratic efforts to counter some of those gains elsewhere.

Money provides Republicans another national advantage. Republican Party committees and allied organizations hold roughly a $500 million cash advantage over their Democratic counterparts, according to Brookings. That advantage is not uniform at the candidate level, however: Democratic challengers outraised Republican incumbents during the second quarter in about two-thirds of districts rated as toss-ups. Recent changes allowing party committees to purchase some television advertising at candidate rates could also increase the importance of national party resources during the campaign's closing weeks.

Those advantages are colliding with an unfavorable political environment for the president's party. An August Reuters/Ipsos poll placed Trump's job approval at 33%, a low for his political career, while Democrats reported greater enthusiasm about voting in November. Republicans have nevertheless placed Trump at the center of their national campaign, including the party's unusual midterm convention in Dallas. Historical midterm patterns generally work against the president's party, but this year's altered district boundaries and large differences in national campaign resources complicate comparisons with previous elections.
