Bipartisan Senators Push Trump to Cancel Russian Diesel Deal

Republicans Join Democrats in Challenging Sanctions Waivers and Legality of Imports

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 14, 2026 at 5:15 PM ET

A growing bipartisan group of U.S. senators is urging President Donald Trump to reverse his agreement to import Russian diesel, arguing that the arrangement could violate federal law and provide additional revenue to Russia during its war in Ukraine.

The opposition includes Republican senators who have joined Democrats in challenging the administration's decision to temporarily ease restrictions on Russian petroleum products. Among those raising objections are Sens. Susan Collins of Maine, Lisa Murkowski of Alaska, Thom Tillis of North Carolina and John Curtis of Utah, alongside Democratic senators including Jeanne Shaheen of New Hampshire and Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut.

The dispute follows Trump's agreement with Russian President Vladimir Putin allowing the United States to receive millions of tons of Russian diesel as the administration seeks to ease fuel costs and address supply concerns heading into winter. The administration has defended temporary sanctions waivers as a way to increase available supplies and reduce pressure on diesel and heating-fuel prices.

Opposing lawmakers argue that the administration does not have authority to bypass restrictions established by Congress. They have pointed to the Ending Importation of Russian Oil Act, enacted in 2022 following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which prohibits imports of Russian crude oil and certain petroleum products into the United States.

The senators have also raised national security concerns about sending additional energy revenue to Moscow. Critics of the agreement argue that purchasing Russian petroleum undermines broader Western efforts to restrict the resources available to Putin's government. Some Republican lawmakers have described the arrangement as a benefit to a U.S. adversary even if it produces short-term relief for American fuel markets.

The White House has defended the policy as a temporary response to domestic energy conditions rather than a broader reversal of U.S. policy toward Russia. Administration officials contend that increasing diesel supplies could help transportation companies, farmers and consumers facing higher fuel and heating costs.

The congressional opposition represents a new development in an issue NSM has previously covered: the original Russian diesel agreement. The latest dispute centers on the growing bipartisan Senate challenge, including questions about whether the executive branch has legal authority to permit the imports despite restrictions enacted by Congress.

Lawmakers calling for the agreement to be withdrawn have not yet forced a change in administration policy, leaving open the possibility of additional congressional or legal challenges over the sanctions waivers.
