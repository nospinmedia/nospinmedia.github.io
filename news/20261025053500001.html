DOJ tells California counties to set aside questioned ballots

Federal list of suspected noncitizens includes apparent U.S. citizens

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 25, 2026 at 10:15 AM ET

The Justice Department has told election officials in at least 18 California counties to identify and verify ballots associated with people the federal government suspects may be noncitizens, giving counties 48 hours to provide voter information and directing them to set aside ballots when citizenship cannot be confirmed.

The demand affects names drawn from a federal list of roughly 20,000 people the government considers likely noncitizens who may be registered to vote. Federal prosecutors requested information including identification documents, voter-registration records and Social Security numbers. First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli warned that election officials who knowingly process a noncitizen's ballot could face legal consequences.

But reviews by California election officials have raised significant questions about the accuracy of the federal list. According to the Associated Press, an initial review of 200 names in Los Angeles County found that more than 40% had already canceled their registrations, more than 70% had no voting history, and nearly 40% were born in the United States. Officials in other counties also reported finding apparent errors. The federal government maintains that its effort is intended to identify ineligible voters and protect election integrity.

The dispute comes as voting is already underway for the November 3 midterm elections. More than 330,000 mail ballots had already been counted statewide when the demands were issued. California officials argue that unreliable federal data could result in eligible citizens having their ballots unnecessarily held and could complicate election certification.

The latest action follows federal charges against 28 lawful permanent residents accused of illegally registering to vote. Some are also accused of illegally voting or making false statements. Those criminal cases are separate from the much larger list of approximately 20,000 suspected voters, and inclusion on that list does not establish that a person is a noncitizen or has voted illegally.
