SNAP benefits rise as tougher eligibility rules reshape program

Maximum reaches $1,023 for family of four as states face higher costs

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 2, 2026 at 11:45 AM ET

New SNAP benefit amounts took effect October 1, giving millions of recipients a modest inflation adjustment while broader changes to the federal food-assistance program expand work requirements and shift more administrative costs to states.

For the 48 contiguous states and Washington, D.C., the maximum monthly benefit increased from $298 to $306 for a one-person household and from $994 to $1,023 for a family of four. The minimum benefit for eligible one- and two-person households increased from $24 to $25. Actual benefits depend on household income, size and allowable deductions, so recipients do not necessarily receive the maximum. The gross monthly income limit for a four-person household also increased to $3,575, while the standard deduction for households of one to three people rose to $217.

Those annual cost-of-living adjustments arrive alongside major eligibility changes created by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025. The law expanded the SNAP time-limit work rules to able-bodied adults through age 64, up from 54, and narrowed the exemption for adults with children to households containing a child under age 14. People subject to the rule generally must complete at least 80 hours per month of qualifying work, training or other approved activity to continue receiving benefits beyond three months in a three-year period. The law also removed automatic exemptions previously available to veterans, people experiencing homelessness and certain former foster youth, although people may still qualify for other exemptions.

Another major change begins in fiscal year 2027: states will be responsible for 75% of SNAP administrative costs, compared with 50% previously, while the federal government's share falls from 50% to 25%. Beginning in fiscal year 2028, states can also be required to pay part of SNAP benefit costs depending on their payment-error rates.

The result is a mixed picture for recipients: maximum benefits increased with inflation on October 1, while changes already underway could affect who remains eligible and how states finance the program.
