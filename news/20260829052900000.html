Judge Throws Out KSM's FBI Confession in 9/11 Case

Military court cites lasting effects of CIA torture

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 29, 2026 at 2:30 PM ET

A U.S. military judge at Guantanamo Bay ruled that alleged September 11 mastermind Khalid Sheikh Mohammed's 2007 confessions to FBI agents cannot be used as evidence at his capital trial, concluding prosecutors failed to prove the statements were voluntary after years of harsh treatment in CIA custody. The ruling removes one of the government's most significant pieces of evidence as pretrial proceedings continue nearly 25 years after the terrorist attacks.

In a 45-page decision, Air Force Lt. Col. Michael Schrama found that Mohammed's interviews with FBI agents remained tainted by what he described as an "unbroken continuation" of the psychological effects of the CIA interrogation program. The judge concluded that the government did not show Mohammed's decision to speak with investigators was an act of free will after years of coercive treatment. The FBI interviews took place in January 2007, about four months after Mohammed was transferred from secret CIA detention sites to the military prison at Guantanamo Bay.

The ruling relies on the Military Commissions Act of 2009, which bars statements obtained through torture or cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment. Mohammed was captured in Pakistan on March 1, 2003, and later acknowledged by U.S. officials to have been subjected to 183 waterboarding sessions along with sleep deprivation and other coercive interrogation techniques while in CIA custody. Prosecutors argued that enough time had passed between those interrogations and the FBI interviews to make the later statements voluntary, but Schrama rejected that argument.

Chief prosecutor Rear Adm. Aaron C. Rugh said the government is reviewing the decision and considering an appeal. The ruling follows earlier military commission decisions suppressing statements made by co-defendant Ammar al-Baluchi and other evidence linked to the CIA interrogation program. Despite the setback, prosecutors continue preparing for a trial currently scheduled to begin on June 5, 2028, although years of legal disputes have repeatedly delayed proceedings.
