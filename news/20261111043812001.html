U.S. inflation rises more than expected in October

Food, shelter and energy costs keep pressure on households

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 11, 2026 at 8:45 AM ET

U.S. consumer prices rose more than economists expected in October, showing that inflation pressures extended beyond the earlier surge in gasoline costs and remained a challenge for households and the Federal Reserve.

The Consumer Price Index increased 0.4% from September to October on a seasonally adjusted basis, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. That was slower than September's 0.6% increase but above economists' expectations for a 0.3% rise. Core prices, which exclude volatile food and energy costs, increased 0.3%, also one-tenth of a percentage point above forecasts.

Several major household expenses contributed to the increase. Shelter costs rose 0.3% and accounted for more than 30% of the overall monthly CPI increase. Food prices climbed 0.5%, including a 0.4% increase for groceries and a 0.5% rise for food purchased away from home. Energy prices increased 0.7% after jumping 5.2% in September. Medical care costs rose 0.3%, while new-vehicle prices increased 0.2%. Used car and truck prices moved in the opposite direction, falling 1.2%.

The government did not publish the usual 12-month inflation rate for October because the 2025 federal government shutdown prevented collection of the October 2025 price data needed for a year-over-year comparison. In September, the most recent month with a comparable annual figure, consumer prices were 3.7% higher than a year earlier.

The October report also increased expectations that the Federal Reserve could raise interest rates at its December meeting. Higher rates can help restrain inflation by slowing borrowing and spending, but they also increase financing costs for mortgages, vehicles, credit cards and businesses. Financial markets fell following the report as investors adjusted expectations for future interest rates.
