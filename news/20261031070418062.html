Appeals court rejects DOJ bid to isolate California ballots

Judges leave state's voting procedures in place ahead of Election Day

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 31, 2026 at 10:16 AM ET

A federal appeals court has rejected the Justice Department's emergency request to force California to set aside ballots associated with roughly 20,000 registered voters whom the federal government says may not be U.S. citizens, resolving an appeal that judges had questioned earlier in the week.

A three-judge panel of the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled 2-1 Friday against the government. The majority was formed by Judge Mark J. Bennett, appointed by President Trump, and Judge Gabriel P. Sanchez, appointed by former President Joe Biden. Judge Patrick J. Bumatay, another Trump appointee, dissented.

The Justice Department sued California after state officials refused to segregate ballots connected to a federal list of people suspected of being noncitizens. California has challenged the accuracy of that list, saying it likely includes eligible U.S. citizens. The federal government argues that separating the ballots is necessary to prevent potentially ineligible votes from being counted.

The appeals court upheld a lower court's refusal to order the change while voting is already underway. The majority cited findings that California's existing ballot-processing system has no mechanism for identifying and removing ballots from specified voters after they enter the system. The court said there was "no feasible way" to segregate those ballots without risking major disruption to election operations.

Hundreds of thousands of California ballots have already been processed, and election officials say processed ballots are anonymized and can no longer be connected to individual voters.

Bumatay disagreed with the majority, arguing that California's refusal to separate potentially unlawful ballots undermines the federal government's interest in enforcing the prohibition against noncitizen voting. Justice Department lawyers have previously indicated that they could seek review by the U.S. Supreme Court.

The decision is a substantive update to the earlier court proceedings in which the appellate judges questioned the government's authority and the reliability of the voter list. The midterm election concludes November 3.
