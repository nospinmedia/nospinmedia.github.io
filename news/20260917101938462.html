Greenland and Antarctica lose 12.5 trillion tons of ice

Loss could cover continental U.S. five feet deep

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 17, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

Greenland and Antarctica lost about 12.5 trillion U.S. tons of ice between 1979 and 2023, according to a major new analysis combining decades of satellite observations. The loss was enough to cover the continental United States in roughly five feet of water and raised average global sea level by about 1.24 inches, researchers found.

The study, published September 16 in the journal Scientific Data, combines 42 independent estimates using observations from 27 satellite missions. In scientific units, the researchers calculated a combined loss of 11.309 trillion metric tonnes of ice from the two ice sheets between 1979 and 2023. Greenland accounted for the majority of the loss, despite being considerably smaller than Antarctica.

Researchers found that 84% of the combined ice loss resulted from changes in glacier dynamics, primarily ice flowing more rapidly from land into the ocean. Changes in surface conditions, including melting and snowfall, accounted for the remaining 16%. Greenland's losses accelerated substantially after the 1990s, while Antarctica's losses have been concentrated particularly in West Antarctica.

The amount of ice lost does not translate into five feet of global sea-level rise because the comparison with the United States describes what would happen if all that water were concentrated over a relatively small land area. In reality, meltwater spreads throughout the world's enormous interconnected oceans. Researchers calculated that the ice loss raised the global average sea level by 31.4 millimeters, or approximately 1.24 inches. Even that relatively small global average can increase coastal flooding because storms and high tides begin from a higher baseline.

Ice loss slowed temporarily between 2020 and 2023, helped by unusually high snowfall in East Antarctica and less extreme surface melting in Greenland. Researchers said the short-term change does not reverse the longer-term pattern of accelerating ice-sheet loss observed since the 1990s.
