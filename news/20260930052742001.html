Supreme Court allows rapid third-country deportations to resume

Justices will hear challenge to Trump policy in December

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 30, 2026 at 8:46 AM ET

The U.S. Supreme Court has allowed President Trump's administration to resume rapidly deporting migrants to countries other than their own while the justices consider whether the policy is legal. The court granted an emergency request from the Justice Department on September 29, temporarily blocking lower-court restrictions that required migrants to receive notice and an opportunity to raise fears of persecution or torture before being sent to a third country.

The Supreme Court also agreed to hear arguments in the case in December. The order appeared to divide the justices 6-3, with Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson saying they would have denied the administration's request. The order does not resolve the legality of the deportation policy; it allows the government to continue using it while the case proceeds.

The dispute stems from a ruling by U.S. District Judge Brian Murphy in Massachusetts, who found the Department of Homeland Security policy unlawful because migrants could be removed without a meaningful opportunity to show they might face persecution, torture or other serious harm in the destination country. The 1st U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals upheld key protections ordered by Murphy before the administration sought emergency intervention from the Supreme Court.

The administration argues that third-country removals are an important enforcement tool, particularly when migrants' home countries will not accept them. Government attorneys said the lower-court requirements had already forced officials to cancel a deportation flight carrying about 70 people to three countries. Human-rights groups, meanwhile, say migrants can be sent to nations where they have no personal connection and may face serious danger without first having an adequate opportunity to object.

More than 25,000 migrants have been deported to 29 countries under the policy since it was implemented last year, according to a tally by human-rights groups cited by Reuters, with most sent to Mexico.
