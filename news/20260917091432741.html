Fed raises interest rates for first time since 2023

Quarter-point hike means higher borrowing costs

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 17, 2026 at 10:15 AM ET

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by 0.25 percentage point on September 16, its first increase since 2023, as policymakers respond to inflation that remains above the central bank's 2% target. The unanimous decision raises the federal funds target range to 3.75% to 4.00%, potentially increasing borrowing costs for households and businesses while offering higher returns to some savers.

The Federal Open Market Committee approved the increase by a 12–0 vote. The Fed said economic activity continues to expand at a solid pace, domestic spending has remained resilient and inflation remains elevated. The central bank said the increase is intended to support a more timely return of inflation to its 2% goal. The Fed also raised the interest rate paid on reserve balances to 3.90%, effective September 17, and increased its primary credit rate by a quarter point to 4.00%.

For consumers, the effects will vary by type of borrowing. Rates on credit cards, auto loans and other variable-rate debt tend to respond relatively quickly to changes in the Fed's benchmark rate. Mortgage rates are not directly set by the Fed, but they are influenced by Treasury yields and expectations for inflation and monetary policy. The average 30-year mortgage rate had already climbed to about 6.76% as bond yields increased ahead of the Fed's decision. Savings accounts and certificates of deposit could benefit if banks pass higher rates along to depositors.

The increase comes as U.S. consumer prices were 3.4% higher in August than a year earlier, with rising energy costs contributing to renewed inflation pressure. The Fed's latest economic projections also show that many policymakers believe additional monetary tightening may be appropriate this year, though those projections are individual forecasts rather than commitments to future rate increases.

The decision came despite President Trump's public calls for substantially lower interest rates. Fed officials have emphasized that monetary policy decisions are based on their congressional mandate of maximum employment and stable prices.
