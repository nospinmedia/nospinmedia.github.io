Government testers bypass safeguards on four major AI platforms

GAO says systems turned public information into viable attack plans within hours

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 19, 2026 at 2:30 PM ET

Federal investigators were able to bypass safety protections on ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok and obtain information sufficient to help plan potentially deadly attacks, according to a newly released Government Accountability Office investigation examining how commercially available artificial intelligence could be misused.

The GAO tested the four platforms during spring 2026, assigning them scenarios involving either a mass-casualty explosive attack or an attack on the U.S. electrical grid. All four initially activated safeguards — including refusing requests, removing information or suspending accounts — but investigators eventually circumvented those protections.

Each platform produced a plan in less than 12 hours. In one scenario, an AI system recommended logistical steps for an explosive attack and suggested timing it around crowded public events. In tests involving the electrical grid, platforms proposed attacks on substations near a U.S. airport, including scenarios involving a drone and firearms, and generated supporting maps and images.

Those plans were not flawless. GAO investigators physically visited proposed locations and found errors that the AI systems had missed, including incorrectly positioned roads and barriers that would have obstructed access or visibility. Subject-matter experts nevertheless determined the plans contained enough feasible information to potentially assist an attacker.

GAO deliberately withheld the specific prompts, bypass methods, targets and other sensitive details from its public report. The agency also emphasized that much of the underlying information was already publicly available. The concern was AI's ability to gather scattered information and rapidly organize it into a potentially actionable plan.

The report, GAO-27-108814, is dated October and was publicly released November 18. GAO made no formal recommendations because its investigation examined private-sector products rather than federal agencies.

The companies said they continue improving their safeguards, and some noted that GAO tested earlier versions of their models. GAO also cautioned that the study represented a point-in-time assessment rather than a statistical comparison of the platforms.

Incoming Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee chair Sen. Maggie Hassan said AI safety legislation will be an early priority in January, including consideration of mandatory safety testing before new AI models are publicly released.
