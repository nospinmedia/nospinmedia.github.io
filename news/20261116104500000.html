U.S. attorney warns Warren over planned probes of Trump associates

DOJ prosecutor orders records preserved as Democrats prepare investigations

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 16, 2026 at 2:30 PM ET

A federal prosecutor has ordered Sen. Elizabeth Warren and her staff to preserve records related to potential congressional investigations of President Trump's associates, escalating a dispute over oversight Democrats are planning when they take control of Congress in January.

Aaron Reitz, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, sent Warren a records-preservation notice accusing the Massachusetts Democrat of preparing to use congressional investigations against private individuals associated with Trump. The notice itself does not establish that Warren or her staff committed a crime.

Reitz argued that retaliatory congressional investigations could chill First Amendment rights to free association and cited federal civil rights statutes 18 U.S.C. Sections 241 and 242. His letter directs Warren, her Senate staff, consultants and campaign personnel to preserve relevant documents and electronic communications and gives her until 6 p.m. Eastern Time on November 23 to confirm compliance in writing.

Warren is the top Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee and is positioned to become its chair when Democrats take control of Congress. She and other Democrats have signaled plans for investigations involving major corporations and administration figures.

President Trump reposted Reitz's letter on Truth Social. Similar preservation notices have been sent to Democratic Sens. Richard Blumenthal and Elissa Slotkin and Rep. Robert Garcia.

The warnings do not mean criminal investigations have been opened. After Reitz sent a similar letter to Blumenthal on November 11, Attorney General Todd Blanche told reporters the following day that there were "no open investigations" into Democrats' rhetoric and acknowledged that warning of future congressional investigations is not itself a crime.

The confrontation comes as incoming Democratic committee leaders prepare to exercise expanded oversight authority following the midterm elections.
