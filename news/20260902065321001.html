Tarrant County Cuts Election Day Polling Sites

Texas Officials Approve 92-Location Reduction

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 2, 2026 at 10:15 AM ET

Tarrant County commissioners voted 3-2 on Sept. 1 to reduce the number of Election Day polling locations ahead of the Nov. 3, 2026, general election. Under the approved plan, the county will operate 224 Election Day voting sites, down from 316 during the 2022 midterm elections. The county also reduced the number of early voting locations from 50 to 47. The decision followed hours of public testimony and debate over how the changes could affect voters in one of Texas' largest and most politically competitive counties.

County Elections Administrator Clint Ludwig and County Judge Tim O'Hare said the consolidation was driven by staffing shortages, facility availability, accessibility requirements under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and operational efficiency. County officials said the revised plan continues to exceed the minimum number of polling locations required under the Texas Election Code while providing sufficient access for voters across the county.

Opponents argued the reductions could increase wait times and make voting less accessible in some communities, particularly after commissioners rejected proposals to restore Election Day polling locations at the University of Texas at Arlington and several Tarrant County College campuses. Democratic Commissioners Alisa Simmons and Roderick Miles Jr. voted against the plan, while Tim O'Hare, Matt Krause and Manny Ramirez supported it. More than 100 residents addressed the court during an extended public hearing, with most speakers urging commissioners to retain additional polling places.

Tarrant County, home to more than 2 million residents and including Fort Worth and surrounding communities, has become one of Texas' most closely watched political battlegrounds. The polling location changes are expected to remain a focus of debate as local election officials prepare for November, with county leaders defending the plan as a practical administrative decision and voting-rights advocates continuing to question its impact on voter access.
