U.S. prepares new tariffs on China, EU and 14 other economies

Country-specific duties would target subsidized production

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 25, 2026 at 8:46 AM ET

The Trump administration is preparing new tariffs on China, the European Union and 14 other economies, expanding its effort to counter foreign industrial subsidies after the U.S. Supreme Court struck down broader tariffs earlier this year.

The U.S. Trade Representative's Office, led by U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, has been conducting a Section 301 investigation since March into excess industrial capacity and subsidized production across 16 economies. The countries under examination include China and India, while the 27-nation European Union is treated as a single economy for purposes of the investigation.

People familiar with the plans said the resulting tariffs are expected to be country-specific rather than a single uniform rate. The exact rates and products covered have not yet been publicly disclosed and could still change. The duties are also expected to account for limits established under existing U.S. trade agreements rather than automatically exceeding previously negotiated tariff caps.

The administration says excessive manufacturing capacity supported by foreign subsidies has produced large quantities of lower-priced goods that can undercut American manufacturers. Chinese industries including automobiles, solar panels, steel and cement have drawn particular scrutiny as slowing domestic demand has pushed producers toward overseas markets. China's trade surplus reached nearly $1.2 trillion last year.

The planned action is part of the administration's effort to rebuild its tariff policy after the Supreme Court in February 2026 invalidated sweeping duties President Trump had imposed on nearly every U.S. trading partner. The administration subsequently turned to other legal authorities, including Section 301, to pursue replacement tariffs.

China's government has warned Washington against imposing the new duties as the countries approach the January 10, 2027, expiration of their current trade truce. The development also follows President Trump's recent meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen and comes ahead of the Group of 20 summit in Florida.

Because tariffs are collected from U.S. importers, companies must decide how much of the added expense to absorb and how much to pass along through prices.
