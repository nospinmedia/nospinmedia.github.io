U.S. Consumer Sentiment Falls to Second-Lowest Level on Record

High Prices and Borrowing Costs Weigh on Americans Ahead of Midterm Elections

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 24, 2026 at 5:15 PM ET

U.S. consumer sentiment remained near a record low in October, with Americans continuing to express frustration over purchasing power, high prices and borrowing costs less than two weeks before the November 3 midterm elections.

The University of Michigan's Consumer Sentiment Index registered 48.0 in October, virtually unchanged from 48.1 in September. It was the second-lowest final reading in the survey's 74-year history, behind the record low of 44.8 reached in May. The October figure was 10% below its level a year earlier and 15% below February.

The headline number changed little, but Americans' assessment of current economic conditions weakened. The Current Economic Conditions Index fell from 50.9 to 46.7, driven by an 18% decline in consumers' views of whether it is a good time to buy durable goods. Survey director Joanne Hsu said higher interest rates and concerns about affordability were behind that decline.

Inflation expectations also moved higher. Consumers expect prices to rise 4.7% over the next year, up from 4.6% in September. Longer-term inflation expectations increased to 3.5%.

The deterioration was not uniform politically. Sentiment improved among self-identified Republicans and Democrats but declined sharply among independents, whose index fell from 47.7 in September to 42.4 in October. The university also found growing concern about high prices across the political spectrum.

Gasoline remains particularly visible to consumers, with more than 40% of respondents spontaneously mentioning gas prices during survey interviews. The university said consumers with little or no stock-market wealth were also increasingly likely to report that high prices were hurting their personal finances.

The survey measures how Americans feel about their finances and the economy rather than economic output itself, but it provides one of the final readings of household attitudes before voters decide control of Congress.
