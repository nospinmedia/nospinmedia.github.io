NASA Clears Roman Space Telescope for Falcon Heavy Launch

Flagship observatory targets Sunday morning liftoff

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 30, 2026 at 6:16 AM ET

NASA and SpaceX have completed their final Launch Readiness Review, clearing the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope for liftoff aboard a Falcon Heavy rocket from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center in Florida. The $4.3 billion observatory is scheduled to launch no earlier than 7:26 a.m. EDT on August 30, with meteorologists forecasting about a 60% chance of favorable weather. If needed, a backup launch opportunity is available the following morning.

Named after Dr. Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first chief astronomer, the observatory is designed to investigate some of the universe's biggest mysteries, including dark energy, dark matter and the formation of planetary systems. Roman carries a 2.4-meter primary mirror and a powerful Wide Field Instrument capable of capturing images with resolution comparable to the Hubble Space Telescope while covering an area of sky roughly 100 times larger in a single observation. The mission also includes an advanced coronagraph technology demonstration designed to test new methods for directly imaging planets orbiting nearby stars.

If the launch proceeds as planned, the spacecraft will travel for approximately 30 days to the Sun-Earth L2 Lagrange point, nearly 1 million miles from Earth. After arriving, engineers will spend about three months commissioning and calibrating the observatory before beginning full science operations. During its planned five-year prime mission, Roman will survey billions of galaxies, help measure how the universe has expanded over time, map the distribution of dark matter through gravitational lensing and search for thousands of previously undiscovered exoplanets.

Roman is designed to complement—not replace—the Hubble Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope. While Hubble and Webb excel at detailed observations of individual objects, Roman's wide-field surveys will allow astronomers to study enormous regions of the universe quickly, providing data expected to support scientific discoveries for years to come. NASA plans to make the mission's scientific observations publicly available after processing.
