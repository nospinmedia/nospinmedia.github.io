Federal judge revives Missouri's disputed House map

Appeal deepens fight over November districts

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 9, 2026 at 10:16 AM ET

Missouri's congressional districts are caught between conflicting state and federal court orders after U.S. District Judge Stephen Clark temporarily required election officials to use the state's 2025 congressional map, shortly after U.S. Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh declined to stay a separate state-court ruling requiring use of the older map.

Clark issued a 14-day temporary restraining order on September 8, blocking Missouri from reverting to its 2022 congressional districts. The federal lawsuit was brought by Republican Rep. Bob Onder, state Sen. Rick Brattin and other voters after Missouri conducted its August 4 primary using the 2025 boundaries. The plaintiffs argued that changing districts after the primary would leave some voters facing November nominees they had no opportunity to help select. Clark found they were likely to succeed on claims under Article I, Section 2 of the U.S. Constitution and the Equal Protection Clause.

Hours earlier, Kavanaugh denied Missouri officials' emergency request to stay a unanimous Missouri Supreme Court ruling that the 2025 map had never taken effect because a valid referendum petition suspended the law. Kavanaugh provided no explanation, and the action did not decide the underlying constitutional questions.

The legal conflict intensified immediately. Opponents of the 2025 map filed notice that they would appeal Clark's order to the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. People Not Politicians, which sponsored the referendum petition, also asked the Missouri Supreme Court to hold Secretary of State Denny Hoskins in contempt, arguing that the state court's order requiring use of the older districts remains binding.

Hoskins also formally placed the redistricting referendum on the November 3 ballot as Proposition A. Voters will decide whether to approve the 2025 congressional map, even as courts determine which boundaries govern this year's congressional election. The dispute is unfolding as election officials approach deadlines for preparing and distributing November ballots.
