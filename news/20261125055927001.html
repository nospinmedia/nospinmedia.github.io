Unsealed records reveal unusual fight before search of Ilhan Omar's home

DOJ sought evidence dating to 1980 — two years before she was born

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 25, 2026 at 2:31 PM ET

Newly unsealed federal court records reveal an unusual legal battle that unfolded before agents searched Rep. Ilhan Omar's Washington, D.C., home, including a judge's initial refusal to approve the Justice Department's proposed search procedure and a request for evidence dating back to 1980 — two years before Omar was born.

The records concern the October 12, 2026, search of the Minnesota Democrat's residence. Before authorizing it, U.S. Magistrate Judge Matthew Sharbaugh challenged the Justice Department's position that the Constitution's Speech or Debate Clause, which protects legitimate legislative activity from executive-branch intrusion, did not apply to materials kept inside a member of Congress's private home.

Sharbaugh wrote that "this court sees differently" and required prosecutors to revise their procedure. Omar's representatives were allowed to identify potentially privileged legislative materials before investigators reviewed them. Sharbaugh also required prosecutors to obtain a second warrant before searching files stored on electronic devices seized from the home.

The documents reveal another striking detail: prosecutors sought evidence covering "1980 to the present." Omar was born in 1982. In another filing, the government said it suspected Omar of unspecified offenses occurring "over decades — beginning long before she was ever in Congress." The released records do not explain why investigators selected 1980 as the starting point.

Much of the case remains secret. Chief U.S. District Judge James E. Boasberg declined to release the government's 67-page search warrant affidavit describing the evidence supporting the investigation, concluding that disclosure would be premature while the investigation remains active. Omar has not been charged with a crime. News organizations have until November 30 to challenge remaining proposed redactions.

The unsealed records also exposed a dispute with U.S. House General Counsel Matthew Berry. Berry called a DOJ filing's assertion that his office agreed Speech or Debate protections did not extend to Omar's residence "categorically false." Late Tuesday, the Justice Department filed a formal correction and apologized to the court, attributing the statement to an "inadvertent misunderstanding" and acknowledging that Berry had taken no position on the scope of those protections.

Agents ultimately seized 17 items, including phones and notebooks. Omar has denied wrongdoing and described the investigation as politically motivated.

With the central affidavit still sealed, the public now knows considerably more about the constitutional dispute surrounding the search — but still not what evidence persuaded the court that the search itself was justified.
