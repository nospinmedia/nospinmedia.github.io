Cornell crisis drives calls for changes on campus and in state law

Hochul targets intoxication loophole as university reviews Greek life

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 4, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

The fallout from rape allegations involving former Cornell University fraternity members is now driving two separate reform efforts: New York Gov. Kathy Hochul is backing changes to the state's sexual assault laws, while Cornell President Michael Kotlikoff says the university will take a "serious look" at the role of fraternities and sororities on campus.

Hochul is focusing on what advocates call New York's "voluntary intoxication loophole." State law can make prosecution more difficult when an alleged victim became incapacitated after voluntarily consuming alcohol or drugs, rather than being given an intoxicating substance without consent. Hochul has pledged to work with lawmakers on changing the law, although she has not committed to a specific bill. Similar legislation has previously passed the state Senate but stalled in the Assembly.

The debate follows a civil lawsuit filed by a former Cornell student who alleges she was drugged and raped in October 2024 by several members of the Chi Phi fraternity. The defendants have denied wrongdoing. The case has renewed scrutiny of how Cornell and local law enforcement handled the allegations. Hochul previously appointed Attorney General Letitia James as special prosecutor after saying newly released information had undermined her confidence in the original investigation.

Cornell is now confronting the institutional fallout as well. Kotlikoff said the university will examine fraternities and sororities while seeking ways to preserve positive aspects of Greek life and strengthen individual accountability, transparency and student safety. Cornell's trustees are also arranging an independent outside review of the university's handling of the allegations.

Cornell says it conducted an extensive investigation after the allegations were reported, barred Chi Phi from campus and imposed disciplinary penalties that included expulsions. The renewed scrutiny, however, has prompted students and state officials to demand greater transparency and broader changes.
