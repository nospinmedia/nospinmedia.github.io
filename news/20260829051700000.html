Fed Chair Warsh Signals Rates Could Rise Again

Jackson Hole speech shifts expectations before September meeting

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 29, 2026 at 8:46 AM ET

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh signaled that the central bank is prepared to raise interest rates again if inflation does not move more convincingly toward the Fed's 2% target, delivering a hawkish message during his keynote address at the Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium on August 28. Speaking just over 100 days after becoming Fed chair, Warsh said restoring price stability remains the Federal Reserve's primary responsibility and indicated policymakers are willing to tighten monetary policy further if inflation proves more persistent than expected.

Speaking at the annual conference hosted by Kansas City Federal Reserve Bank President Jeff Schmid, Warsh said the central bank must remain flexible rather than commit to a predetermined policy path through forward guidance. He said officials need to see meaningful and sustained progress on inflation before considering easier monetary policy, warning that if inflation fails to improve sufficiently, 'we have work to do.' The Federal Open Market Committee's next policy meeting is scheduled for September 15-16, when officials will decide whether to leave the federal funds target range at 3.50% to 3.75% or raise rates.

Financial markets quickly adjusted following the speech. Futures tracked by the CME FedWatch Tool showed the implied probability of a 25-basis-point rate increase at the September meeting rising to nearly 60%, up from roughly 37% the previous day. The yield on the 2-year U.S. Treasury note, which is particularly sensitive to expectations for Federal Reserve policy, climbed by more than 6 basis points to about 4.29% as investors priced in the possibility of tighter monetary policy. Higher interest rates generally increase borrowing costs for mortgages, auto loans, business financing and credit cards while helping restrain inflation by slowing economic activity.

Warsh's remarks reinforce the Federal Reserve's message that future policy decisions will depend on incoming economic data rather than a fixed timetable. Investors will closely watch upcoming inflation, employment and consumer spending reports before the September meeting for signs of whether price pressures are easing enough to avoid another rate increase.
