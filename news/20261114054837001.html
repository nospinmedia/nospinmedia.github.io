U.S. measles deaths rise to 10 as cases top 5,400

PAHO panel determines U.S. has lost measles elimination status

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 14, 2026 at 3:46 PM ET

The number of measles-associated deaths reported in the United States this year has reached 10 as confirmed infections climbed to 5,412, while multiple news organizations report that an international health commission has determined the country has lost the measles-elimination status it held since 2000.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported the national case total as of November 12, more than double the 2,289 cases recorded during all of 2025. About 11% of patients, or more than 590 people, have been hospitalized during this year's outbreaks.

The latest deaths were reported in Wisconsin and Pennsylvania. Wisconsin officials reported the death of a child in Grant County, the state's second measles-associated death within two weeks. Officials did not disclose the child's vaccination status. Pennsylvania reported the death of an unvaccinated resident of Montour County but did not disclose the person's age. Pennsylvania has reported seven measles-associated deaths and 1,542 confirmed cases this year.

Federal data indicate roughly 96% of reported cases have occurred among people who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown. The standard two-dose MMR vaccine is about 97% effective at preventing measles.

The Associated Press, Reuters and Wall Street Journal reported late Friday, citing people familiar with the decision, that a commission of the Pan American Health Organization determined continuous domestic measles transmission has lasted more than 12 months, meaning the United States no longer meets the definition of elimination. PAHO's formal public announcement is scheduled for Tuesday, November 17.

CDC spokesperson Erica Schwartz said vaccination, rather than the elimination designation itself, protects Americans and that federal officials are continuing to work with state and local health departments to stop outbreaks.
