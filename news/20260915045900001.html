10-year Treasury yield climbs above 5.03%

Global bond selloff pushes benchmark to highest since 2007

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 15, 2026 at 8:45 AM ET

The yield on the 10-year U.S. Treasury climbed above 5.03% Tuesday, reaching its highest level since 2007 as a global bond selloff intensified amid persistent inflation concerns, elevated oil prices and expectations for additional interest-rate increases. The move extends Monday's surge above 5%, pushing one of the world's most important borrowing benchmarks to a nearly two-decade high.

The selloff has spread well beyond the United States. Japan's 10-year government bond yield climbed to 3.04%, its highest level in about 30 years, while government bond yields also rose across Europe and other major markets. Rising sovereign yields increase financing costs for governments and can feed through to mortgages, business loans and other forms of credit.

Energy prices have added to the pressure. Middle East supply disruptions have pushed oil sharply higher in recent weeks, increasing concerns that more expensive fuel and transportation could keep inflation elevated. Those concerns have dramatically shifted expectations for the Federal Reserve's September 16 decision. CME FedWatch data cited by Reuters showed markets assigning more than a 94% probability to a rate increase Wednesday, while a Reuters poll found 85% of economists expected a quarter-point increase.

The rise in yields has continued despite an earlier effort by the U.S. Treasury Department to improve conditions in the government debt market. On September 9, Treasury announced that it would buy back up to $6 billion of older 10- to 20-year Treasury securities, tripling the size of its previous long-dated buyback operation. The bond selloff continued afterward as investors remained focused on inflation, government borrowing and the outlook for interest rates.

With the 10-year Treasury yield serving as a benchmark for borrowing throughout the economy, a sustained move above 5% could make financing more expensive for households, businesses and the federal government while central banks confront renewed inflation pressure.
