Supreme Court takes Maryland case over race and school admissions

Parents say race-neutral magnet policy discriminates against Asian American students

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 20, 2026 at 6:30 PM ET

The U.S. Supreme Court has agreed to hear a challenge to the admissions system for four selective public middle schools in Montgomery County, Maryland, in a case that could affect how school districts nationwide design race-neutral policies intended to broaden access to competitive programs.

The lawsuit was brought by the Association for Education Fairness, a group of parents who argue that changes to the county's magnet-school admissions process were designed to increase Black and Latino enrollment while reducing the number of Asian American students admitted. The school district says its policy is race-neutral and does not use an applicant's race when selecting students.

Under the revised system, students qualify for a lottery based on academic performance, including standardized test results. The process also considers conditions at a student's home school, including poverty levels. Students attending higher-performing schools can face higher academic thresholds for entering the lottery.

The parents argue those criteria disadvantage Asian American students, who are disproportionately concentrated in some of the county's higher-performing schools. They contend that the policy can violate the 14th Amendment's Equal Protection Clause even though it does not explicitly classify individual applicants by race.

Lower courts dismissed the lawsuit. The 4th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals concluded that the parents had not shown the policy disproportionately harmed Asian American applicants or that school officials acted with discriminatory intent. Asian American students continued to be admitted at rates higher than their representation in the applicant pool.

The Supreme Court will consider whether the parents have presented enough evidence of discrimination for their lawsuit to proceed. A ruling for the parents would not automatically invalidate Montgomery County's admissions system; it could instead return the case to the lower courts for further proceedings.

The dispute follows the Supreme Court's 2023 decision restricting the direct use of race in college admissions and could clarify how courts evaluate policies that are race-neutral on their face but are challenged as intentionally discriminatory.
