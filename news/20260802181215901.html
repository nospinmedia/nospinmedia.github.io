DOJ Seeks To Dismiss Reflecting Pool Case

Prosecutors blame contractor errors; President Trump objects

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 3, 2026 at 8:12 PM ET

The Justice Department moved on July 31, 2026 to dismiss the felony case against former Olympic canoeist David Hearn, saying newly obtained Interior Department records show the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool damage stemmed from a botched installation—not vandalism. The filing marks a rare public split between prosecutors and the White House after President Trump said he “disagrees 100%” with U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro and continued to blame vandals.

In a motion filed in D.C. Superior Court, prosecutors wrote that Interior’s late-produced documents and a site visit indicated the damage resulted from “flawed installation by the contractor” amid a rush to finish work ahead of America 250 celebrations. The government’s filing identifies Atlantic Industrial Coatings as the sealant contractor and notes the project advanced through no-bid awards that also included Green Water Solutions for filtration. Hearn, 67, was indicted on one count of felony destruction of property—an offense that carries up to 10 years in prison under D.C. law—after his June 2026 arrest near the pool.

The reversal has immediate legal and political stakes. Prosecutors acknowledged the new evidence undercuts their ability to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. On August 1, 2026, Trump criticized the move on Truth Social, writing, “I disagree 100% with Jeanine Pirro,” and asserting that “the major damage was caused by VANDALS!” Defense attorneys Norm Eisen, Mary Dohrmann, and Steve Levin welcomed the development and said the government owes Hearn an apology.

Additional case materials describe problems that surfaced shortly after the “American Flag Blue” coating was applied and the pool refilled. A National Park Service official has also testified that damage predated Hearn’s contact with the liner and that the same repairs would have been required regardless—further weakening the case. With prosecutors now seeking dismissal, attention is likely to turn to how hurried timelines and no-bid contracting contributed to the high-profile failure.
