USDA Moves To End Roadless Forest Protections

Proposal could expand logging and road construction on federal lands

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 19, 2026 at 3:46 PM ET

The U.S. Department of Agriculture and the U.S. Forest Service have formally proposed rescinding the 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule, a long-standing policy that generally prohibits new road construction and most commercial logging in designated roadless areas of national forests. Agriculture Secretary Brooke L. Rollins said the proposal would give local forest managers greater flexibility to reduce wildfire risks through thinning projects, fuel breaks and improved emergency access.

The proposal begins a public comment period that runs through September 21, 2026, and is accompanied by a draft environmental impact statement. The original rule applied to roughly 58–59 million acres of national forest land. Because Idaho and Colorado now operate under separate state-specific roadless rules, the current federal rule directly governs about 45 million acres of undeveloped national forest lands across much of the country, including parts of Alaska's Tongass National Forest, California, Oregon and the Southern Appalachian region.

Supporters argue the change would make it easier to remove hazardous fuels, manage insect- and disease-damaged forests, improve access for firefighters and strengthen local forest management. Conservation organizations, including Earthjustice, the Sierra Club and the Natural Resources Defense Council, argue the proposal would increase habitat fragmentation, threaten watersheds and open previously protected backcountry areas to expanded road construction and commercial timber harvesting.

The proposal does not immediately change forest management policies. Instead, it begins the federal rulemaking process, allowing public comments before the administration decides whether to finalize the repeal. Environmental groups have already indicated they are preparing legal challenges if the rule is ultimately rescinded. Because the roadless rule has been revised and litigated repeatedly since its adoption in 2001, any final action is likely to face additional court scrutiny before broad changes take effect.
