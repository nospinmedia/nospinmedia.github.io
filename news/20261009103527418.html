Army reveals failures behind DC plane crash that killed 67

Broken radio calls, altitude errors and missed warnings exposed

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 9, 2026 at 6:31 PM ET

The U.S. Army has released an investigation identifying widespread safety failures behind the January 29, 2025, midair collision between an Army Black Hawk helicopter and American Airlines Flight 5342 near Washington, D.C. The crash killed 67 people, making it the deadliest U.S. aviation disaster in more than two decades.

The Army's report, released on October 8, 2026, found that multiple breakdowns in communication, training, flight procedures and safety oversight contributed to the collision. The findings reinforce conclusions reached earlier by the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB).

The disaster occurred as the American Airlines regional jet approached Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport after departing Wichita, Kansas. An Army UH-60 Black Hawk, conducting a nighttime training mission, collided with the passenger aircraft over the Potomac River. All 64 people aboard the airliner and three soldiers aboard the helicopter died.

Investigators identified two incomplete radio transmissions as particularly significant. The communications prevented the helicopter crew from receiving critical information needed to recognize the approaching passenger jet. The Army also found that air traffic controllers could have provided clearer warnings to both aircraft.

The helicopter crew was wearing night-vision goggles, which restricted their field of vision and made identifying the airliner difficult against Washington's city lights.

Another major concern involved the helicopter's designated flight route. At one location, the published route allowed just 75 feet of vertical separation from aircraft approaching the airport's secondary runway. Investigators determined that this margin was insufficient to account for normal helicopter altimeter inaccuracies.

The NTSB previously determined that the Black Hawk was flying nearly 100 feet higher than its instruments indicated, placing it above the route's permitted altitude.

The Army also acknowledged failures in its broader aviation safety practices, including inadequate monitoring of dangerous flight conditions and insufficient coordination with the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA).

Following the crash, the Army inspected and reconfigured 3,585 helicopters after discovering that the accident helicopter's ADS-B Out system, which broadcasts an aircraft's position, had not been functioning properly. The military has also strengthened pilot training and is reviewing helicopter operations near other major cities, including Los Angeles and Seattle.

The NTSB recently reported satisfactory progress on seven of eight safety recommendations directed at the Army. However, investigators remain concerned about unresolved aviation risks, including the need for improved collision-avoidance technology.

Families of the victims welcomed the release but called for greater accountability. Tim Lilley, a former Army Black Hawk pilot whose son Sam Lilley was the airliner's first officer, said the military still owes families a fuller explanation of how the risks were allowed to continue.

Congress is considering aviation safety legislation addressing collision-avoidance systems and other protections. Victims' families are urging lawmakers to act before another preventable tragedy occurs.
