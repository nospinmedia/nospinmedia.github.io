30-Year U.S. Mortgage Rate Hits 1-Year High At 6.66%

Borrowing costs climb as inflation worries keep yields elevated

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 3, 2026 at 3:45 PM ET

U.S. mortgage costs climbed again this week, with the average 30-year fixed rate rising to 6.66% for the week ending July 30, 2026, according to Freddie Mac. That’s the fourth straight weekly increase and the highest level since July 2025, adding fresh pressure to homebuyers during the peak summer season.

The latest reading, drawn from Freddie Mac’s Primary Mortgage Market Survey, compares with 6.58% the previous week. Mortgage rates typically move with the 10-year Treasury yield, which advanced this week as investors weighed stubborn inflation and the potential for tighter financial conditions. While the Federal Reserve left its policy rate unchanged in late July 2026, markets remain focused on incoming price data that could influence borrowing costs in the months ahead.

- Average 30-year fixed: 6.66% (up from 6.58% last week) - Highest since: July 2025

Affordability remains the central hurdle. At 6.66%, a typical $400,000, 30‑year fixed mortgage carries a monthly principal‑and‑interest payment of roughly $2,570, about $175 more than at 6.00%. With home prices still elevated and inventory tight, real‑estate economists say sustained rates near 7% could sideline more first‑time buyers and keep would‑be sellers from listing, limiting choice and keeping prices from adjusting quickly. Refinancing activity also tends to cool as rates move higher, reducing one avenue households sometimes use to lower monthly expenses.

Rate quotes vary widely by lender and borrower profile, and day‑to‑day movements can be volatile. Still, the trend of the past month—rising long‑term yields and a steadier path for the Federal Reserve’s policy rate—has kept mortgage costs elevated compared with earlier this year, when averages briefly dipped below 6%. Buyers considering a purchase may want to compare multiple lenders, evaluate buydown points, and assess whether locking a rate now or waiting for upcoming inflation releases better suits their risk tolerance and timeline.
