Pentagon plans to livestream firing-squad execution to the public

Fort Hood gunman's December execution could mark a historic first

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 9, 2026 at 2:30 PM ET

The Pentagon plans to livestream the execution of Nidal Hasan, the former Army major convicted of killing 13 people in the 2009 Fort Hood mass shooting, allowing the general public to watch a military firing squad carry out his death sentence. A Pentagon official confirmed the broadcast plan on October 8, 2026, following an announcement by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

Hasan's execution is scheduled for December 3, 2026, at 1 p.m., at Fort Hood, Texas, the same military installation where he carried out the attack. The execution would be the first carried out by the U.S. military since 1961.

Hegseth announced the decision during an interview with conservative commentator Jack Posobiec, saying the public should be able to witness the consequences of Hasan's actions. A Pentagon official subsequently confirmed to CBS News that the military intends to broadcast the execution live.

The announcement represents an extraordinary departure from modern American execution procedures. Executions are generally witnessed by a limited group that may include victims' relatives, legal representatives and selected journalists. The last widely recognized public execution in the United States occurred in Kentucky in 1936, when Rainey Bethea was hanged before thousands of spectators.

Hasan, a former Army psychiatrist, was convicted at a 2013 court-martial of murdering 13 people and wounding more than 30 others during the November 5, 2009, shooting. He acknowledged responsibility during his trial and was sentenced to death.

Earlier this week, President Trump approved the firing-squad execution following Hegseth's recommendation. Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell announced the authorization. Hasan has been held on military death row at Fort Leavenworth, Kansas.

The planned execution would also revive a method rarely used by the American military. The last U.S. military firing-squad execution occurred in 1945, when Army Private Eddie Slovik was executed for desertion during World War II. Firing squads have been used more recently in civilian executions, including in South Carolina during 2025.

Geoffrey Corn, a Texas Tech University law professor and former Army legal officer, questioned the decision to make the execution publicly viewable. He said the traditional restrictions on witnesses reflect the gravity of capital punishment and warned that a livestream could transform the proceeding into a public spectacle.

The Pentagon has not publicly detailed the livestream platform, viewing arrangements or potential restrictions on access. No admission fee or pay-per-view arrangement has been announced.
