U.S. cost of Iran war climbs above $47 billion

Replacing missiles and other munitions accounts for most of the estimate

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 28, 2026 at 5:16 PM ET

The cost of U.S. military operations in the war with Iran has climbed to at least $47.1 billion, according to a new Pentagon estimate provided to Congress, with replacing missiles and other munitions accounting for the largest share of the expense.

The latest figure represents a substantial increase from the approximately $37.5 billion estimated in July. It includes costs accumulated through mid-October but remains incomplete because some expenses, including repairs to U.S. military installations and diplomatic facilities damaged during Iranian attacks, have not yet been fully calculated.

More than $29 billion of the current estimate is tied to replenishing weapons used during the conflict. That category includes air-defense interceptors, precision-guided missiles and bombs fired or dropped during U.S. operations and efforts to defend American forces and regional allies from Iranian missile and drone attacks. Another approximately $6.1 billion is attributed to replacing military equipment and platforms that were lost or damaged.

Direct operational expenses add billions more. The U.S. Air Force has reported approximately $7.6 billion in costs since joint U.S.-Israeli military operations against Iran began in February 2026. The Army has recorded roughly $2.1 billion, while the Navy has reported approximately $1.5 billion in operational expenses.

Those figures do not represent the full long-term cost of the conflict. Damage assessments and repairs are continuing at American military and diplomatic facilities across the Middle East, including locations struck by Iranian missiles and drones. Future operations and the eventual replacement of additional weapons could push the total higher.

The growing cost also comes as the Pentagon works to replenish weapons inventories after months of intensive combat. Air-defense interceptors and other precision weapons can cost millions of dollars each, meaning sustained operations can rapidly increase both immediate spending and future procurement requirements.

The $47.1 billion estimate measures military expenditures rather than the broader economic consequences of the conflict, such as changes in energy prices, shipping costs or other effects on the U.S. economy.
