Study estimates summer heat killed more than 50,000 across European cities

Nearly two-thirds of deaths attributed to human-caused climate change

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 23, 2026 at 8:46 PM ET

Extreme heat during the summer of 2026 caused an estimated 50,391 deaths across hundreds of European cities, making it the deadliest summer for heat in the researchers' records dating to 2000, according to new research. The analysis estimated that 32,619 deaths, or about 65%, were attributable to additional warming caused by human-driven climate change.

Researchers from Imperial College London and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine analyzed temperatures and mortality patterns in 854 cities across 30 European countries during June, July and August. Those urban areas have a combined population of about 173 million people, representing roughly 40% of the population of the countries studied. Because the analysis covers urban areas rather than all of Europe, it does not represent a complete continent-wide death toll.

The figures are estimates produced using established statistical relationships between temperature and mortality rather than a count of death certificates listing heat as the cause. Researchers compared observed temperatures with modeled temperatures in a world without human-caused warming to estimate how much of the mortality burden was attributable to climate change.

Older adults were particularly vulnerable. People 85 and older accounted for about 22,500 deaths, or roughly 45% of the estimated total, while approximately 6,000 people under 65 also died from heat-related causes. Italy had the largest estimated national toll at 13,303, followed by France with 7,268, Spain with 6,824 and Germany with 6,340.

Nearly one-third of the estimated deaths occurred during an intense heatwave between June 17 and July 1, when average temperatures across the cities studied were about 5.3 degrees Celsius above normal. On individual days, temperatures in some locations reached as much as 12 degrees Celsius above historical averages.

The researchers said the findings point to increasing health risks as temperatures rise and emphasized measures such as heat-warning systems, access to cooling, urban green spaces and protections for older and medically vulnerable people.
