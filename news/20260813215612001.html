DR Congo Ebola Death Toll Passes 2,000

WHO warns outbreak could become deadliest on record

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 13, 2026 at 6:16 AM ET

Health officials in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) say the country's Ebola outbreak has killed more than 2,000 people among more than 4,300 confirmed cases, while the World Health Organization (WHO) warns the epidemic is spreading faster than response efforts can contain it. WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said on August 12, 2026, that the outbreak is on track to surpass the 2014–2016 West Africa epidemic, which killed more than 11,300 people, if its current trajectory continues.

The outbreak is caused by the rare Bundibugyo ebolavirus, for which there is currently no approved vaccine or specific treatment. It was formally declared on May 15, but genetic sequencing indicates transmission began as early as February 2026, giving the virus months to spread before the emergency was recognized. Cases have been recorded across five eastern DRC provinces, with Ituri among the hardest-hit areas, and related infections have also been confirmed across the border in Uganda.

Children have been disproportionately affected. According to UNICEF, they account for nearly one-quarter of confirmed cases and almost one-third of all deaths, with children younger than 5 experiencing a case-fatality rate more than twice that of adults. Health officials say the combination of delayed diagnosis, limited access to care, and continued community transmission has made protecting young children especially difficult.

Containment efforts face severe obstacles. Armed conflict, mass displacement, damaged roads, misinformation, and health-worker strikes continue to disrupt response operations across eastern Congo. Recent attacks in Ituri, including an assault on Banana École that displaced about 28,000 people, have further limited access to affected communities and interrupted treatment and surveillance activities. Officials have also reported that many new infections are occurring outside known contact chains, making case-finding and contact tracing increasingly difficult.

Researchers have begun clinical trials in Ituri involving experimental vaccines and treatments for the Bundibugyo strain. WHO and the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) have also convened emergency consultations to review the outbreak's progression and monitor the virus through ongoing genomic surveillance. Officials said the outbreak's trajectory will depend heavily on improving security, expanding access for health workers, strengthening contact tracing, and rapidly isolating new cases.
