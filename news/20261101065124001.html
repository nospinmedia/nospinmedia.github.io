NASA's Swift telescope is about to fall back to Earth

Failed $30 million robotic rescue leaves 21-year science mission at its end

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 1, 2026 at 8:46 PM ET

NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory is expected to plunge back into Earth's atmosphere after a commercial robotic mission failed to raise the aging telescope into a safer orbit. The 1.5-ton spacecraft ended science operations in October after more than 21 years studying some of the universe's most powerful explosions.

Swift launched on November 20, 2004, with a primary mission planned to last just two years. Instead, it became a long-running observatory specializing in gamma-ray bursts, rapidly detecting explosions and directing other telescopes toward them. During its mission, Swift detected more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts and 1,400 supernovas, while also observing black holes, comets and asteroids.

Its orbit began deteriorating more rapidly as increased solar activity expanded Earth's upper atmosphere, increasing the drag on satellites in low Earth orbit. Swift has no propulsion system capable of raising its own orbit.

NASA awarded Katalyst Space Technologies a roughly $30 million contract in 2025 to attempt an unusual rescue. Katalyst built the LINK robotic spacecraft, equipped with three robotic arms and designed to capture Swift and slowly push it into a higher orbit. LINK launched on July 3, 2026, but developed attitude-control problems in space. NASA and Katalyst abandoned the boost attempt in August.

LINK later approached to within roughly 7.5 to 9 miles of Swift while testing rendezvous technology but never captured the telescope. LINK itself re-entered Earth's atmosphere on September 25.

Swift continued collecting scientific observations until October 20, when it fell to about 171 miles above Earth and could no longer maintain the stable orientation required for its telescopes. NASA then ended operations and prepared the spacecraft for re-entry.

Most of Swift is expected to burn up in the atmosphere, although NASA says some dense components could survive to the surface. The exact re-entry location cannot be predicted far in advance.
