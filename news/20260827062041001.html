Army awards up to $2.2B for nuclear microreactors at five bases

Program aims to strengthen military energy security by 2028

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 27, 2026 at 4:20 PM ET

The U.S. Army, working with the Defense Innovation Unit, has awarded contracts worth up to $2.2 billion to five companies to develop, own and operate nuclear microreactors at military installations across the United States. The initiative, known as the Janus Program, is intended to provide reliable, on-site electricity for mission-critical military operations even if commercial power grids fail because of cyberattacks, natural disasters or armed conflict. Army officials said the first operational reactor is targeted for September 30, 2028, under Executive Order 14299, which directed the military to accelerate deployment of advanced nuclear technology to strengthen national security.

The selected companies are Antares Nuclear at Fort Bragg, North Carolina; BWXT Advanced Technologies at Fort Campbell, Kentucky; General Atomics Electromagnetic Systems at Fort Hood, Texas; Radiant Industries at Fort Benning, Georgia; and Westinghouse Government Services at Fort Drum, New York. The milestone-based contracts require companies to meet technical, licensing and safety objectives before receiving additional funding. Radiant Industries received the program's largest potential award, valued at up to $750 million, to deploy as many as 15 one-megawatt microreactors under the Janus initiative.

Army officials said the reactors are expected to generate between 1 and 20 megawatts of electricity, providing dependable baseload power while reducing reliance on diesel generators and vulnerable civilian infrastructure. The service expects substantial private-sector investment alongside government funding and believes the program could eventually expand to more than 20 microreactors across Defense Department installations. Officials also noted that Antares Nuclear's Mark-0 reactor achieved criticality during earlier testing in 2026, demonstrating progress toward field-ready reactor technology.

The contracts require developers to own and operate the reactors while assuming responsibility for removing all spent nuclear fuel within two years after a reactor is permanently shut down. Supporters say the initiative will improve military readiness and strengthen the domestic advanced nuclear industry, while critics have questioned reactor costs, long-term oversight and the decision to rely on Army-led regulation rather than the Nuclear Regulatory Commission. Army officials said every project must still satisfy strict nuclear safety and environmental requirements before entering operation.
