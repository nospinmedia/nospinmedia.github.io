Scientists find evidence of blood cells in 115-million-year-old dinosaur bone

Remarkable preservation offers new clues about dinosaur biology

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 7, 2026 at 8:46 PM ET

Scientists studying a 115-million-year-old dinosaur fossil have found what they describe as the strongest evidence yet that remnants of red blood cells and blood-related molecules can survive the fossilization process for more than 100 million years.

The discovery comes from the hind-leg bones of Aratasaurus museunacionali, a meat-eating theropod that lived during the Early Cretaceous in what is now northeastern Brazil. Researchers estimate the animal was about 3.1 meters (10 feet) long, weighed roughly 34 kilograms and was approximately four years old when it died.

Using electron microscopy and several forms of chemical analysis, researchers found numerous oval microscopic structures inside the bone's vascular canals, where blood vessels once ran. More importantly, chemical testing detected heme and heme-derived molecules associated with the structures. Heme is the iron-containing component of hemoglobin that helps red blood cells transport oxygen.

That combination is important because scientists have previously reported cell-like structures in dinosaur fossils, but distinguishing original biological material from minerals, microbes or later contamination has been difficult. The new study, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, combines the structures' location inside the bone with multiple chemical signatures consistent with blood.

The researchers are not claiming that liquid blood, intact cells or usable dinosaur DNA survived. The structures are mineralized and chemically transformed remnants of the dinosaur's original biology. Tests for ancient DNA were negative, meaning the discovery provides no path toward cloning a dinosaur. DNA is considerably more fragile than the molecular traces identified in the fossil.

Researchers believe unusual burial conditions and a manganese mineral called hausmannite may have helped stabilize the structures during fossilization. If similar evidence is found in other fossils, scientists could potentially learn more about dinosaur metabolism, oxygen transport and physiology rather than relying almost entirely on bones and anatomy.

Some researchers not involved in the study remain cautious about definitively identifying the structures as red blood cells, making further confirmation important.
