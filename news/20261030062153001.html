DOJ expands investigation into Alaska Senate race

New subpoenas target controversy involving two Dan Sullivans

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 30, 2026 at 1:15 PM ET

The Justice Department has issued a new wave of subpoenas and records-preservation requests as it expands a criminal investigation into Alaska's closely watched U.S. Senate race, where two candidates named Dan Sullivan appear on the ballot.

The investigation centers on allegations that Democratic operatives helped recruit Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., a retired teacher running without a party affiliation, in an effort to draw votes from Republican incumbent Sen. Dan Sullivan. CBS News reported that the new subpoenas target Democratic officials believed to have connections to the recruitment effort. Reuters reported that more than 40 subpoenas and preservation requests had been issued, including to the campaigns of Democratic nominee Mary Peltola and Daniel J. Sullivan Jr.

No one involved in the race has been charged with a crime, and the precise scope and potential targets of the federal investigation remain unclear. Peltola has denied knowing about efforts to recruit the second Sullivan, and her campaign has denied involvement with either Sullivan campaign. Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. has maintained that he is genuinely running for office.

The Alaska Supreme Court previously ruled that Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. qualified to remain on the ballot. Election law experts cited by AP and NBC News said recruiting a candidate who could draw votes from an opponent is not inherently illegal, although other conduct surrounding such an effort could potentially violate the law.

The federal investigation is proceeding alongside a separate inquiry ordered by Republican Gov. Mike Dunleavy, who directed Alaska's Department of Public Safety to determine whether state laws were violated. The Justice Department has also filed a separate civil lawsuit seeking to preserve its ability to challenge certification if the incumbent Sullivan loses and officials determine voter confusion affected the result.

The controversy comes days before the November 3 midterm election, with Alaska's Senate contest among the races that could help determine control of the chamber.
