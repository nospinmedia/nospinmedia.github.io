Judge orders partial release of records behind search of Ilhan Omar's home

Key 67-page affidavit detailing government's evidence will remain sealed

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 18, 2026 at 5:15 PM ET

A federal judge has ordered the government to prepare portions of the sealed court record behind last month's search of Rep. Ilhan Omar's Washington, D.C., home for public release, while keeping the key affidavit describing investigators' evidence sealed for now.

Chief U.S. District Judge James Boasberg ordered prosecutors to submit proposed redactions by Friday, November 20. Unless the government obtains a stay, redacted records could be released as soon as 5 p.m. Monday, November 23, with news organizations given until November 30 to challenge proposed redactions.

The records stem from an October 12 search of the Minnesota Democrat's home by Homeland Security Investigations as part of a federal investigation into possible fraud involving her family's immigration paperwork. Omar has not been charged and denies wrongdoing.

The search occurred 22 days before the November 3 midterm election, despite longstanding Justice Department guidance cautioning against overt investigative steps involving political candidates close to an election. Federal authorities have also issued grand jury subpoenas to several of Omar's relatives.

Agents seized 17 items, including phones, notebooks, a Somali-language children's book and a vintage camera that Omar's husband had received as a gift. Omar is separately seeking the return of seized property, and Boasberg ordered the government to report whether records from that sealed proceeding should also be made public.

The 67-page search-warrant affidavit detailing witnesses and evidence presented to obtain the warrant will remain sealed. Prosecutors argue its release could jeopardize the ongoing investigation.

During Tuesday's hearing, Boasberg noted that President Donald Trump, Vice President JD Vance and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin had publicly discussed allegations surrounding the case while prosecutors argued that even basic investigative details should remain confidential.

The dispute also involves constitutional protections under the Speech or Debate Clause, which protects legislative activities from certain executive-branch intrusions.
