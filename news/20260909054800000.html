U.S. bans Canadian alcohol, motorcycles and some dairy

New restrictions deepen trade fight with Canada

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 9, 2026 at 11:45 AM ET

The United States is banning imports of certain Canadian dairy products, motorcycles and most alcoholic beverages, escalating a trade dispute between two countries whose economies have long been closely integrated. The import bans ordered by President Trump take effect September 29, while a revised set of 50% tariffs on other Canadian goods begins September 15.

The prohibited products include certain dairy items such as whey, motorcycles and mopeds, molasses, nonalcoholic beer and many Canadian alcoholic beverages. Separately, the administration expanded or modified 50% tariffs covering products including certain Canadian cheeses, steel and aluminum goods, boats, all-terrain vehicles and furniture. Some products, including cement, were removed from the additional tariff coverage.

The administration invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, a rarely used provision allowing restrictions against countries the president determines are discriminating against U.S. commerce. The White House says Canada's treatment of American dairy, alcohol and motor-vehicle exports prompted the measures. The administration also directed federal agencies to exclude Canadian products from certain large, long-term government contracts unless Canada provides reciprocal access for U.S. goods.

The action came after Canada imposed retaliatory tariffs on approximately $20 billion in U.S. exports on September 8. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney's government has defended those measures as a response to earlier U.S. trade restrictions while saying Canada remains willing to negotiate.

The new restrictions could raise costs for some affected U.S. businesses and consumers by making targeted Canadian imports more expensive or unavailable, although the ultimate effect on overall food prices will depend on which products are affected, available substitutes and how long the restrictions remain. The trade escalation comes as energy markets are also under pressure from the U.S.-Iran conflict, with oil prices recently climbing above $100 a barrel, creating a separate source of potential cost increases throughout transportation and supply chains.
