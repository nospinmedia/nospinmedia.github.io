Eight States Sue OpenAI Over ChatGPT Safety Concerns

Attorneys General Allege Children Were Harmed and Parents Misled About Risks

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 15, 2026 at 3:46 PM ET

A coalition of eight U.S. states has filed coordinated lawsuits against OpenAI, alleging that its artificial intelligence chatbot, ChatGPT, endangered children and vulnerable users while misleading consumers about its safety protections.

The lawsuits were announced on October 14, 2026, by attorneys general from Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana. Although coordinated, each state filed its own case under applicable state consumer protection laws.

Iowa Attorney General Brenna Bird led the public announcement alongside attorneys general Kris Kobach of Kansas, Mike Hilgers of Nebraska and JB McCuskey of West Virginia.

The complaints accuse OpenAI of designing ChatGPT to be excessively agreeable and emotionally engaging, allegedly encouraging users to develop unhealthy attachments and place inappropriate trust in its responses.

State officials contend that these characteristics are particularly dangerous for children and people experiencing emotional distress. They allege that ChatGPT has reinforced delusions, provided unsafe medical advice and encouraged harmful behavior, including self-harm and violence.

The lawsuits also challenge how OpenAI communicates its safety capabilities to parents and consumers. Prosecutors argue that the company's public assurances create expectations that its safeguards do not consistently meet.

The allegations have not been proven in court. The lawsuits represent claims brought by state governments, not judicial findings that OpenAI violated the law or caused the specific harms described.

The states are seeking financial penalties and court orders requiring changes to OpenAI's practices. Some complaints also seek restrictions on developing advanced AI systems capable of improving themselves unless their behavior can be demonstrated to align with human safety requirements.

That proposed restriction extends the legal dispute beyond ChatGPT's current consumer features into broader questions about the development and oversight of increasingly autonomous artificial intelligence.

OpenAI disputes the characterization of its safety practices. Company spokesperson Drew Pusateri said OpenAI has implemented age-specific protections, parental controls, break reminders and safeguards intended to provide stronger protections for younger users.

The company also pointed to its recently introduced restricted ChatGPT experience for teenagers ages 13 through 18, which includes additional safety measures.

OpenAI said it recognizes that no safety system is perfect and remains committed to working with policymakers on practical regulations that apply across the artificial intelligence industry.

The lawsuits arrive as AI chatbots become increasingly common in education, workplaces and personal conversations. They also reflect growing pressure on technology companies to demonstrate that safety measures are effective rather than relying solely on company assurances.
