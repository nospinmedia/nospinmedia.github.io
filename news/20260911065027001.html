America marks 25 years since the September 11 attacks

Families gather to remember nearly 3,000 lives lost

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 11, 2026 at 6:16 AM ET

Americans are marking 25 years since the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks, with families gathering at Ground Zero, the Pentagon and Shanksville, Pennsylvania, to remember the nearly 3,000 people killed in the deadliest terrorist attack on U.S. soil.

At the National September 11 Memorial in New York, relatives are continuing the annual tradition of reading aloud the names of the victims. The ceremony includes moments of silence marking when the hijacked planes struck the World Trade Center towers and Pentagon, when the towers collapsed and when United Airlines Flight 93 crashed in Pennsylvania after passengers and crew fought back against the hijackers.

This year's New York ceremony also includes a seventh moment of silence honoring people who have died from illnesses and injuries linked to exposure to toxic material following the attacks. The observance follows the reading of the names, recognizing a health toll that has continued long after September 11 itself.

The attacks killed 2,977 people, excluding the 19 hijackers. The New York commemoration also remembers six people killed in the 1993 World Trade Center bombing, bringing the number of names read at the Memorial to 2,983.

Commemorations are taking place across the country, including ceremonies at the Pentagon and the Flight 93 National Memorial. Communities are also observing September 11 through moments of silence and volunteer projects as part of the federally recognized Patriot Day and National Day of Service and Remembrance.

A quarter-century has also created a generational divide in how Americans experience the anniversary. The 9/11 Memorial & Museum estimates about 100 million Americans are now too young to personally remember the attacks, making education and preservation of firsthand accounts an increasingly important part of remembrance.

The attacks reshaped U.S. national security, air travel and foreign policy and led to wars whose consequences extended for decades. Twenty-five years later, today's ceremonies remain centered on the individuals who were killed, their families and the first responders who rushed toward the disaster.
