Cuba Hit by New Nationwide Blackout After Grid Collapse

Hospitals prioritized as crews rebuild the SEN

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 3, 2026 at 10:55 AM ET

Cuba experienced another island-wide power failure late on August 2, 2026, when Unión Eléctrica de Cuba (UNE) reported a total disconnection of the Sistema Electroenergético Nacional (SEN). Officials acknowledged the collapse but did not immediately specify a technical cause or a firm restoration timeline. The outage left roughly 10 million residents without electricity and forced authorities to activate emergency recovery procedures.

UNE said crews were stabilizing generation step by step and cautioned that service would return unevenly as plants and transmission lines were brought back online. As in previous collapses, the utility’s initial focus is on forming small, isolated “microsystems” to power critical services—prioritizing hospitals, municipal water systems, and food facilities—before attempting to resynchronize larger power stations to the national grid. In Havana, the provincial power company had already been reporting worsening interruptions tied to damaged transformers, circuit trips, and planned load shedding prior to the nationwide failure.

The blackout underscores the severity of Cuba’s energy crisis: aging thermoelectric units, chronic maintenance gaps, and persistent fuel shortages have repeatedly strained capacity. July alone saw multiple island‑wide failures—on July 6, July 10, and July 14—with authorities warning that generation frequently falls far short of peak demand. Beyond lost lighting and refrigeration, widespread outages disrupt public transport, halt water pumping in many buildings, and force hospitals onto diesel generators, compounding risks for patients and essential services when fuel is tight.

While Cuban officials often cite restrictions on fuel supplies and a deteriorated generation fleet, reporters on the ground also note decades of under‑investment and spare‑parts scarcity as key drivers of instability. What is clear in the immediate term is operational, not political: technicians are working to re‑energize priority circuits, expand stable “micro‑islands,” and then cautiously reconnect major plants to restore national service. If restoration mirrors July’s collapses, some regions could see power hours before others as voltage and frequency are balanced across the SEN.
