New federal policy narrows protections for endangered wildlife

Unintended deaths may no longer trigger prosecution under key law

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 18, 2026 at 6:31 PM ET

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has adopted a new enforcement interpretation of the Endangered Species Act under which killing or injuring endangered wildlife during otherwise lawful activities generally will not be treated as an illegal "take" unless harming the animal was the purpose of the activity. The change could affect activities including logging, construction, mining and other development where protected animals are killed unintentionally.

Fish and Wildlife Service Director Brian Nesvik outlined the policy in a September 14 directive. The Endangered Species Act defines "take" to include actions such as harassing, harming, pursuing, hunting, shooting, wounding, killing, trapping or capturing protected wildlife. Under the new interpretation, officials are directed to focus enforcement on actions specifically aimed at protected animals rather than deaths that occur as unintended consequences of another lawful activity. Deliberately hunting, shooting or otherwise targeting endangered wildlife remains prohibited.

The directive follows a separate rule finalized in July by the Fish and Wildlife Service and National Marine Fisheries Service eliminating the longstanding regulatory definition of "harm." That definition had included significant habitat modification or degradation when it actually killed or injured protected wildlife. The July rule took effect September 14, the same day as Nesvik's directive.

The Trump administration says the changes return enforcement to the statutory language and original intent of the law while reducing regulatory burdens on landowners and businesses. Environmental organizations and former federal wildlife officials dispute that interpretation, arguing that Congress intended the law to protect species from activities that kill them even when killing wildlife is not the activity's primary purpose.

The changes are already the subject of litigation. Conservation organizations sued over the July rule, and 20 states and Washington, D.C. filed additional challenges in September. Those lawsuits could determine whether the administration's narrower interpretation of the Endangered Species Act remains in effect.
