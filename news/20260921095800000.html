Trump administration prepares sanctions against entire ICC

Measures could restrict payments, services and investigations

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 21, 2026 at 5:15 PM ET

The Trump administration is preparing sanctions against the International Criminal Court as an institution, a significant expansion of U.S. pressure that has previously focused primarily on individual judges and prosecutors. Two people familiar with the plans told Reuters an announcement could come soon, potentially around this week's United Nations General Assembly gathering in New York.

If implemented, the sanctions would generally prohibit U.S. citizens and companies from providing money, goods or services to the ICC without authorization from the U.S. Treasury Department. The measures reportedly would include a grace period before taking full effect. Because many international banks and service providers depend on access to the U.S. financial system, restrictions could also affect companies outside the United States that do business with the court.

ICC officials have previously warned that institution-wide sanctions could interfere with basic operations, including purchasing information-technology and insurance services, hiring investigators and making routine payments. The court, based in The Hague, was established in 2002 to prosecute individuals accused of genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity when national authorities are unable or unwilling to prosecute them.

The United States has never joined the ICC. The Trump administration argues that the court has exceeded its authority by pursuing officials from countries that have not accepted its jurisdiction. Washington has objected both to the ICC's investigation of alleged abuses involving U.S. personnel in Afghanistan and its 2024 arrest warrants for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant over alleged crimes in Gaza. Israel also is not an ICC member and disputes the court's jurisdiction.

The administration has already sanctioned more than a dozen ICC judges and prosecutors. Expanding those restrictions to the court itself would represent a broader step by targeting the institution's ability to conduct financial transactions and obtain services rather than only restricting specific officials.
