NASA opens applications for its next astronaut class

New recruits could live and work on the Moon under Artemis

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 9, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

NASA is accepting applications for a new class of astronauts who could eventually fly missions to the Moon, live and work on the lunar surface and help prepare for future human exploration of Mars. The agency is seeking U.S. citizens from scientific, engineering, medical and aviation backgrounds to join its astronaut corps.

The recruitment comes as NASA works toward establishing a sustained human presence on and around the Moon through its Artemis program. Future astronauts could conduct scientific research on the lunar surface, work aboard spacecraft in lunar orbit and participate in missions designed to test technologies and procedures needed for longer journeys into deep space.

NASA's basic education requirement is a master's degree in a STEM field, including engineering, biological science, physical science, computer science or mathematics, from an accredited institution. Certain alternatives can satisfy that requirement, including completion of medical school or a nationally recognized test-pilot program. Applicants also generally need at least three years of related professional experience after completing their degree. Pilots can qualify with at least 1,000 hours of pilot-in-command time, including substantial experience in high-performance jet aircraft.

Candidates must also pass NASA's long-duration astronaut physical and demonstrate leadership, teamwork and communication skills. Those selected will undergo approximately two years of training covering areas including robotics, spacewalking, spacecraft systems, geology, survival skills and operating NASA's T-38 training aircraft.

Selection does not guarantee a trip to the Moon. Astronauts are assigned to missions based on NASA's needs, and members of the corps can support missions from Earth for years before receiving a flight assignment. NASA's current exploration plans include missions in lunar orbit and eventual surface expeditions intended to build experience for future crewed missions to Mars.

The recruitment effort continues a tradition dating to 1959, when NASA selected its first seven astronauts for Project Mercury. Today's candidates are being recruited for missions considerably farther from Earth and potentially much longer than those flown by the earliest astronaut classes.
