Liechtenstein Ends Male-Only Royal Succession

Future firstborn children will inherit the throne regardless of sex

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 16, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

Liechtenstein has approved a historic change ending centuries of male-only royal succession. During the country's National Day celebrations on August 15, 2026, Hereditary Prince Alois announced that the Princely House of Liechtenstein had voted by the required two-thirds majority to adopt absolute primogeniture, meaning future firstborn children will inherit the throne regardless of sex.

The reform amends the ruling family's House Law and also grants female descendants equal participation rights in the family's internal governance. The change applies only to future generations and does not alter the current line of succession. Prince Hans-Adam II remains Liechtenstein's head of state, while Hereditary Prince Alois, who has exercised most constitutional duties since 2004, remains first in line. His eldest son, Prince Joseph Wenzel, continues as heir apparent.

The decision brings the Alpine principality of about 40,000 residents into line with a broader trend among European monarchies that have modernized succession rules to remove gender preference. Countries including Sweden, Norway, Belgium, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom have all adopted forms of absolute primogeniture over the past several decades.

The announcement also reflects broader changes within Liechtenstein itself. The country became the last in Europe to grant women the right to vote in national elections in 1984, and in 2025 appointed its first female prime minister, Brigitte Haas. Royal officials said the succession reform is intended to preserve continuity while recognizing equality for future generations of the princely family.

Although the change will not affect today's succession order, it ensures that future heirs will no longer be determined by gender. Constitutional experts say the reform aligns one of Europe's last remaining male-preference monarchies with modern succession standards while avoiding disruption to the current royal line.
