Pentagon watchdog confirms U.S. weapons shortfalls

Iran war costs $33.4 billion as bases and aircraft take heavy damage

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 15, 2026 at 11:45 AM ET

The Pentagon's inspector general has issued its first formal accounting of the Iran war's impact on U.S. forces, confirming that heavy weapons use has created "strategic inventory shortfalls" and exposed bottlenecks in the defense industry's ability to replace advanced munitions. The congressionally mandated report provides one of the clearest official assessments yet of the conflict's financial cost and damage to American military assets.

Through June 30, Operation Epic Fury had cost the Defense Department an estimated $33.4 billion, including approximately $22.3 billion in expended munitions, $3.7 billion in equipment losses and $7.4 billion in other expenses. Those figures do not include the still-undetermined cost of repairing all damaged military facilities.

Iranian missile and drone attacks damaged or destroyed hundreds of buildings and structures at U.S. bases in Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Oman and Jordan. Aircraft losses included four F-15s destroyed, one F-35 damaged, seven KC-135 refueling aircraft damaged and up to 30 MQ-9 Reaper drones destroyed. The report also provides official confirmation that Iran attacked the U.S. Navy's main regional logistics hub in Bahrain with ballistic missiles and drones.

The government's direct military spending represents only part of the conflict's economic cost. A Brown University estimate found that by early September, higher gasoline and diesel prices associated with the war had cost U.S. consumers approximately $100 billion — more than $760 per average household. Regular gasoline climbed from roughly $2.98 before the war to more than $4 a gallon, while diesel rose from about $3.76 to nearly $6. Higher diesel costs also affect trucking, agriculture and other parts of the supply chain, creating additional pressure on prices for food and other goods.

The watchdog said the Pentagon is working to address weapons shortfalls by accelerating procurement and production and stockpiling critical materials and components. The report does not say the U.S. has exhausted its overall ammunition supply, but it shows that sustained combat has placed significant pressure on some advanced weapons inventories and the industrial capacity needed to replace them.
