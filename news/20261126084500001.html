New U.S. solar tariffs and price floors take effect Dec. 4

Rules could make switching from costly heating fuels more expensive

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 26, 2026 at 1:16 PM ET

New U.S. trade restrictions taking effect December 4 will increase minimum prices on imported solar equipment and add a 15% tariff to many solar imports, potentially raising the upfront cost of going solar as households in parts of the country already face high energy costs.

Under a proclamation issued by President Trump, imported solar modules covered by the rules must meet a minimum import price of $0.38 per watt, while solar cells face a minimum of $0.22 per watt. Minimum prices are also set at $21 per kilogram for raw polysilicon and $100 per kilogram for polysilicon ingots and wafers. Raw polysilicon is exempt from the additional 15% tariff.

The proclamation includes an onshoring waiver allowing companies with approved plans for U.S. manufacturing to import covered products without the additional duties, part of the administration's effort to encourage domestic solar production and reduce reliance on foreign supply chains.

The restrictions come on top of separate trade actions against solar cells and modules from India, Indonesia and Laos. Following an affirmative injury determination by the U.S. International Trade Commission, final antidumping and countervailing duty orders took effect November 9. Antidumping rates include 123.04% for India, 94.36% for Indonesia and 65.43% for Laos, while additional anti-subsidy rates can exceed 100%, depending on the country and manufacturer.

That does not mean every solar panel or complete residential installation will double or triple in price. Labor, inverters, permitting and equipment sourcing all contribute to installation costs. However, affected imported components can face dramatically higher costs, potentially reducing the availability of lower-priced equipment.

The changes arrive as heating costs are already elevated. U.S. residential heating oil averaged $5.47 per gallon for the week of November 23, while New England averaged $5.39 and New Hampshire $5.56 per gallon. The Energy Information Administration expects households using heating oil to spend about 21% more this winter than last winter. Higher solar equipment costs could make the upfront investment needed to reduce dependence on expensive heating fuels harder for some households to afford.

The policy could still face challenges in 2027. Democrats will control both chambers of Congress and could pursue legislation changing presidential tariff authority or reversing the policy. However, President Trump could veto such legislation, and Democrats do not have the two-thirds majorities required to override a veto without Republican support. The separate antidumping and countervailing duties would also not automatically disappear if Congress changed the Section 232 policy.
