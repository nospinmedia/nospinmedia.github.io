Supreme Court lifts block on Trump mail-voting order

6–3 ruling lets federal agencies move ahead for now

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 25, 2026 at 8:46 AM ET

The U.S. Supreme Court allowed President Trump's administration to move ahead for now with key parts of Executive Order 14399, a March 31, 2026 directive that seeks to reshape how federal agencies handle mail-in and absentee voting. The unsigned 6–3 emergency ruling lifted an injunction covering 23 Democratic-led states and Washington, D.C., but the justices did not decide whether the executive order itself is constitutional or ultimately lawful.

The Court concluded that the states challenged the order too early, before federal agencies had finished implementing the policies it directed them to develop. The ruling allows the Department of Homeland Security to continue work on state citizenship lists using federal databases and permits other agencies to proceed with implementation efforts. The order also directs the U.S. Postal Service to establish rules involving ballot-envelope design, unique Intelligent Mail barcodes and state-specific lists of people receiving mail ballots. It further instructs the Justice Department to prioritize investigations and, when appropriate, prosecutions involving federal ballots issued to people who are not eligible to vote.

The decision does not mean those policies will necessarily take effect for the November 3, 2026 midterm election. A separate August 11 injunction issued by U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in a lawsuit brought by voting-rights organizations continues to block enforcement of the USPS provisions, although the administration could argue that the Supreme Court's reasoning affects that case as well. The Postal Service has already issued a final rule implementing portions of the executive order, but its practical effect remains tied to the ongoing litigation.

The Supreme Court emphasized that states may bring new challenges against specific agency rules once those policies are finalized. The three liberal justices dissented, with the disagreement centered largely on whether the states had shown sufficient present harm to justify blocking implementation before the agencies completed their work.
