Former deputy convicted in Sonya Massey killing dies in prison

Sean Grayson succumbed to cancer while serving 20-year sentence

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 24, 2026 at 8:46 PM ET

Sean Grayson, the former Sangamon County Sheriff's Office deputy convicted in the fatal shooting of Sonya Massey, has died while serving a 20-year prison sentence, according to his attorney. Grayson, 32, died on August 23 after battling Stage 4 colon cancer, which had spread to his liver and lungs. He had been receiving medical treatment while in the custody of the Illinois Department of Corrections, and his request for compassionate release had been denied by the Illinois Prisoner Review Board just weeks earlier.

Grayson was convicted of second-degree murder for the July 6, 2024, shooting of Massey, a 36-year-old Black woman who had called 911 to report a suspected prowler outside her Springfield home. Body camera footage released after the incident showed Grayson confronting Massey over a pot of boiling water before firing multiple shots, killing her inside her own home. The case drew national attention and sparked widespread protests, renewed calls for police reform, and scrutiny of law enforcement hiring and accountability practices.

Following his conviction in October 2025, Grayson was sentenced in January 2026 to 20 years in prison. His attorney later sought compassionate release, citing his terminal cancer diagnosis, but the request was unanimously rejected after members of Massey's family opposed the petition during a public hearing. Civil rights attorneys Ben Crump and Antonio Romanucci, who represented the Massey family, said Grayson's death does not lessen the family's loss but offered condolences to his relatives as they mourn.

The criminal case prompted reforms within the Sangamon County Sheriff's Office, including changes to hiring practices, officer oversight, and training. Massey's family has continued to advocate for broader policing reforms, arguing that the circumstances surrounding her death exposed systemic failures extending beyond the actions of a single deputy.
