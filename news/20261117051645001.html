Supreme Court Rejects Trump Appeal Over UC Research Grants

Lower-court First Amendment injunction remains in place

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 17, 2026 at 6:16 AM ET

The U.S. Supreme Court declined on Monday, November 16, 2026, to hear the Trump administration's appeal in Trump v. Thakur, No. 26-210, leaving in place a preliminary injunction protecting federal research grants awarded to University of California researchers whose projects were targeted under administration policies against diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives. The court denied review without comment or noted dissents.

The dispute stems from executive orders issued by President Trump in January 2025 directing federal agencies to end programs associated with diversity, equity and inclusion. Lead plaintiff Dr. Neeta Thakur, a physician-scientist at UCSF, and other researchers challenged grant cancellations by federal agencies, arguing that existing awards were being terminated based on the perceived viewpoints expressed in their research.

U.S. District Judge Rita Lin issued a preliminary injunction after concluding that the researchers were likely to succeed on their claim that the cancellations violated the First Amendment's prohibition on viewpoint discrimination. In May 2026, the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals upheld the First Amendment portion of that injunction. The appeals court, however, reversed the portion covering grants terminated solely through standardized form letters.

That distinction followed an earlier December 2025 9th Circuit ruling that claims under the Administrative Procedure Act challenging standardized grant termination letters must be pursued in the U.S. Court of Federal Claims, rather than federal district court.

Solicitor General D. John Sauer, representing the administration, asked the Supreme Court to intervene, arguing that the federal government is not constitutionally required to continue funding research that conflicts with presidential policy priorities. Approximately $59 million remains at stake in grants from the National Science Foundation and National Endowment for the Humanities, some extending through November 2029.

The Supreme Court's action is a procedural denial of review, not a ruling on the merits of the dispute. It does not establish a nationwide Supreme Court precedent, and the underlying litigation and other research-funding disputes remain active in lower courts.
