Fed officials signal faster rate hikes as inflation persists

Half-point December increase is now under consideration

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 11, 2026 at 10:16 AM ET

Federal Reserve officials are signaling that interest rates may need to rise faster after October inflation came in hotter than expected, with Governor Christopher Waller saying a half-percentage-point increase should be considered at the Fed's December 8-9 meeting.

Hours before the inflation report was released, Waller said he was already leaning toward supporting a 0.25 percentage-point increase in December. He added that if October core consumer inflation came in around 0.3% or higher, a larger move might be necessary. The subsequent report showed core prices, excluding food and energy, rose exactly 0.3% for the third consecutive month. Headline consumer prices increased 0.4%, above economists' expectations.

Waller said limiting the increase to a quarter point under those circumstances could put the central bank's inflation-fighting credibility at risk. The comments do not mean a half-point increase has been decided. The Federal Open Market Committee makes rate decisions collectively, and officials could change their views as additional economic data arrive before the December meeting.

Dallas Federal Reserve President Lorie Logan separately said the Fed needs at least two more quarter-point increases under the current outlook and that it may be possible to move more rapidly this winter. The Fed's benchmark policy rate is currently 3.75% to 4.00% after policymakers raised it by a quarter point in September and held rates steady in October.

Financial markets reacted sharply to the inflation data and Fed comments. The yield on the policy-sensitive 2-year Treasury note posted its largest increase since early April 2025. By late Tuesday, traders were pricing roughly a 10% chance of a half-point December increase, while a quarter-point move remained the more likely outcome.

Higher Federal Reserve rates can help restrain inflation by slowing borrowing and spending, but they can also raise financing costs for households and businesses.
