Three scientists win Nobel Prize for controlling brain cells with light

Optogenetics breakthrough transformed neuroscience research

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 6, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel have won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries that made it possible to control individual nerve cells using light, a technique that has transformed scientists' ability to study the brain.

The Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet honored the three scientists for work involving light-sensitive proteins known as channelrhodopsins. Hegemann and Nagel studied the proteins in microorganisms, including algae, while Deisseroth and other researchers helped develop their use for controlling neurons. By genetically equipping selected nerve cells with the light-sensitive proteins, researchers can activate or silence those cells with precisely timed pulses of light.

The resulting field, known as optogenetics, allows researchers to manipulate specific neural circuits with far greater precision than older techniques that stimulated broader areas of the brain. Scientists have used the method to investigate how neural circuits contribute to movement, behavior, memory and neurological and psychiatric disorders. Research involving optogenetics has helped scientists study conditions including Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, Alzheimer's disease and schizophrenia.

The technology is also being investigated for potential medical applications. Researchers have explored using optogenetic approaches to restore aspects of vision in people with certain forms of blindness, improve cochlear implants and develop more targeted approaches to neurological disorders. Many of these applications remain experimental, meaning the Nobel-winning discoveries have had their greatest impact so far as a research tool rather than as a routine medical treatment.

The three laureates will share the 12 million Swedish kronor Nobel award, worth about $1.2 million. Their discoveries trace back more than two decades: light-sensitive channel proteins were demonstrated in human cells in 2003, followed by a landmark demonstration of optical control of nerve cells in 2005.
