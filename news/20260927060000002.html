FAA investigates Boeing 737 MAX software glitch

Boeing first learned of issue in 2024 but initially found no safety risk

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 27, 2026 at 1:16 PM ET

The Federal Aviation Administration is reviewing a software problem affecting certain Boeing 737 MAX flight computers that can cause an automated flight-guidance feature to disengage during a specific type of aborted landing.

The problem can occur during a missed approach, also known as a go-around, when pilots are using a precision approach with the autopilot engaged and make changes to their programmed flight plan. Under those circumstances, the aircraft's vertical navigation system, or VNAV, can disengage and switch to a less sophisticated automated pitch-control mode.

Boeing says the autopilot itself continues functioning and pilots are trained to safely operate without VNAV. However, the malfunction can require additional pilot actions at a point in the flight when crews may already have a high workload.

The newly disclosed timeline shows Boeing first learned about the issue from an airline in November 2024. The company reviewed the problem and determined in February 2025 that it was not a safety issue. Boeing later received additional information from airline operators that prompted the company to conduct a formal safety review. The findings from that review were sent to the FAA in September 2026.

Boeing notified 737 operators about the software problem in August and reinforced existing procedures for handling the situation. The company says engineers are developing a permanent software update and are working to accelerate a fix that had initially been expected in 2028.

The FAA says it will convene a Corrective Action Review Board to examine the problem and will take immediate action if the review identifies a safety concern.

The affected software is found in versions 14 and 14.1 of the MAX flight management computer operating system. American Airlines, Alaska Airlines, Southwest Airlines and United Airlines say their currently operating MAX fleets do not use those versions. Southwest and United have asked Boeing not to deliver new aircraft equipped with the affected software, instead requesting an earlier version.

The review could also affect Boeing's newest MAX variants. The 737 MAX 10 had been approaching FAA certification, while deliveries of the recently certified MAX 7 were expected to begin this fall. The timing of both programs could change depending on the outcome of the FAA review.
