Horse smuggling exposes gap in U.S. screwworm defenses

Animals crossing from Mexico can bypass inspections and quarantine safeguards

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 6, 2026 at 9:33 AM ET

Illegal horse smuggling across the U.S.-Mexico border is emerging as a potential weakness in efforts to contain New World screwworm, a flesh-eating parasite that returned to U.S. livestock this year after decades of eradication. A Reuters investigation found that animals transported through remote sections of the border can bypass veterinary examinations, health certificates and quarantine procedures designed to prevent the parasite from spreading.

In one case, sheriff's deputies intercepted a trailer carrying six horses near Redford, Texas, after the animals had illegally crossed the Rio Grande from Mexico. The horses, discovered in late May, were not infected with screwworm. However, authorities and animal-health experts say such crossings create a surveillance gap because illegally transported animals do not undergo the inspections required at official ports of entry.

New World screwworm flies lay eggs in wounds on warm-blooded animals. Their larvae then feed on living tissue, potentially causing severe injury or death without treatment. The parasite was eradicated from the United States in the 1960s, but began infesting U.S. cattle again in June 2026 after spreading north through Central America and Mexico. Federal and state authorities have responded with livestock movement restrictions, surveillance, veterinary inspections and the mass release of sterile male flies intended to suppress reproduction.

The smuggling routes complicate those defenses. Reuters found that criminal networks move horses and other animals through isolated stretches of the Texas border, including animals intended for racing, breeding and other markets. Authorities in Presidio County have intercepted more than 50 smuggled horses during the past two years, although officials have not established that an illegally imported animal caused any U.S. screwworm infection.

The USDA says animal trafficking presents a biosecurity risk and continues monitoring the outbreak. Federal officials describe the current risk to people and animals nationally as very low, but states including Texas and Florida continue heightened surveillance and animal-movement controls in affected or higher-risk areas.
