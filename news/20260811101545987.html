9th Circuit Lets Youth Social Media Lawsuits Move Forward

Court says Section 230 defense cannot halt more than 3,000 cases before trial

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 11, 2026 at 11:47 AM ET

A three-judge panel of the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled on August 10, 2026 that major technology companies cannot immediately halt litigation over alleged youth harms by invoking Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act. Writing for the court, Circuit Judge Jacqueline Nguyen said Section 230 provides a defense to liability rather than immunity from being sued, making the companies' interlocutory appeals premature. The decision allows more than 3,000 lawsuits against Meta Platforms, Alphabet's Google/YouTube, ByteDance's TikTok, Snap Inc., and other companies to continue in federal court.

The ruling leaves in place earlier decisions by U.S. District Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers in Oakland, California, where the multidistrict litigation is centralized. Parents, school districts, municipalities, states, and other plaintiffs allege the companies intentionally designed their platforms to encourage compulsive use by minors while failing to adequately warn users about potential mental health risks. The appeals court concluded the companies must continue defending those claims before raising Section 230 arguments at a later stage of the litigation.

The panel also denied Meta's request to delay a separate multistate trial scheduled to begin August 12, 2026, involving 29 state attorneys general who allege the company illegally collected children's data and misrepresented the safety of its platforms. The decision returns the broader federal litigation to discovery and other pretrial proceedings while allowing the state-led case to move forward as scheduled.

The ruling adds to mounting legal pressure on major social media companies. Earlier this year, a Los Angeles jury awarded $6 million in a youth addiction case involving Meta and YouTube, while a New Mexico court ordered Meta to pay an additional $567 million tied to youth mental health claims. The companies deny wrongdoing and have indicated they will continue challenging the claims through the courts.
