AI data center boom pushes power-line battles hundreds of miles away

Rural communities face new transmission projects to feed distant electricity demand

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 26, 2026 at 8:45 PM ET

The rapid growth of AI data centers is creating new fights over the power infrastructure needed to supply them, including high-voltage transmission projects that can affect rural communities far from the facilities consuming the electricity.

One of the clearest examples is unfolding in Wisconsin, where American Transmission Co. has proposed a major expansion of the grid to serve a large data center campus being developed in Port Washington. The revised project includes new and rebuilt high-voltage lines, upgrades to existing facilities and as many as five new substations across several counties.

The Port Washington campus, being developed by Vantage Data Centers for customers including Oracle and OpenAI, is expected to require enormous amounts of electricity. Wisconsin regulators previously forced the transmission company to restart its approval process after extensive changes made the original proposal difficult for regulators and the public to evaluate. ATC refiled the project in September with an estimated cost of roughly $2.5 billion to $2.7 billion.

For some landowners, the dispute is about where the new lines will go. Residents in communities including Fredonia have organized against routes that could cross farms, homes and conserved land. Proposed infrastructure includes 345-kilovolt transmission lines, and acquiring easements could involve eminent domain if agreements with property owners cannot be reached.

The conflict illustrates a broader challenge created by rapidly growing electricity demand from data centers. New facilities can require power comparable to large industrial operations, while generation and transmission infrastructure may have to be constructed well beyond the communities where the data centers themselves are located.

Utilities say the projects are needed to provide requested service while maintaining grid reliability. Wisconsin consumer advocates and regulators are also examining how data-center-related infrastructure costs should be allocated so ordinary electricity customers are protected from expenses created by unusually large new users.
