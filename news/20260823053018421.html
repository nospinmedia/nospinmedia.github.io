Former Kansas Sen. Nancy Kassebaum Baker Dies at 94

Trailblazing Republican Helped Shape Landmark Health Care Law

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 23, 2026 at 6:31 PM ET

Nancy Kassebaum Baker, the former Kansas Republican senator who became the first woman elected to the U.S. Senate without succeeding her husband in office, has died at the age of 94. Her son, Bill Kassebaum, said she died of natural causes on August 21. Elected in 1978, Kassebaum Baker served 18 years in the Senate before retiring in 1997, earning a reputation as an independent-minded lawmaker who frequently worked across party lines and remained one of the chamber's leading moderate Republicans.

The daughter of former Kansas Gov. Alf Landon, the Republican presidential nominee in 1936, Kassebaum Baker built a legislative record that often crossed ideological boundaries. She chaired the Senate Labor and Human Resources Committee, becoming the only woman leading a Senate committee at the time of her retirement. In 1996, she partnered with Democratic Sen. Ted Kennedy to pass the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), a landmark law that expanded health insurance portability for workers changing jobs and established national standards for protecting medical information.

Throughout her career, Kassebaum Baker supported measures that sometimes put her at odds with fellow Republicans, including voting to establish the Martin Luther King Jr. Day federal holiday, backing sanctions against apartheid-era South Africa, and supporting the 1994 federal assault weapons ban. After leaving the Senate, she continued public service through bipartisan commissions and accompanied her husband, former Senate Majority Leader Howard Baker, during his tenure as U.S. ambassador to Japan. Howard Baker died in 2014.

In later years, Kassebaum Baker became increasingly critical of the direction of the modern Republican Party, endorsing several Democratic candidates in Kansas and publicly criticizing President Trump following the January 6, 2021, attack on the U.S. Capitol. Political leaders from both parties remembered her as a principled public servant whose willingness to seek bipartisan solutions helped shape major legislation and left a lasting mark on the Senate.
