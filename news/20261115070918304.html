Trump and Putin push for three-way summit as Xi remains wary

Nuclear weapons and Ukraine complicate historic China meeting

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished November 15, 2026 at 1:16 PM ET

President Trump, Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping are preparing for an unprecedented three-way meeting in China next week, but disagreements over nuclear weapons and the possibility of discussing Ukraine are complicating preparations for the gathering.

The leaders are expected to meet during the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, which runs November 18–19. The meeting would bring the leaders of the world's three largest nuclear powers together in this format for the first time.

Trump wants nuclear arms control to be a central subject. China, however, has long resisted joining a three-way arms-control framework while its nuclear arsenal remains substantially smaller than those of the United States and Russia. Washington and Moscow each possess more than 5,000 nuclear warheads, compared with roughly 500 for China.

Beijing has also argued that any broader nuclear-reduction framework involving China should account for the arsenals of other nuclear powers, including Britain and France, rather than treating China as an equal counterpart to the much larger American and Russian stockpiles.

Xi's caution does not necessarily indicate personal distrust of Trump. Reuters reported that Trump's less-scripted diplomatic style creates uncertainty for a Chinese government that carefully plans major leader-level events. Beijing also has substantive concerns about the agenda and about appearing to participate in agreements affecting countries that are not represented.

Ukraine is the most sensitive issue. Putin has sought discussions about ending the war, while China has resisted making Ukraine part of the three-way agenda. U.S. officials have said they do not expect Ukraine to be negotiated during the trilateral meeting, although Moscow has indicated Putin intends to continue discussing a settlement with Trump.

European governments and Ukraine are concerned that decisions affecting European security could be discussed without them. European and Ukrainian officials have emphasized to Washington that any settlement must respect Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity and that Kyiv must be involved in decisions affecting its future.

Those concerns have revived comparisons with the 1945 Yalta Conference, where the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union negotiated major elements of the postwar order. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has warned against a modern "Yalta" in which major powers make decisions affecting smaller countries without their participation. The Kremlin has rejected that comparison.

The three governments also enter the meeting with different objectives. Washington wants to begin a conversation about nuclear reductions, Moscow wants recognition of its security concerns and continued discussion of Ukraine, while Beijing is trying to prevent the summit from placing China under nuclear restrictions it considers disproportionate or drawing it into negotiations over a European war.

U.S. officials have cautioned that they do not expect a major agreement from the first meeting. Instead, the Shenzhen gathering is being described as an opening conversation among the three leaders, with some of the most difficult questions still unresolved.
