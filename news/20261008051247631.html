Most Fed officials see another rate hike likely this year

September minutes show inflation concerns remain elevated

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished October 8, 2026 at 3:46 PM ET

Most Federal Reserve policymakers believed another interest-rate increase would likely be appropriate before the end of 2026, according to minutes from the central bank's September 15–16 meeting released Wednesday. The outlook comes after officials unanimously raised the federal funds target range by 0.25 percentage points to 3.75%–4% at that meeting, the first increase after nine months of holding rates steady.

The minutes show broad agreement on the September increase but differing views about why higher rates were necessary. Many participants described a higher rate path as prudent insurance against inflation remaining persistently above the Fed's 2% target because of stronger demand or additional supply shocks. Others viewed higher rates as necessary based on their central economic forecasts rather than primarily as insurance against future risks.

Almost all participants judged that inflation risks remained tilted to the upside while risks to the labor market had diminished and were broadly balanced. Several officials said they considered the current federal funds rate either not restrictive or only mildly restrictive. Policymakers pointed to a resilient economy, solid consumer spending and robust business investment, while also discussing inflation pressure associated with higher energy costs, geopolitical disruptions and demand connected to the artificial-intelligence investment boom.

The minutes do not commit the Fed to raising rates at its next meeting. Officials specifically emphasized that they would approach each meeting with an open mind and base future decisions on incoming economic data and changes in the outlook. The Fed's next policy meeting is scheduled for October 27–28, followed by its final meeting of the year on December 8–9.

The distinction has become important because financial conditions and economic data have continued changing since the September meeting. The minutes capture policymakers' views from three weeks ago rather than a decision about what the Fed will do in October. Still, they show that another increase remained the prevailing expectation among officials when they last met.
