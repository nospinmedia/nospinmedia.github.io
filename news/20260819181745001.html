U.S. Debt Tops $40 Trillion for First Time

Interest Costs and Higher Bond Yields Intensify Fiscal Pressure

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 19, 2026 at 6:31 PM ET

The U.S. national debt has surpassed $40 trillion for the first time, reaching $40.047 trillion as persistent deficits, rising benefit costs and mounting interest expenses continue to push federal borrowing higher. Treasury Department data for August 18 showed $32.266 trillion in debt held by the public and another $7.782 trillion in intragovernmental holdings.

The milestone came less than five months after the debt crossed $39 trillion. The total has more than doubled from $19.95 trillion when President Trump began his first term in January 2017. Roughly one-third of that increase accumulated during the federal response to the COVID-19 pandemic under Trump and former President Joe Biden. Debt rose about $7.8 trillion during Trump's first term, $8.4 trillion during Biden's term and another $3.8 trillion since Trump returned to office in January 2025.

More recent fiscal pressures include tax and spending legislation, growing mandatory programs and lost tariff revenue. The Congressional Budget Office estimates the 2025 reconciliation law, including extensions of provisions from the 2017 tax law and increased defense and homeland-security spending, will increase projected deficits by about $4.7 trillion from 2026 through 2035. Treasury reported a $432 billion July deficit, the fourth-largest monthly deficit on record, as tariff refunds pushed net customs receipts below zero for a third consecutive month while Social Security and Medicare spending continued to rise. Roughly 60% of approximately $7 trillion in annual federal spending is committed to mandatory programs, while about $1.1 trillion now goes toward interest on federal borrowing.

Pressure is also showing in financial markets. A recent $25 billion auction of 30-year Treasury bonds cleared at its highest yield since 2021, and the 30-year yield subsequently climbed above 5.3%, its highest level since 2007, as investors demanded greater compensation for holding long-term U.S. debt. Foreign investors still hold nearly one-third of Treasuries, but their demand has declined over the past year. Maya MacGuineas, president of the nonpartisan Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, warned that continued borrowing can increase inflation pressure, crowd out other federal priorities and leave the government less able to respond to future emergencies.

On August 19, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced that long-term Treasury buybacks will increase from a maximum of $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation beginning September 9, a move intended to improve liquidity in the 10- to 30-year Treasury market. The program may ease market strains, but it does not reduce the underlying federal debt or deficit.
