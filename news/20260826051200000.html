Canada Announces $20 Billion in Retaliatory U.S. Tariffs

Ottawa pairs new duties with C$7.5B relief package for businesses and workers

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 26, 2026 at 11:46 AM ET

Canada has announced a new round of retaliatory tariffs covering C$27.6 billion (about US$20 billion) in American imports after trade negotiations with the Trump administration broke down. Finance and National Revenue Minister François-Philippe Champagne and Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said the measures are intended to match recently imposed U.S. tariffs while providing financial support to Canadian industries expected to face higher costs and disrupted trade.

The new tariffs are scheduled to take effect at 12:01 a.m. EDT on September 8, 2026, with duty rates of 15%, 25%, and 50% applied across more than 700 categories of U.S. products. Canadian tariffs on American steel and aluminum will increase to 50%, while 25% duties will apply to products including dairy goods, seafood, household appliances, and pulp and paper. A lower 15% tariff tier will cover products such as air-conditioning equipment, power tools, and selected electronic components. Officials said the measures were structured to mirror U.S. trade actions while targeting a broad range of American exports.

Alongside the tariff package, Ottawa unveiled a C$7.5 billion domestic support program designed to help businesses and workers affected by escalating trade tensions. The package includes a C$2 billion investment in the Canada Strong Diversification Fund to strengthen domestic manufacturing and supply chains, along with loan programs, financing assistance, and worker support intended to reduce the impact on Canadian industries facing supply disruptions.

The latest action marks another escalation in the trade dispute between the two neighboring countries. While Canadian officials said they remain willing to negotiate, they argued that equivalent countermeasures were necessary to respond to U.S. tariffs and protect domestic industries. The tariffs are expected to affect manufacturers, agricultural producers, and consumers on both sides of the border as negotiations remain at an impasse.
