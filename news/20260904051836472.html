Automakers press Congress to lock in restrictions on Chinese vehicles

Industry wants permanent federal ban enacted before year-end

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 4, 2026 at 5:43 PM ET

Major automakers are urging Congress to permanently prohibit Chinese connected vehicles and related technology from the U.S. market, pressing lawmakers to turn existing federal restrictions into law before the current congressional session ends. The Alliance for Automotive Innovation, which represents companies including General Motors, Ford, Toyota, Volkswagen, Hyundai, Honda and Stellantis, sent the request to congressional leaders on September 3.

Alliance President and CEO John Bozzella called for a permanent ban on the sale, import and manufacture of Chinese connected vehicles, hardware and software. He argued that heavily subsidized Chinese automakers are expanding rapidly in markets outside the United States and that connected vehicles can collect, process and transmit sensitive vehicle and consumer information. Chinese vehicles have not established a significant U.S. market presence, in part because tariffs and existing Commerce Department rules already create substantial barriers to their entry.

Congress is considering legislation that would make those protections more permanent. The bipartisan Connected Vehicle Security Act of 2026 would prohibit the importation, manufacture and sale of connected vehicles and related software and hardware associated with designated foreign adversaries. The Senate version, introduced by Sens. Bernie Moreno and Elissa Slotkin, advanced unanimously through the Senate Commerce Committee in July. A House version was introduced by Reps. John Moolenaar and Debbie Dingell.

The push reflects both economic and national-security concerns. Automakers say China's government-supported automotive industry could undercut U.S. manufacturers while connected vehicle systems create potential data-security risks. The legislation would build on a federal connected-vehicle rule adopted before President Trump returned to office, while putting restrictions into statute rather than leaving them primarily dependent on executive-branch regulation. Lawmakers have also debated how broadly the restrictions should apply to companies with partial Chinese ownership, an issue that could affect some non-Chinese manufacturers.
