Navy Reviews Renaming Future Carrier for President Trump

Proposal Would Remove WWII Hero Doris Miller's Name

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 23, 2026 at 10:16 AM ET

The U.S. Navy is reviewing whether to rename the future aircraft carrier CVN-81, which is currently designated as the USS Doris Miller, after President Trump, according to multiple reports citing defense officials familiar with the discussions. The Gerald R. Ford-class carrier was named in 2020 for Doris Miller, the World War II sailor who became the first African American to receive the Navy Cross after displaying extraordinary heroism during the December 7, 1941, attack on Pearl Harbor. Officials emphasized that no final decision has been announced, and the review remains under consideration within the Department of Defense.

According to the reports, Navy officials have increasingly referred to the ship by its hull designation, CVN-81, rather than by name in some internal communications while the review continues. The discussions reportedly involve Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao. If approved, the move would mark a significant departure from modern Navy naming practices because aircraft carriers have traditionally been named for former presidents, senior political leaders, or nationally recognized historical figures rather than a sitting president.

The possibility of renaming the carrier has drawn immediate criticism from veterans' organizations, civil rights advocates, and some lawmakers who argue that removing Miller's name would diminish recognition of his historic service and sacrifice. Supporters of the proposal have argued that President Trump has significantly expanded defense spending and modernized the military, making him worthy of such an honor. As of now, the Pentagon has not publicly confirmed any final recommendation or announced whether the proposal will proceed.

Huntington Ingalls Industries is constructing the estimated $12.5 billion carrier, which is expected to enter service in the 2030s. Any official renaming would occur before the ship is commissioned into the fleet. The debate has renewed broader discussions over how the military selects names for major warships and how those decisions reflect American history and national service.
