US, Denmark and Greenland reach Arctic security deal

Agreement expands American military presence while Greenland remains Danish

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished September 19, 2026 at 11:46 AM ET

The United States, Denmark and Greenland have reached an agreement that would significantly expand the American military presence in Greenland while leaving sovereignty over the Arctic territory unchanged. President Trump announced the agreement on September 18, saying it gives the United States permanent security rights in Greenland and allows his administration to begin expanding its military presence there.

The agreement is expected to be formally signed by all three governments during the United Nations General Assembly next week. It still requires parliamentary action in Denmark and Greenland before it can take effect. Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen said the agreement recognizes the sovereignty and territorial integrity of Denmark and Greenland as well as Greenlanders' right to self-determination. Greenland Prime Minister Jens-Frederik Nielsen said the arrangement recognizes Greenland's interests.

Under the emerging agreement, the United States would be able to increase its military presence and establish additional bases in Greenland. It would also prohibit bases belonging to countries outside NATO and restrict investments by countries considered security threats. A State Department official said the provisions would prevent China and Russia from establishing military bases on the island.

The United States already operates Pituffik Space Base in northwestern Greenland under a defense agreement dating to 1951. The installation supports missile warning, missile defense and space surveillance operations. Greenland's location between North America and Europe and its position largely inside the Arctic Circle have made it strategically important to U.S. and NATO defense planning for decades.

The agreement follows months of tension after Trump repeatedly said the United States needed Greenland for national security and declined to rule out using force to acquire it. Denmark and Greenland rejected transferring sovereignty but had indicated they were willing to discuss an expanded American military presence and additional U.S. investment.
