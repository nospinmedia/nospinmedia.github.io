Trump Signs Two Orders Narrowing Birthright Citizenship

Measures target birth tourism and parents tied to foreign governments

By No Spin Media NewsroomPublished August 7, 2026 at 8:46 AM ET

President Trump signed two executive orders on August 6, 2026, renewing his effort to restrict birthright citizenship after the U.S. Supreme Court rejected his broader policy in Trump v. Barbara. One order identifies narrower categories of U.S.-born children the administration says should not receive federal documents recognizing citizenship, while the second directs federal agencies to restrict entry for people traveling to the United States specifically to give birth.

The citizenship order applies when neither parent is a U.S. citizen and includes children of certain foreign government employees, members of designated Foreign Terrorist Organizations or other specified terrorist designees, and parents accused of using commercial transactions or fraud to obtain citizenship for a child. It also covers some commercial surrogacy arrangements and births in U.S. territories where citizenship is not granted by federal statute. Federal agencies must issue implementation guidance within 30 days.

The separate birth-tourism order directs the State Department and Department of Homeland Security to tighten visa and entry rules for foreign nationals whose purpose in entering the country is to give birth. The order permits actions including visa denial or revocation, removal, and potential permanent entry bans for people found to have participated in birth-tourism schemes. Humanitarian and national-interest exemptions remain available.

The actions follow the Supreme Court's June 30, 2026, decision in Trump v. Barbara, which ruled that children born in the United States to parents who are unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth. The Court affirmed the judgment 6–3, although the justices differed on whether the protection rested directly on the 14th Amendment, federal law, or both. Trump's new orders focus on narrower exceptions rather than the broad limits contained in his January 2025 order.

The ACLU has said the new restrictions will face legal challenges, arguing that executive action cannot override the Constitution's Citizenship Clause. The administration maintains the new categories fit within exceptions recognized by the Supreme Court and existing immigration law.
